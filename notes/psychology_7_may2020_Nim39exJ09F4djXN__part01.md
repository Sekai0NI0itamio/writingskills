**Paragraph 2 (Loftus & Palmer study)**

- **Hand-off:** The phrase "whether these memories had been reconstructed or not" creates a *pending verdict* — the reader needs to see the data before the question can be answered.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This section is the **end of a definition/function paragraph + the body of a study-evidence paragraph** introducing the first supporting study. The ordered content moves are:

1. **Function of the concept** — state *what schemas do* (simplify + create heuristics). This sets up why distortion matters.
2. **Authority + dual research aim** — name the researchers and the *two-part question* (effect + reconstruction). This frames the data the reader will see.
3. **Sample + core procedure** — who participated and what they did in baseline form.
4. **IV specification (the trick)** — name the leading question and the manipulation (verb swap).
5. **Conditions enumerated** — list the alternative IV levels so the reader sees the full manipulation.
6. **Design justification** — independent measures + small n (rationale, not just description).
7. **Headline result (directional)** — state the direction of the effect using the DV.
8. **Specific data points** — anchor in two contrasting numerical means.
9. **Causal mechanism** — explain *why* the verb influenced estimates (perceived intensity).
10. **Implication back to the research question** — answer the original "reconstructed or not" question.

**WHY this order works:** Each move answers a question the previous one left open. The reader is never told more than they need before they need it (e.g., conditions are only listed after the IV is named; numbers appear after the headline claim; the mechanism appears after the data; the verdict appears last). To replicate: state the concept → name the study → build the method from sample → IV → conditions → design → result → numbers → mechanism → verdict.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### SKELETON A — "Concept function" tail paragraph
"[Concept] is defined as [definition]; [author, year] explain that [concept] allows us to [verb] [object] in order to [purpose], which [broader cognitive benefit]."

1. **Slot 1 — Definition** (relative clause, past/present tense): defines the technical term.
2. **Slot 2 — Authority + functional clause** (present tense, "allows us to"): names a scholar and states the cognitive function.
3. **Slot 3 — Purpose clause** ("in order to"): explains *why* the function exists.
4. **Slot 4 — Benefit clause** ("which aid/bias/influence..."): extends to the broader consequence.

**HOW to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** Slot 1: pick your IB concept (e.g., "confirmation bias"); Slot 2: choose an authority and a functional verb (e.g., "Nickerson (1998) explain that confirmation bias allows us to filter information"); Slot 3: give the evolutionary/heuristic purpose (e.g., "in order to reduce cognitive load"); Slot 4: name the cost/benefit (e.g., "which can lead to polarisation").

**Original fill:** "We rely on schemas in order to simplify the world and create heuristics (mental shortcuts) which aid our thinking."

**Demo fill:** "We rely on heuristics in order to reduce cognitive load and make rapid judgements (System 1 thinking) which speed up our decision-making."

### SKELETON B — "Study introduction with dual aim"
"[Authors (year)] conducted a [type] experiment to investigate [question 1], and whether [question 2]. [Sample + setting] were asked to [core task]. The [IV] was disguised as [manipulation] in [stimulus] which [did what]. [Other conditions] where [IV] was replaced with [list]. The sample followed a [design] design such that [design consequence]. The findings showed that [headline result]. For instance, [numerical contrast A], whereas [numerical contrast B]. This is due to [mechanism]. As such, [verdict linked to question 2]."

1. **Slot 1 — Authority + dual research aim** (one sentence, two-part "and whether…" clause).
2. **Slot 2 — Sample + core procedure** (number + institution + task).
3. **Slot 3 — IV specification** (the "trick"/manipulation, embedded quote optional).
4. **Slot 4 — Conditions enumerated** (list of IV levels).
5. **Slot 5 — Design justification** ("such that…").
6. **Slot 6 — Headline result** (directional, using the DV).
7. **Slot 7 — Numerical contrast** ("For instance… whereas…").
8. **Slot 8 — Mechanism** ("This is due to…").
9. **Slot 9 — Verdict** ("As such, we could see that…").

**HOW to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** Slot 1: pick a classic study and write its two-part aim (effect + mechanism). Slot 2: state n + population + the task they performed. Slot 3: name the IV and describe how it was hidden in a stimulus. Slot 4: list the other IV levels. Slot 5: name the design and explain the n-per-group consequence. Slot 6: state the directional finding using the DV. Slot 7: pick two contrasting means. Slot 8: give a one-clause cognitive/perceptual explanation. Slot 9: answer the original "or not" question.

**Original fill:** "Loftus and Palmer (1974) conducted a lab experiment to investigate the extent to which such post-event information could affect participants' memory, and whether these memories had been reconstructed or not."

**Demo fill:** "Asch (1951) conducted a lab experiment to investigate the extent to which group pressure could influence a participant's judgement, and whether conformity would occur even when the answer was obviously wrong."

### SKELETON C — "Method-then-data" sentence pair
"The findings showed that [headline finding using DV]. For instance, [condition A] reported [mean X], whereas [condition B] reported [mean Y]."

1. **Slot 1 — Headline finding** (directional, "higher/lower/more/less…when…").
2. **Slot 2 — Condition A + mean** (specific number + unit).
3. **Slot 3 — Contrastive connective + Condition B + mean**.

**HOW to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** Slot 1: choose your DV and state the directional pattern. Slot 2: pick the extreme condition and its mean. Slot 3: pick the control condition and its mean. Use "whereas" for contrast.

**Original fill:** "The findings showed that participants provided higher speed estimates when more 'emotionally intense' verbs were used. For instance, those who read the verb 'smashed' reported a mean estimate of 40.5 mph, whereas those who read the verb 'hit' reported a lower mean estimate of 34.0 mph."

**Demo fill:** "The findings showed that participants conformed more often when the group was unanimous. For instance, those in the 3-person condition conformed on 32% of trials, whereas those in the 1-person condition conformed on only 12.5% of trials."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / continuation**
- "followed by" — *signals next step in procedure*: "videos of traffic accidents followed by a questionnaire about the events."

**Cause / consequence / mechanism**
- "due to the belief that" — *explains the mechanism behind the data*: "This is due to the belief that the impact perceived…"
- "As such" — *draws conclusion from preceding evidence*: "As such, we could see that participants' memory…"

**Specification / enumeration**
- "either… or…" — *lists alternative IV levels*: "replaced with either 'smashed', 'collided', 'bumped' or 'contacted'."
- "such that" — *specifies the consequence of a design choice*: "followed an independent measures design such that each of the five groups…"

**Evidence handling / authority**
- "conducted a lab experiment to investigate" — *signals empirical support*: "Loftus and Palmer (1974) conducted a lab experiment to investigate…"
- "The findings showed that" — *introduces results as evidence*: "The findings showed that participants provided higher speed estimates…"

**Explanation / definition verbs**
- "defined as" (carried from the prior paragraph) — *anchors a term*.
- "disguised as" — *frames the IV as a hidden manipulation*: "disguised as a change of wording in a 'leading' question…"
- "For instance" — *signals concrete evidence follows*: "For instance, those who read the verb 'smashed'…"
- "whereas" — *sets up contrast between two conditions*: "whereas those who read the verb 'hit' reported…"

**Contrast / concession**
- "whereas" — *contrasts two condition means*.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern used: AUTHORITY → AIM → METHOD (sample → IV → conditions → design) → RESULT (headline → numbers) → MECHANISM → VERDICT.**

This is the classic **"study-as-evidence" pattern**: a concept (schemas) is supported by walking the reader through one study as if it were a piece of evidence answering a specific question.

**Step-by-step instructions to replicate with a NEW idea:**

1. **State the broader concept's function** in one sentence (what does it let us do, and why). This is the "why does this matter" hook.
2. **Name the authority** (researchers + year) and **frame the research as a question with two parts** (does X happen? + is mechanism Y true?). Use "conducted a [type] experiment to investigate [Q1], and whether [Q2]."
3. **Open the method** with sample size + institution + the core task they performed.
4. **Specify the IV as a "hidden" manipulation** — describe how the key variable was disguised inside a stimulus (a question, a confederate, a display). Quote or paraphrase the stimulus.
5. **Enumerate the other IV levels** with "either… or…" so the reader sees the full manipulation.
6. **Justify the design** with "such that" — name the design and state the consequence for n per group.
7. **State the headline result** directionally, using the DV ("higher/lower… when…").
8. **Anchor with two contrasting numerical means** using "For instance… whereas…".
9. **Provide the mechanism** with "This is due to the belief that…" (one cognitive/perceptual reason).
10. **Close the loop on the original question** with "As such, we could see that…" + restate the concept in light of the finding.
