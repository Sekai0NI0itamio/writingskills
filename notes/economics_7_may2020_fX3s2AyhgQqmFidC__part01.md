## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **policy trajectory + mixed-verdict + reform agenda** section. The ordered moves are:

1. **Legislative close** — finishes the procedural sentence from the previous section so the reader is not left hanging.
2. **Historical origin of the policy** — establishes that the measure is old, so current weakness cannot be blamed on novelty.
3. **Delay / inertia causes** — names the political forces responsible for the gap between proposal and implementation.
4. **Quantitative dilution** — proves the delay produced concrete weakening (rate cut from 10 % to 2 %).
5. **Concessive general verdict** — delivers the balanced "significant but limited" judgment that the rest of the section will expand.
6. **Specific named endorsement** — converts the general verdict into a quotable, traceable expert opinion.
7. **Counter-authority critique** — uses an even higher source (government's own analysis) to introduce the inadequacy.
8. **Forward imperative / recommendation** — issues the "must be strengthened" prescription.
9. **Supporting-measures specification** — widens the prescription from one lever to many, preventing a narrow fix.

**WHY this order works:** chronology must precede evaluation (you cannot judge a policy before locating it in time); weakening evidence must precede expert verdict (data first, judgment second); general verdict must precede named attribution (so the reader already knows the shape of the claim); positive endorsement must precede counter-evidence (so the contrast bites); counter-evidence must precede recommendation (you prescribe only after diagnosing); primary recommendation must precede supporting measures (the main lever is named first, then broadened). Each move creates the logical slot the next one fills.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — Origin + delay (origins move)

**SKELETON:** "[Policy X] was first proposed in [year], then repeatedly delayed by [specific force] and [broader political force]."

1. *Slot 1:* "[Policy X]" — noun phrase naming the measure; *Slot 2:* year — single date; *Slot 3:* specific force (e.g. industry, lobby group) + *Slot 4:* broader force (e.g. political inertia, fiscal caution).
2. **How to fill:** pick a real policy you are analysing; google its earliest announcement date; name the narrow opponent AND the wider systemic cause. Use "then repeatedly delayed" so the pattern of postponement is unmistakable.
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "A sugar tax was first proposed in 2015, then repeatedly delayed by beverage-industry lobbying and Treasury concerns about regressive effects on lower-income households."

### Skeleton B — Concessive expert verdict (judgment move)

**SKELETON:** "[Experts] said [the introduction of X] was [positive adjective] but [its immediate impact] would be [limitation]."

1. *Slot 1:* category of expert (plausible and traceable); *Slot 2:* "the introduction of X" — nominalised action; *Slot 3:* approving adjective; *Slot 4:* limitation noun phrase (e.g. "limited," "modest," "short-term").
2. **How to fill:** select a class of authority that fits the topic; nominalise the action ("the introduction of," "the rollout of," "the adoption of"); pair one approving word with one limiting word; the "but" must do real work — both halves must be true.
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "Public-health researchers said the introduction of the HF advertising ban was welcome but its immediate effect on childhood obesity rates would be marginal."

### Skeleton C — Authority endorsement → counter-evidence → imperative (reform move)

**SKELETON:** "[Authority, title, affiliation] said [recent concrete step] is [positive adjective]. However, according to [opposing source]'s own analysis, [current design] [fails to meet criterion]. It is crucial that [lever] is [strengthened] to [achieve stated goal]."

1. *Slot 1:* named authority + role + institution; *Slot 2:* recent concrete step + approving label; *Slot 3:* opposing or internal source; *Slot 4:* current design + shortfall verb; *Slot 5:* lever + action; *Slot 6:* goal phrase.
2. **How to fill:** place the endorsement first so the contrast in sentence two lands harder; the counter-source should outrank the endorsing source (e.g. government analysis beats NGO analyst); the imperative must name a lever AND a goal so the reader knows what success looks like.
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "Dr Amina Yusuf of WHO said the 2023 mask mandate in schools was a responsible move. However, according to the Department of Health's own modelling, current ventilation standards fall short of post-pandemic guidance. It is crucial that school infrastructure budgets are ring-fenced to bring classroom CO₂ levels below 800 ppm."

### Skeleton D — Supporting-measures specification (broadening move)

**SKELETON:** "[This primary lever] has to be accompanied by [broader category of measures], such as [specific example]."

1. *Slot 1:* the lever just named; *Slot 2:* a collective noun ("a range of," "a package of," "a suite of"); *Slot 3:* one concrete instance.
2. **How to fill:** the lever must be the one just prescribed; the category must be obviously broader than the lever; the example must be the most concrete and plausible item in the category. Use "such as" not "e.g." to keep it report-register.
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "This fine has to be accompanied by a package of compliance measures, such as mandatory quarterly pollution-disclosure filings."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / time-anchoring**
- "first proposed in 2010" — opens a historical arc.
- "then repeatedly delayed" — chains a weakening effect to its cause.
- "starting in 2022" — pins a recommendation to a calendar.

**Concession / contrast**
- "but its immediate impact" — splits a verdict into approval + limit.
- "However, according to the South African government's own analysis" — pivots from endorsement to critique.

**Evidence handling / attribution**
- "Climate experts said" — anonymous category authority.
- "said Patrick Curran of LSE" — named + institutional authority.
- "according to the South African government's own analysis" — source that outranks the previous one.

**Specification**
- "such as greater investment in renewable energy" — narrows a broad claim to one example.
- "starting in 2022" — narrows a timeline to a trigger date.
- "a range of supporting policies" — names the category before the instance.

**Explanation / framing verbs**
- "said" — does the work of attribution throughout.
- "has been scaled down" — frames numeric weakening as a deliberate revision, not a slip.
- "will not do enough" — frames the tax as inadequate without litigating the tax itself.

**Purpose / goal clauses**
- "to support meeting South Africa's national and global" — attaches the recommendation to a stated target.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern name:** *Historical trajectory → concessive expert verdict → authority endorsement → counter-evidence → reform imperative → supporting-measures broadening*.

This is an **authority-then-counter-authority-then-prescription** explanation: the writer lets credible voices make the case, then lets a higher-ranked voice puncture it, then prescribes the remedy the puncture demands. To replicate with a new idea, follow these steps in order:

1. **Close the previous move.** Begin with the short clause that completes whatever procedural sentence the prior section left unfinished ("becomes law," "takes effect," "is signed").
2. **Anchor the policy in time.** State when it was first proposed and name two distinct forces that held it back (one narrow, one systemic).
3. **Quantify the dilution.** Show how the original ambition shrank — a percentage, a rate, a budget line — so the reader can see weakening numerically.
4. **Deliver a concessive verdict.** Use "[experts] said … was [good] but [limited]" so the section immediately signals balance.
5. **Name one endorsing authority.** Quote a specific person + institution commenting on a *recent* concrete step, so the endorsement is datable.
6. **Counter with a higher authority.** Pivot on "however" and cite an analysis from inside the government / international body / peer-reviewed source that *itself* admits the policy falls short.
7. **Issue a dated imperative.** Use "it is crucial that … is strengthened … starting in [year] to [meet stated goal]." The verb "crucial" plus a date plus a goal makes the prescription measurable.
8. **Broaden the prescription.** Close by saying the main lever must be accompanied by a wider category of measures, naming one specific example so the broadening is concrete rather than vague.

Each step makes the next one necessary: chronology demands evaluation; evaluation demands attribution; attribution demands critique; critique demands remedy; remedy demands specification.
