## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**

S1 (claim/verdict): "The function that I've found … seems to be accurate through real life testing!" — asserts the model's validity. Hands to S2 because a positive verdict invites a concession ("However").

S2 (concession/cause): "However, deviations in results … thickness of the rings … surrounding air pressure … concentration of the soap solution." — names error sources. Hands to S3 by listing problems, which prompts a "what's needed next" move.

S3 (implication/specification): "For a more accurate model … these would also have to be mathematically defined." — turns the listed causes into a forward requirement. Hands to P2 because the same error logic now drills into a specific instrument cause.

**Paragraph 2**

S1 (specification of error): "Moreover, given that I've used a ruler … prone to parallax error … limited accuracy of measurement to 1 decimal point." — narrows from general factors to one concrete measurement instrument. Hands to S2 via "Despite this", which is the natural re-assertion move after specifying a limitation.

S2 (re-affirmed verdict): "Despite this, however, my equation … seems to be a rather good approximation … in room conditions." — reclaims validity under the stated limit. Hands to S3 because the verdict is followed by "This means that", an implication trigger.

S3 (application chain): "This means that I can use equation 20 … to then compute C … finally rotate that function 360◦ … to obtain the final simulation." — operationalises the verdict as a step-by-step procedure. Hands to P3 by closing the loop on the main model and opening the door to a side observation ("equation 21 also caught my eye").

**Paragraph 3**

S1 (new observation/proposal): "However, equation 21 also caught my eye … almost linear as seen in figure 6, therefore it is worth considering … follow some linear equation and thus a ratio." — flags a secondary pattern worth checking. Hands to S2 via "This can be useful", the standard implication move.

S2 (future implication): "This can be useful in the future for optimisation of my model … the computer will simply have to downscale" — projects the observation onto future use (text cuts off mid-extension).

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

An IB Evaluation/Conclusion section runs these moves, in this order, because each one sets up the next:

1. **Overall verdict on the model** — anchors the reader's takeaway before caveats dilute it.
2. **Concession of general error sources** — once a verdict is stated, the reader expects honesty about weakness; this preserves credibility.
3. **Forward-looking requirement** — converts listed weaknesses into what *would* improve the model, showing reflective thinking.
4. **Drill-down into a specific instrument/measurement error** — moves from abstract factors to a concrete personal experimental limitation, which makes the work feel honest and specific.
5. **Re-affirmation of verdict despite limitation** — "Despite this" restores confidence and reframes the model as *good enough* under stated conditions.
6. **Application chain (use equation X → restrict → compute → rotate)** — shows the verdict is not just praise but actionable; this is the "conclusion leads somewhere" move.
7. **Side observation / follow-up question** — signals scholarly breadth beyond the main question.
8. **Future implication of the side observation** — closes by handing the project forward rather than just ending.

This order works: verdict → caveat → fix → specific caveat → rescue verdict → use-it-this-way → bonus observation → future use.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / addition:** "Moreover, given that" (S1 P2); "to then" (S3 P2); "and finally" (S3 P2).

**Cause / consequence:** "This means that" (S3 P2); "and thus" (S3 P2); "therefore it is worth considering" (S1 P3).

**Contrast / concession:** "However, deviations in results" (S2 P1); "Despite this, however" (S2 P2); "However, equation 21 also caught" (S1 P3).

**Specification / definition:** "such as thickness of the rings" (S2 P1); "as well as limited accuracy" (S1 P2); "in room conditions" (S2 P2); "as seen in figure 6" (S1 P3).

**Evidence handling:** "shown in table 1" (S1 P1); "as seen in figure 6" (S1 P3).

**Verdict / claim verbs:** "seems to be accurate through" (S1 P1); "seems to be a rather good approximation" (S2 P2); "caught my eye" (S1 P3).

**Implication / future-direction verbs:** "For a more accurate model … would have to be mathematically defined" (S3 P1); "This can be useful in the future for optimisation" (S2 P3).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **verdict → concede → operationalise → side-flag pattern** to explain whether a model works and what to do next.

Step 1. **State a soft verdict.** Open with the main finding and hedge with "seems to be" before citing the evidence type that supports it (testing, data, simulation).

Step 2. **Concede with "However".** Immediately introduce limitations the reader expects; group them with "such as" and add escalating variety with "or even".

Step 3. **Project the fix.** Use "For a more [accurate / rigorous / complete] [model / study], these would also have to be [verb]-ed" to convert the list into a forward-looking requirement.

Step 4. **Drill into one specific instrument error.** Move from general factors to a single named tool and its systematic flaw, plus a rounding/precision limit, introduced by "Moreover, given that".

Step 5. **Rescue the verdict.** Use "Despite this, however" to re-assert the model as a "rather good approximation" under a stated, narrowed condition (e.g. "in room conditions").

Step 6. **Operationalise the verdict as a chain.** Begin with "This means that", then string 3–4 actions with "whilst … to then … and finally … to obtain …". This shows the verdict is usable, not just a compliment.

Step 7. **Flag a side observation.** Open the next move with "However, [X] also caught my eye" and attach a visual/numerical cue ("as seen in figure N").

Step 8. **Propose a check and project its use.** Use "therefore it is worth considering and checking whether … follow … and thus …", then close by stating a future benefit: "This can be useful in the future for [purpose], as [agent] will simply have to [action]".
