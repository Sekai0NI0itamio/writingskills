## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **bibliography / works-cited block**, so its "content sequence" is *not* idea-development but **credibility construction**. The moves, in order, are:

1. **Open with a primary source** (interview) → signals the writer has done *original* fieldwork, not just desk research.
2. **Stack a peer-reviewed journal article** → upgrades authority to academic standard.
3. **Insert a specialist non-academic source** (NGO blog) → broadens the evidence base beyond ivory-tower journals.
4. **Return to a primary interview** → re-anchors the voice of the field.
5. **Insert a mainstream news source** (Al Jazeera) → demonstrates public-awareness reach of the issue.
6. **Cluster two specialist journals** → consolidates the academic backbone.
7. **Cite a foundational / older journal** → proves the dispute has historical literature, not just current news.
8. **Close with a primary interview** → mirrors the opening, producing symmetry and the implicit verdict: *the argument rests on both testimony and scholarship.*

**Why this order works (for replication):** Primary → Secondary → Specialist-public → Primary → Mass-media → Academic → Foundational → Primary. The sequence alternates *voice-type* (interview vs publication) so no two adjacent entries are the same *kind* of source; this is what makes a bibliography look rigorous to an IB marker. Ending on a primary source mirrors the opening and visually "frames" the essay's authority.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

A bibliography has a *small* connective vocabulary because it is not arguing, it is **labelling**. The "ideas" it expresses are *categories of evidence*. Grouped by job:

- **Sequencing / numerical ordering:** the numbering itself (`15.`, `16.`, `17.` …) is the sequencing device; it shows the writer has **more than 14 prior sources** and so the list continues rather than restarts.
- **Source-type tagging (explanation verbs):** `"Zoom Interview"` — used as a fixed tag that *defines the source-type* the same way `"defined as"` would define a term.
- **Authority signalling (no connectives, but stylistic markers):** `"DOI: 10.1080/03932729.2017.1333272"` in entry 16 functions like `"according to"` — it locates the claim inside a citable academic record.
- **Container punctuation (specification markers):** the `"vol. 17, no. 2, 2017"` in entry 20 and `"41 Nat. Resources J. 731"` in entry 21 act like `"in particular"` — they specify *where inside* the publication the source lives.
- **Date precision (evidence handling):** `"Oct 5, 2020"`, `"Oct 1, 2020"`, `"Oct 8, 2020"` on the three Zoom interviews — day-level precision does the same job as `"this suggests"`: it makes the evidence *verifiable*.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section does **not** explain a single idea; it builds a **credibility frame** around the essay's claims. The pattern is:

> **Primary → Secondary → Specialist-public → Primary → Mass-media → Academic-cluster → Foundational → Primary-mirror**

**Replication steps to build the same credibility frame for any IB topic:**

1. **Step 1 — Collect ≥ 3 primary sources** (interviews, surveys, fieldwork notes). These are your "voices."
2. **Step 2 — Place one primary source at the start of the bibliography** so the reader sees original testimony before any scholarship. *Function: signals you did the work.*
3. **Step 3 — Add a peer-reviewed journal article** ≤ 10 years old directly after. *Function: anchors the topic inside academic discourse.*
4. **Step 4 — Insert a non-academic but specialist source** (NGO report, government white paper, trade publication). *Function: broadens the evidence base beyond universities.*
5. **Step 5 — Drop a second primary source** (different interviewee). *Function: prevents the academic sources from clustering without voice.*
6. **Step 6 — Add a mainstream news piece** (BBC, Reuters, Al Jazeera). *Function: shows public salience.*
7. **Step 7 — Cluster two more journal articles** (one co-authored, one older/foundational). *Function: builds the academic spine in two beats — current consensus + historical root.*
8. **Step 8 — Close the list with a third primary source.** *Function: mirrors Step 2; the bibliography now begins and ends on a human voice, and every academic citation sits *between* two pieces of testimony. That symmetry is the "verdict" of the section: this argument is grounded in both *what people said* and *what scholarship records.*
