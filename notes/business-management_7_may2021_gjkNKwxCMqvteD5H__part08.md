## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (begins mid-sentence: "begun mass production of Huawei's Kirin 710A chip...")
1. **Continuity anchor + claim** — *"…begun mass production of Huawei's Kirin 710A chip."* — Carries forward from the prior paragraph's setup; hands to the reader the concrete outcome (production has started), so the next sentence must qualify what this product actually is.
2. **Context / scope definition** — *"This processor is designed for a lower tier phone under Huawei's budget Honor brand."* — Narrows the significance of the claim; hands to the reader by signaling "this is small-scale," which forces the next move to ask whether it solves the bigger problem.
3. **Implication / verdict** — *"This chip is only for one device and does not solve Huawei's problems for the rest of its product portfolio."* — Pivots from description to assessment; hands the reader outward by closing the chip's micro-story, opening space for an external reaction.
4. **Evidence / source beat** — *"Huawei declined to comment on the Global Times report."* — Adds a corporate non-response (often treated as data in business writing); hands the reader by removing the company as a source, so the next move must introduce a third-party authority.
5. **Authority quote + evaluation** — *'"TSMC is miles ahead of SMIC in terms of leading edge technology prowess,"'* — Brings in an expert to validate the implication; hands the reader by naming the gap, which sets up the technical detail that explains the gap.

**Paragraph 2** (starts with "Huawei's flagship chip, the Kirin 990…")
1. **Comparison subject / setup** — *"Huawei's flagship chip, the Kirin 990, is based on so-called 7-nanometer technology."* — Sets up a contrasting benchmark to the Kirin 710A; hands to the reader by introducing a term ("7-nanometer") that requires unpacking.
2. **Definition** — *"This essentially refers to the size of certain aspects of the chips."* — Defines the jargon introduced; hands to the reader by promising the consequences of that size.
3. **Mechanism / consequence** — *"These 7nm chips are smaller and faster than predecessors."* — Translates the definition into performance outcomes; hands to the reader by establishing the production maturity required to achieve this.
4. **Evidence on maturity** — *"TSMC has been producing these for around two years."* — Provides proof of who already leads; hands to the reader by leaving SMIC's position open, which the final sentence must close.
5. **Open verdict / implication** — *"It's unclear when SMIC may introduce a 7nm production process at scale."* — Resolves the comparison with uncertainty; hands the reader by signaling the story is still unfolding (cuts off mid-paragraph in the source).

[Actor] declined to comment on the [source] report. On top of that, '[expert verdict in quoted form],' according to [named expert + title], who added [the new development] could be '[secondary risk phrase]' for [actor]."

1. **Slot 1 — Event**: past-tense report of a concrete milestone (3-5 words). Fill: pick one verifiable business outcome tied to a named company.
2. **Slot 2 — Scope qualifier**: "This X is designed for…" narrow market segment. Fill: name the specific sub-brand, tier, or product line.
3. **Slot 3 — Implication**: verdict sentence using "does not solve…for the rest of…". Fill: name the wider portfolio or category the niche piece fails to cover.
4. **Slot 4 — Non-response beat**: "[Actor] declined to comment on the [report name]." Fill: insert the actor and the named publication.
5. **Slot 5 — Authority verdict**: direct quote + "according to [Name], [title]". Fill: pick an industry analyst with a quoted comparison and a one-word risk label ("blow", "setback", "win").

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / continuity**: "This processor is designed for…" (narrows scope); "These 7nm chips are smaller and faster…" (moves from definition to consequence).
- **Contrast / comparison**: "miles ahead of SMIC in terms of…" (institutional benchmark); "smaller and faster than predecessors" (generational benchmark).
- **Authority / evidence handling**: "according to Neil Shah, research director at Counterpoint Research" (credentials inline); "TSMC has been producing these for around two years" (industry-fact evidence).
- **Quoted risk framing**: "could be a 'big blow' for Huawei" (named consequence).
- **Uncertainty / open-ended verbs**: "It's unclear when SMIC may introduce…" (hedged projection).
- **Definition verbs**: "This essentially refers to the size of certain aspects" (jargon-to-plain).
- **Corporate non-response as evidence**: "Huawei declined to comment on the Global Times report" (silence treated as data point).
- **Causal / "on top of that" connectors**: "On top of that, 'TSMC is miles ahead…'" (stacks a new finding on the previous implication).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section relies on the **"jargon → plain definition → comparative consequence → maturity gap → open verdict"** pattern — an explanation chain used whenever a technical term must be justified for a non-specialist reader.

Steps to replicate with a new idea:

1. **Name the flagship example** carrying the term (e.g., the Kirin 990 carrying "7-nanometer").
2. **Introduce the jargon with "so-called"** to signal it is a label, not a fact the reader owns.
3. **Translate the jargon with "This essentially refers to…"** into one plain physical or functional property.
4. **Push the translation into two comparative consequences** using a parallel "-er than predecessors" structure.
5. **Cite one industry actor's maturity** ("has been producing these for around…years") so the consequence is grounded in a real timeline.
6. **Close with a hedged verdict** ("It's unclear when…may introduce…at scale") so the explanation ends in open risk rather than false certainty.
