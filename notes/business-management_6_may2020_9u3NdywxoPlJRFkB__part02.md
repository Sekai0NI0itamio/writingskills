## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A strengths–weaknesses analytical block performs this ordered sequence:

1. **Mirror structure**: two parallel columns where the second is the **logical inverse** of the first, not a new topic.
2. **Lead with the strongest demand-side asset** (enthusiasts/brand support) to anchor the reader in opportunity.
3. **Layer infrastructure + digital + political + leadership evidence**, narrowing from broad endorsement down to a named individual, building cumulative credibility.
4. **Insert a hinge item on the strength side that previews a weakness** (subsidy eligibility) — this is what makes the pivot to weaknesses feel earned rather than abrupt.
5. **Open weaknesses with a soft/cultural gap** (intangible liability) before any numbers.
6. **Quantify the first concrete cost** (price figure), because the reader needs an anchor for severity.
7. **State a precondition that has not been met** (funding / production readiness), which **sets up** the next move.
8. **Deliver the operational consequence** of the unmet precondition at a specific scale (units/week), giving the weakness a measurable failure point.
9. **Loop back to the price figure** and reframe it as an economic constraint rather than a market price, deepening the earlier bullet.
10. **Close on a procedural/regulatory lapse**, the smallest but most current unresolved item — leaves the reader with a pending-action feel.

Why this order works: each strength becomes a target the matching weakness can puncture. The hinge bullet in the strengths column prevents the reader from feeling whiplash when the table flips. The weakness column moves **intangible → quantified → causal → re-quantified → procedural**, which mirrors how a reader naturally escalates concern.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The dominant explanation pattern in this section is **parallel asset-vs-liability mirroring with numeric anchoring**. To explain a new idea using the same pattern:

1. **Decide the two opposing columns** (e.g. opportunities vs threats; enablers vs constraints; assets vs debts). Make sure each row on the left has a *plausible* counterpart on the right.
2. **Open the asset column with the broadest demand-side endorsement** — name a stakeholder group and a verb of approval. This sets the reader's optimism level high.
3. **Stack three more asset rows**, narrowing each time: physical scale → digital mechanism → political relationship → named individual. The narrowing builds credibility through specificity.
4. **Insert one "hinge" asset that doubles as a risk** (e.g. subsidy eligibility, tax relief, regulatory carve-out). This sentence makes the pivot to the liability column feel motivated, not arbitrary.
5. **Open the liability column with an intangible gap** — a "lack-of" phrase (unaware of, untrained in, unfamiliar with). Soft liabilities come before hard numbers because they frame how the numbers will be interpreted.
6. **Quote a price or cost figure** that the reader can hold in mind. Use "approximately" or "roughly" to mark it as an estimate.
7. **State an unmet precondition** ("needs to secure X") — do not yet state the consequence. This creates a *forward-pointing* sentence that pulls the reader to the next bullet.
8. **Deliver the consequence** of that precondition in measurable units ("unable to produce Z per week"). Use "without" to make the cause explicit.
9. **Re-anchor to the earlier cost figure** and re-explain it as an economic constraint, so the reader sees the price from a second angle.
10. **Close on a procedural/regulatory lapse** (a filing, audit, or disclosure still pending). This leaves the section feeling *open* rather than concluded, which is appropriate for a strengths-weaknesses snapshot that precedes a recommendation.
