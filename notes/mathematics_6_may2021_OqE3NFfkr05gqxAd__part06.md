## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Applied context → algorithm authority** ("Adobe. Photoshop." then Dijkstra/IEEE): establishes *relevance and origin* so the reader understands both the "why" and the "who."
2. **Encyclopedic foundation → accessible introduction** (Britannica → Tutorialspoint): moves from *broad overview to simplified entry*, scaffolding the reader before deeper content.
3. **Progressive article chain** (gentle intro → shortest path → theory to practice): *narrows scope* from general concept to specific algorithm to practical representation, building competence stepwise.
4. **Historical origin → educational basics → interactive tool → definitive reference** (Euler → GeeksforGeeks → Dijkstra Solver → Weisstein): *loops from origin back to learning, then to doing, then to citing*, giving the reader historical grounding, understanding, experimentation, and authority in sequence.

**Why this order works for replication**: any student can open with their applied subject, name the key figure, build from general to specific theory, then close with origin, basics, a tool, and a hard reference — this creates a credibility arc from *context → understanding → ability → authority*.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern: Context → Authority → Scaffolded Theory → Progressive Deepening → Historical Root → Consolidation → Tool → Definitive Reference**

1. **Name the applied context** in one or two concrete nouns so the reader immediately sees *where the idea lives in the real world*.
2. **Cite the originator with institutional credential** using the format "[Person] | [Institution]" to establish *who* created the idea and *why their voice matters*.
3. **Open with an encyclopedic reference** then immediately follow with an **accessible introduction** — move from *broad to simple* so the reader is not lost in jargon.
4. **Chain 2–3 articles from general to specific to applied**: pick titles that move *concept → mechanism → implementation*, letting each article's subtitle signal its narrow focus.
5. **Insert the historical origin** of the idea as a *founding problem* from a recognised institution, giving the reader *roots and motivation*.
6. **Add an educational basics resource** from a teaching platform to *consolidate understanding* after the historical and theoretical layers.
7. **Provide an interactive tool or solver** so the reader can *experiment with the idea directly*, bridging reading and doing.
8. **Close with a definitive, citable reference** from an authoritative publisher (e.g., Wolfram, MathWorld) to give the section a *rigorous endpoint* the reader can return to.
