## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**

**Move 1 — Claim (statistical magnitude):** "...mineral production was higher than all of Europe combined"
*Hand-off:* This superlative ("higher than…combined") establishes the magnitude of US industrial capacity, which is the premise that makes the next sentence's market mechanism logically necessary — if supply is this large, the reader expects to hear *how* it gets sold.

**Move 2 — Contrast + mechanism:** "Unlike in Europe, China's 'Open Door' policy prevented the country from administering tariffs to imports, allowing the United States to sell its manufactured goods cheaply."
*Hand-off:* The contrast ("Unlike in Europe") answers the implicit "why could this surplus be absorbed?" by introducing a policy exception as the causal link; the participle "allowing" then delivers the consequence (cheap US sales) that the next sentence's prediction needs to build on.

**Move 3 — Evidence-backed prediction (source + numerical claim):** "With annexation, the Times predicted that 1898 would mark the largest total exports of American history by 12 million dollars from any other year."
*Hand-off:* "With annexation" specifies the trigger for the surge, and the source ("the Times predicted") plus the precise figure ("12 million dollars") converts the abstract mechanism of Move 2 into a concrete, datable outcome — the reader is now handed to the next subject.

**Move 4 — Topic transition (lead-in to next block):** "Hawaii's role"
*Hand-off:* A dangling noun phrase that pivots from the general US export surge to Hawaii's specific position inside that surge, signalling the next paragraph's focus.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **causal-chain evidence block** in the body of a historical argument. The moves run:

1. **Establish magnitude of US industrial output** (statistical claim) — sets up *why* market access mattered at all.
2. **Contrast with Europe + name the policy exception** (Open Door) — supplies the *mechanism* that converts production into export dominance.
3. **Source-cited numerical prediction** (Times, 12 million dollars) — *verifies* the mechanism with a datable, attributed outcome.
4. **Pivot noun phrase** (Hawaii's role) — *opens* the next sub-argument without closing the present one.

The order matters: magnitude → mechanism → verified consequence → pivot. Skipping magnitude leaves the mechanism floating; skipping the mechanism leaves the prediction unmotivated; skipping the source leaves the prediction sounding editorial; skipping the pivot severs Hawaii from the export story. The shape is generalisable: any argument that "X dominates Y because Z policy/condition, proved by statistic W" follows this template.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — "Magnitude → Contrast mechanism → Attributed prediction"**

SKELETON: "[Authority] notes that [Subject A]'s [output metric] was higher than [comparison set]. Unlike in [comparable context], [policy/condition] prevented [restriction], allowing [Subject A] to [advantageous action] cheaply. With [trigger event], [same authority] predicted that [date/year] would mark the [superlative outcome] by [precise figure] from any other [unit]."

1. **Slot 1 — Authority claim:** 6–12 words; past tense reporting verb ("notes," "writes," "records") + comparative-superlative statistic ("higher than all of X combined").
2. **Slot 2 — Contrast + mechanism:** "Unlike in [Europe/peer], [policy] prevented [restriction], allowing [agent] to [action] cheaply." Starts with the contrastive connective, names the specific policy, uses a participle clause to land the consequence.
3. **Slot 3 — Attributed prediction:** "With [event], [authority] predicted that [year] would mark the [superlative] by [number] [unit] from any other [unit]." Includes author/source, trigger event, date, and precise numerical margin.
4. **Slot 4 — Pivot phrase:** 2–4 word noun phrase naming the next case ("Hawaii's role," "Britain's position," "the 1900 figure").

**How to fill with a different idea:** Pick a historical actor, a measurable output (tonnage, revenue, troops, ships), a comparison set (rivals, regions, prior decades), a specific policy exception that explains advantage, a triggering event, and a datable prediction with a margin. Replace each slot accordingly while preserving the comparative-superlative → contrast-mechanism → source-number-magnitude rhythm.

**Original fill:** "New York Times writes that American manufacturing was higher than any European nation, and its mineral production was higher than all of Europe combined. Unlike in Europe, China's 'Open Door' policy prevented the country from administering tariffs to imports, allowing the United States to sell its manufactured goods cheaply. With annexation, the Times predicted that 1898 would mark the largest total exports of American history by 12 million dollars from any other year. Hawaii's role."

Unlike in continental yards, the Navigation Acts' repeal permitted British yards to purchase foreign iron at cost, allowing merchants to undercut rivals on freight. **SKELETON B — "Comparative claim → Single-sentence mechanism → Numerical verification"** (compact variant)

SKELETON: "[Output] was higher than [set]. Unlike in [peer], [policy] prevented [restriction], allowing [agent] to [action] cheaply."

1. **Slot 1:** Comparative-superlative clause.
2. **Slot 2:** Contrast + causal mechanism in one sentence using "Unlike…prevented…allowing."

**How to fill:** Pick the single most striking statistic you have; pick the one policy/condition that explains the gap; collapse both into two sentences. Use when word-count is tight.

**Original fill:** "...mineral production was higher than all of Europe combined. Unlike in Europe, China's 'Open Door' policy prevented the country from administering tariffs to imports, allowing the United States to sell its manufactured goods cheaply."

**Demonstration fill:** "Cotton exports in 1860 exceeded the combined shipments of India and Egypt. Unlike in Confederate ports, Union naval control permitted blockade-runners to be seized, allowing British mills to source exclusively from Northern agents."

**SKELETON C — "Trigger + Source + Superlative + Margin + Unit"** (Move 3 alone)

SKELETON: "With [event], [source] predicted that [year] would mark the [superlative form of metric] by [number] [unit] from any other [unit]."

1. **Slot 1:** "With [event]" — fronted prepositional phrase naming the catalyst.
2. **Slot 2:** "[source] predicted" — named authority as subject.
3. **Slot 3:** "that [year] would mark the [superlative]" — date + ranking verb.
4. **Slot 4:** "by [number] [unit] from any other [unit]" — precise margin and unit of comparison.

**How to fill:** Identify the year, the ranking metric, the numerical gap to the second-best year, and the unit. Slot the catalyst event before the source to make the trigger visible.

**Original fill:** "With annexation, the Times predicted that 1898 would mark the largest total exports of American history by 12 million dollars from any other year."

**Demonstration fill:** "With the Berlin Conference's conclusion, the *Times* predicted that 1885 would mark the largest colonial annexation of European history by 1.4 million square kilometres from any prior decade."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Contrast / concession (opens the mechanism):**
- "Unlike in Europe, China's 'Open Door' policy prevented…" — frames the exceptional case against a peer to justify the advantage that follows.

**Specification (narrows or pins down):**
- "With annexation" — fronted prepositional phrase that names the precise trigger for the prediction.
- "allowing the United States to sell its manufactured goods cheaply" — participle clause specifying the consequence of the policy.

**Evidence handling (sources and citations):**
- "the Times predicted that 1898 would mark" — in-line authority + reporting verb carries the verification onto the named source rather than the writer.
- "...by 12 million dollars from any other year" — paired quantitative margins give the claim auditable specificity.

**Cause / consequence (mechanism verbs):**
- "prevented the country from administering tariffs" — verb of obstruction names the policy's negative capability.
- "allowing the United States to sell…cheaply" — gerund of permission delivers the commercial consequence.

**Comparison / superlative (sets scale):**
- "higher than all of Europe combined" — "combined" intensifies the comparative by aggregating the peer set.
- "largest total exports of American history" — superlative + possessive time-scope ("of American history") makes the ranking maximal.

**Transition / pivot (hand-off to next sub-argument):**
- "Hawaii's role" — bare noun phrase functions as a deferred subject, handing the reader forward without closing the paragraph.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **magnitude → contrast-mechanism → attributed quantitative outcome** pattern. To replicate with a new idea:

1. **Open with a comparative-superlative statistic** that establishes how dominant the subject is *before* explaining *why*. Use "higher than…combined" or equivalent phrasing so the scale is unmissable.
2. **Pivot with "Unlike in [peer context]…"** to introduce a specific policy, condition, or rule that applies to the subject but not its rivals. Name the policy explicitly rather than generalising.
3. **Use a "prevented…allowing…" construction** to convert the policy exception into a concrete advantage — what was blocked, and what was therefore permitted. Keep this in one sentence with the contrast frame.
4. **Anchor the predicted outcome in a named source and a date** ("the Times predicted that 1898…"), then attach a precise numerical margin ("by 12 million dollars") and a unit of comparison ("from any other year").
5. **Close the paragraph with a two-to-four-word pivot noun phrase** naming the next sub-case ("Hawaii's role"), so the reader is handed forward without a transitional sentence.

The pattern works because each move answers the question the previous one raises: *how big?* → *how is that possible?* → *what will it produce?* → *what next?*
