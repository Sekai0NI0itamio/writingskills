## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**

- **Sentence 1 (broad claim + authority support):** "Seaweed has been increasingly lauded worldwide as a food with various health benefits" — positions seaweed as a celebrated foodstuff globally; the next sentence is forced because the claim is still hollow — *what* benefits, by whose account?
- **Sentence 2 (evidence + specification):** "providing 'a rich and sustainable source of macronutrients and micronutrients' … 'the alleviation of risk factors associated with noncommunicable diseases…'" — hands forward by converting the vague "various health benefits" into two quoted, citation-backed specifics (nutrient profile + disease link); now the reader needs to see the real-world manifestation of those benefits, i.e. *how people actually eat it*.
- **Sentence 3 (implication / consequence):** "As such, edible seaweed has been readily consumed by many in the form of snacks, salads, soups and more." — closes the paragraph by drawing the natural consequence (broad claim → quoted evidence → therefore it is eaten in familiar forms); this sentence also sets up the pivot in the next paragraph, where the writer zooms from seaweed-in-general to one specific micronutrient inside it.

**Paragraph 2**

- **Sentence 1 (definition):** "Iodine is a micronutrient that helps to ensure normal thyroid function, growth and development." — opens by defining the term that the previous paragraph only implied; the next sentence must follow because the reader now needs an *applied* standard for iodine — how much is "enough"?
- **Sentence 2 (authority + numerical evidence):** "According to the World Health Organization, the recommended daily iodine intake ranges from 90-250µg… (World Health Organization)." — handles the next logical question (dosage) with an authoritative range; the colon that follows forces a specification, not a new topic.
- **Sentence 3 (specification / unpacking the range):** "90 µg for preschool children … 250 µg for pregnant and lactating women" — the sentence before promised a range *across groups*; this sentence is the only way to honour that promise; it in turn hands the reader to the final sentence because dosage without source is academic — *where does it come from?*
- **Sentence 4 (cause / source enumeration):** "Food and water are major sources of iodine - seaweeds, seafood and dairy products all contribute…" — closes the paragraph by satisfying the implicit question raised throughout (why iodine matters → how much is needed → where it is obtained), and loops back to "seaweed," setting up whatever the writer does next (likely the investigation / aim).

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **Introduction + Background Information** section, so its job is to move from the *familiar* to the *focused* — a funnel. The reliable order is:

1. **Establish broad significance of the topic** — open with a macro-level claim that the subject matter is widely recognised as valuable, so the reader is primed to care.
2. **Cite authoritative evidence for that significance** — quote one or two specific benefits/claims, attributed, to convert assertion into something verifiable.
3. **Show real-world uptake (implication)** — a short consequence move showing that the valued thing is actually consumed/used/observed in everyday life, which legitimises studying it.
4. **Zoom to one specific sub-component** — pivot from the umbrella topic to the single variable the investigation will centre on (here: iodine inside seaweed).
5. **Define that sub-component** — a textbook-style definition that pins the term down for the rest of the essay.
6. **Quantify with authority** — give the recommended/standard measure(s) using an institutional source, because the investigation will likely compare against this benchmark.
7. **Specify the benchmark across subgroups** — break the range down by population (age, condition), demonstrating command of the nuance.
8. **Close by reconnecting to the umbrella topic** — list the dietary sources of the sub-component, deliberately including the original umbrella item, so the reader sees the bridge into the investigation/rationale.

Why this order works: each move answers the unspoken question the previous move created. A broad claim demands evidence; evidence demands an example of uptake; uptake demands a narrower focus; a narrowed focus demands a definition; a definition demands a numerical standard; a numerical standard demands subgroup specification; subgroup specification demands a return to real sources — and that return is the springboard into the writer's own aim.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — "Significance Paragraph"

**SKELETON:** "[Subject] has been increasingly [lauded/studied/adopted] worldwide as a [category] with [adjective] [benefits/properties], providing "[specific claim 1]" and "[specific claim 2]" ([Authority] [ref]). As such, [subject in applied form] has been readily [consumed/used/examined] by many in the form of [example 1], [example 2], [example 3] and more."

1. **Slot 1 (broad claim):** subject + present-perfect passive of a verb meaning "praised" + worldwide scope + category noun phrase. Filled with a topic the reader already half-accepts.
2. **Slot 2 (quoted evidence):** two short quoted phrases from one source, separated by "and," each naming a distinct benefit. Must be in quotation marks with an in-text citation.
3. **Slot 3 (citation):** `(Authority et al. page)` after the closing quote.
4. **Slot 4 (implication sentence):** "As such," + the subject rephrased as something people actually do with it + a list of 3–4 concrete everyday forms.
5. **HOW to fill with a different idea:** pick a food/material/practice widely promoted in 2020s media. Slot 1: name it and one generic positive descriptor (e.g. "increasingly adopted worldwide as a sustainable material"). Slot 2: pull two short benefits straight from one review article, keep them in quotes. Slot 3: attach the author and page. Slot 4: list three to four concrete ways the public encounters it day-to-day.

**Original filled version (paraphrased):** Seaweed increasingly lauded worldwide as a food with various health benefits, providing "a rich and sustainable source of macronutrients and micronutrients" and "the alleviation of risk factors associated with noncommunicable diseases…" (Cherry et al. 1). As such, edible seaweed has been readily consumed by many in the form of snacks, salads, soups and more.

**Demonstration fill with a different idea:** Oats have been increasingly adopted worldwide as a breakfast grain with well-documented health benefits, providing "a high content of beta-glucan linked to reduced LDL cholesterol" and "a slow-release carbohydrate profile that supports sustained satiety" (White et al. 4). As such, rolled oats have been readily consumed by many in the form of porridge, granola bars, overnight oats and more.

### Skeleton B — "Definition + Standard Paragraph"

**SKELETON:** "[Term] is a [category] that helps to ensure [function 1], [function 2] and [function 3]. According to the [Authority], the recommended [daily/standard] [term] intake ranges from [low]–[high] [unit] for people in different age groups and conditions: [low figure] for [group A]; [mid figure] for [group B]; [higher figure] for [group C]; [highest figure] for [group D] ([Authority]). [Source category] are major sources of [term] - [source 1], [source 2] and [source 3] all contribute to the [daily/standard] intake of [term] for the average person."

1. **Slot 1 (definition):** Term + "is a" + category noun + relative clause with three parallel body-functions separated by commas + "and."
2. **Slot 2 (numerical standard opener):** "According to the [Authority]" + recommended quantity + range with units.
3. **Slot 3 (subgroup specification, 3–4 items):** semicolon-separated list, each item: figure + µg/unit + "for" + named group. Order from youngest to most-needy.
4. **Slot 4 (re-citation):** bracketed authority name after the list.
5. **Slot 5 (source enumeration):** major-source sentence beginning with "[Category] are major sources of [term] –" followed by three examples and closing "for the average person."
6. **HOW to fill with a different idea:** pick a micronutrient taught in any IB Biology syllabus (Vitamin D, iron, calcium, folate). Slots 1 and 5 adapt automatically. Slot 2: take the WHO/NIH daily-recommended range. Slot 3: split into the same four population groups (children, adolescents, adults, pregnant/lactating). Slot 4: keep the bracketed citation pattern.

**Original filled version (paraphrased):** Iodine is a micronutrient that helps to ensure normal thyroid function, growth and development. According to the World Health Organization, the recommended daily iodine intake ranges from 90-250µg for people in different age groups and conditions: 90 µg for preschool children; 150 µg for adolescents and adults; 250 µg for pregnant and lactating women (World Health Organization). Food and water are major sources of iodine – seaweeds, seafood and dairy products all contribute to the daily intake of iodine for the average person.

**Demonstration fill with a different idea:** Iron is a mineral that helps to ensure haemoglobin synthesis, oxygen transport and cognitive development. According to the World Health Organization, the recommended daily iron intake ranges from 7–27 mg for people in different age groups and conditions: 7 mg for adolescent boys; 11 mg for menstruating adolescents; 18 mg for adult women; 27 mg for pregnant women (World Health Organization). Food and supplements are major sources of iron – red meat, legumes and fortified cereals all contribute to the daily intake of iron for the average person.

### Skeleton C — "Pivoting Sentence" (optional but useful)

**SKELETON:** "As such, [umbrella subject in its applied form] has been readily [consumed/observed/used] by [audience] in the form of [concrete instance 1], [instance 2], [instance 3] and more."

1. **Slot 1:** connective "As such," marking logical consequence.
2. **Slot 2:** umbrella subject restated as the consumable/observable form.
3. **Slot 3:** present-perfect passive of an everyday verb (consumed, used, worn, applied).
4. **Slot 4:** three to four short noun phrases of common encounters, Oxford-comma or "and more" close.
5. **HOW to fill:** any topic you can depict both as an abstract benefit and as something readers physically encounter. Slot 2 = the consumer-facing noun; Slot 3 = the matching everyday verb; Slot 4 = list 3–4 formats people meet it in.

**Original filled version:** "As such, edible seaweed has been readily consumed by many in the form of snacks, salads, soups and more."

**Demonstration fill:** "As such, probiotic yoghurt has been readily consumed by many in the form of breakfast pots, smoothies, frozen lollies and more."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / logical connectors**
- "As such" → *As such, edible seaweed has been readily consumed…* (consequence pivot from preceding evidence to real-world use).
- "According to the" → *According to the World Health Organization, the recommended…* (introduces authoritative standard before any number).
- "all contribute to" → *seaweeds, seafood and dairy products all contribute to…* (parallel-source connector inside an enumeration).

**Cause / consequence**
- "providing" → *providing "a rich and sustainable source…"* (presents benefit as the direct result of the subject's nature).
- "helps to ensure" → *helps to ensure normal thyroid function, growth and development* (mechanism verb linking nutrient to bodily outcomes).

**Specification / unpacking**
- colon after "different age groups and conditions:" → splits the 90-250µg range into named subgroups immediately after the range is stated.
- semicolon list "90 µg for preschool children … 250 µg for pregnant and lactating women" → parallel structure expanding a single number into a graded catalogue.

**Evidence handling**
- quotation marks around phrases pulled verbatim from Cherry et al. → signals secondary citation, not original claim.
- bracketed author + page `(Cherry et al. 1)` and `(World Health Organization)` → standard IB in-text citation shape, placed at the sentence-end pivot point.

**Explanation / definition verbs**
- "is a micronutrient that helps to ensure" → textbook definition pattern: category noun + relative-clause mechanism.
- "is defined as / ranges from" (implicit via "recommended daily … intake ranges from") → quantitative definition of adequacy.

**Transition between paragraphs**
- absence of an explicit connective — the link from "snacks, salads, soups and more" in paragraph 1 to "Iodine is a micronutrient…" in paragraph 2 is achieved by *narrowing focus* (umbrella → component), not by a hinge word; the writer trusts the reader to follow the zoom.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **Macro-claim → Authority evidence → Real-world uptake → Zoom-in → Definition → Quantified standard → Subgroup specification → Source enumeration** pattern. To replicate it for a new IB topic, execute these steps in order:

1. **Open with a macro-claim.** State in the present perfect passive that the subject is being increasingly recognised worldwide for X benefits. Use the pattern "Subject + has been increasingly [lauded/studied/adopted] worldwide as a [category] with [adjective] [benefits]."
2. **Cite one authority with two short quotes.** Find a single review-style source that names two distinct benefits. Embed both as brief verbatim phrases inside one sentence, separated by "and," and close with `(Author et al. page)`.
3. **Draw the real-world implication.** Begin the next sentence with "As such," and list 3–4 everyday forms in which the subject is encountered by ordinary people. This proves the topic matters beyond the lab.
4. **Pivot to a single sub-component.** Start a new paragraph by naming one specific ingredient/nutrient/variable *inside* the subject that the investigation will examine.
5. **Define it in one sentence.** Use the formula "[Term] is a [category] that helps to ensure [function 1], [function 2] and [function 3]."
6. **Introduce a quantified standard from a recognised body.** Begin "According to the [Authority], the recommended [daily] [term] [intake/level] ranges from [low]–[high] [unit]…"
7. **Specify the standard across 3–4 subgroups.** Use a colon after the range, then semicolon-separated clauses of the form "[figure] [unit] for [group]." Order groups from lowest requirement to highest.
8. **Re-cite the authority** in brackets at sentence end.
9. **Close by listing the real sources of the sub-component.** Begin "[Sources] are major sources of [term] – [source 1], [source 2] and [source 3] all contribute to the [daily] intake of [term] for the average person." Ensure one of the three sources is the original umbrella subject, so the paragraph visually loops back.
10. **Stop.** Do *not* state the aim or research question here — let the next section pick up from the loop-closed paragraph.
