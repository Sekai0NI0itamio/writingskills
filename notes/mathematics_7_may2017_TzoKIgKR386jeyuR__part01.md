## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **motivation-of-research** section. The ordered content moves are:

1. **Carry-over claim** — picks up the threat claim from the prior section so the section feels continuous (sets up: "I will now show you why this is serious").
2. **Real-world evidence of the threat** — a recent outbreak (sets up: "this isn't abstract").
3. **Personal triggering event** — a game/film/book/lecture that sparked curiosity (sets up: "I didn't enter this cold").
4. **Definition + mechanism of the triggering event** — explains the surface content of the trigger (sets up: "you'll see why it mirrors the real topic").
5. **Causal bridge** — combines the trigger with the real outbreak to motivate the research direction (sets up: "here is what I chose to study").
6. **Selection of the specific model with authority + personal stake** — names the model, cites a real use, anchors it emotionally (sets up the *next* section: defining or unpacking the SIR Model itself).

The order works because it moves from *abstract danger* → *concrete recent case* → *personal anecdote* → *anecdote's logic* → *research direction* → *justified specific choice*, so the reader is emotionally invested in the model before its technical content begins.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / handover**
- *adding to* — "This, adding to the recent outbreak of the Zika virus, led me" (stacks a second cause onto a first).
- *grew after* — "My interest in this topic grew after I played" (marks the trigger event in time).

**Cause / consequence**
- *led me to researching* — "led me to researching epidemic models" (frames a personal choice as a logical consequence of two inputs).

**Specification / apposition**
- *such as* — "their properties, such as the virus' probability of infection" (zooms in from category to instances).
- *a time when* — "Hong Kong in 2003, a time when all of us living in Hong Kong lived in fear" (appends emotional context to a date).

**Authority / evidence handling**
- *stood out as it was used before by* — "The SIR Model stood out as it was used before by the Hong Kong Department of Health" (selection justified by institutional application).

**Definition / explanation verbs**
- *is a game in which you can* — "Plague Inc is a game in which you can create different types of virus" (defines by capability, not category).
- *The goal of … is to* — "The goal of the game is to kill the population" (states function).
- *A recent example would be* — "A recent example would be our inability to control" (labels what follows as illustrative).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **concrete-instance-to-research-justification chain**. It does *not* define the model directly; instead it justifies *why* the model deserves the next section's attention. To replicate:

1. **Open with a residual claim** — pick up a sentence fragment from your prior section so the reader feels continuity. (One noun phrase + verb of ongoing threat.)
2. **Anchor with a recent real-world failure** — name a specific event, place, and date where the topic's danger was visible. This converts abstract → concrete.
3. **Introduce a personal trigger** — state *what you personally did* (played, watched, read, visited) that sparked curiosity. Use past tense; name the medium.
4. **Define the trigger by what the user can do inside it** — rather than describing what it "is," describe what it *lets you do*, and list 2–3 adjustable variables to echo your topic's parameters.
5. **Bridge via accumulation** — combine the trigger (step 3–4) with the real event (step 2) into a single causal clause ending in *"led me to researching [general field]"*. Use *adding to* or *combined with*.
6. **Select and justify the specific tool** — name *one* model/method, cite *one* institution that used it in *one* historical case, and append an appositive that anchors that case in your own emotional memory. This licences the technical content that follows.
