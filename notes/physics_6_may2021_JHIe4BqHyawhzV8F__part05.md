## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** — context → loss → mitigation → limit → quantify → mechanism A → mechanism B → consequence → hypothesis

1. **Context / mechanism** — "In an engine, a combustion reaction occurs to the fuel, producing heat and counterforce for the piston, forcing it to move." Sets the working engine. Hands the reader to sentence 2 by leaving the word "counterforce" as a dangling noun needing a label.
2. **Claim / definition** — "This counterforce is the useful energy generated from the combustion reaction." Names the useful output. Hands to sentence 3 because "useful" invites the question of how much is *not* useful.
3. **Contrast / problem-statement** — "Nevertheless, more energy is lost due to the friction of the components in the engine." "Nevertheless" pivots from gain to loss. Hands to sentence 4 by raising the need for a fix.
4. **Solution / definition** — "Engine oil is used to lower friction between the surfaces within the engine block to raise efficiency." Standard mitigation introduced. Hands to sentence 5 by implying, but not yet showing, a flaw.
5. **Concession / limit-statement** — "However, engine oil shows limitations as it starts to lose its purpose of providing more frictionless contact between components." "However" signals the flaw. Hands to sentence 6 by asking *when* the limit kicks in.
6. **Specification / evidence** — "The engine oil's temperature boundaries where it starts to lose its purpose are at boundaries: lower than 3°C; higher than 300°C." Numerical thresholds. Hands to sentence 7 by choosing one endpoint first (high T).
7. **Mechanism A / unpack** — "When engine oil temperature rises towards 300°C, it starts to lose its properties, such as becoming thinner and its viscosity drops." High-end physics. Hands to sentence 8 by leaving the lower boundary still unexplored ("Whereas…" mirrors it).
8. **Mechanism B / comparison** — "Whereas when it drops towards 3°C the liquid starts to thicken and delays the engine components' movements." Low-end parallel. Hands to sentence 9 by stacking two failures awaiting one consequence.
9. **Consequence / implication** — "These will hamper the movement of the piston, leading to less energy generated." Aggregates both failures into one outcome that loops back to sentence 2's "useful energy." Hands to sentence 10 because the consequence is now framed as testable.
10. **Verdict / hypothesis** — "Considering this, I hypothesise that the increase in engine oil's temperature from the normal (room) temperature, will increase the dynamic friction between the components." "Considering this" closes the chain and operationalises a single variable (temperature). Hands to Paragraph 2 because the experiment that tests this claim needs equipment.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **theory → hypothesis → materials** opening for an experimental IA. The order is fixed by the logic of inquiry:

1. **Establish the system in one clause** so the reader knows the machine the problem lives inside.
2. **Define the useful output** so "loss" later has a baseline to subtract from.
3. **State the dominant loss mechanism** (the variable's antagonist: friction).
4. **Name the industry solution** to that loss (engine oil) — without this, the student's own investigation looks unmotivated.
5. **Reveal the solution's flaw** — this is the *gap* the experiment will exploit.
6. **Quantify the gap's boundaries** with numbers; vague flaws don't justify experiments.
7. **Unpack mechanism A** at one boundary (high T).
8. **Unpack mechanism B** at the opposite boundary (low T), using a parallel clause ("Whereas…").
9. **Aggregate both mechanisms into a single consequence** that loops back to the useful output named in step 2 — the loop is what makes the reader feel the chain is closed.
10. **Operationalise into a hypothesis** that names one independent variable and one dependent variable.
11. **Pivot to materials** with a header.
12. For each material: **specification → role-as-analogy → material justification → scope-limit** before moving to the next item. The order is fixed because each slot answers the question the previous slot raised (What is it? → What does it stand for? → Why this material? → How am I controlling for alternatives?).

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — "gap-and-hypothesis" paragraph**

[Mitigation] is used to lower [loss mechanism] between the [surfaces] to raise [efficiency metric]. However, [mitigation] shows limitations as it starts to lose its purpose of [intended effect]. The [mitigation]'s [boundary variable] boundaries where it starts to lose its purpose are at boundaries: [low limit]; [high limit]. When [boundary variable] rises towards [high limit], it starts to lose its properties, such as [symptom A]. Whereas when it drops towards [low limit] [the substance] starts to [symptom B]. These will hamper the movement of the [component], leading to less [useful quantity] generated. Considering this, I hypothesise that the [change in independent variable] from the [baseline condition], will [predicted change in dependent variable]."

1. *What each slot holds:* the system/process slot is one noun-phrase clause; output slots are concrete results; loss mechanism is a physics term; mitigation is the industry fix; boundary variable is a measurable physical quantity; symptoms are physics consequences (thinner, thicker); component is the moving part; useful quantity is the energy you started with; independent variable is the manipulated quantity; baseline is room/normal condition; predicted change is the measurable effect.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / move markers**
- "forcing it to move" — descriptive, not "firstly" — the writer relies on grammar (participle clause) to chain causation, not on enumerated adverbs.

**Cause / consequence**
- "producing heat and counterforce for the piston" — verb-ing chain: process → outputs.
- "leading to less energy generated" — participle wrapping a consequence.
- "due to the friction of the components" — prepositional cause.

**Contrast / concession**
- "Nevertheless, more energy is lost" — pivots from gain to loss.
- "However, engine oil shows limitations" — pivots from fix to flaw.
- "Whereas when it drops towards 3°C" — parallel mechanism in opposite direction.

**Specification**
- "at boundaries: lower than 3°C; higher than 300°C" — colon-list of thresholds.
- "such as becoming thinner and its viscosity drops" — exemplar list after a general claim.
- "with 4x8x1 cm (0.1kg ± 0.005kg)" — bracketed dimensions.

**Evidence / authority**
- "due to the fact that most of the vehicles use aluminium" — appeals to industry practice, not a citation in this clause itself.

**Explanation verbs**
- "is used to lower friction" — passive + purpose.
- "starts to lose its purpose" — repetitive "purpose" anchors the definition.
- "These will hamper the movement" — forward-pointing demonstrative pronoun chains the two mechanisms.
- "I hypothesise that … will increase" — present-future prediction verb.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is **system → useful output → loss → industry fix → fix's limit → quantified thresholds → paired mechanisms (mirror) → aggregate consequence → operationalised hypothesis**.

1. **Open with the system.** One clause that names where the phenomenon happens ("In an engine…"). Do not define yet.
2. **Name the useful output.** Use "This [output] is the [useful quantity] generated from the [process]." This becomes the baseline you later subtract from.
3. **State the loss with "Nevertheless."** Same useful quantity, but more is lost. Keep the loss mechanism to one noun phrase (friction, heat loss, drag).
4. **Introduce the standard mitigation.** "[Mitigation] is used to lower [loss] between the [surfaces] to raise [efficiency]." Do not yet show the flaw.
5. **Puncture it with "However."** "However, [mitigation] shows limitations as it starts to lose its purpose." Use the same word ("purpose") you used in step 4 so the reader sees the reversal.
6. **Quantify the limits.** "The [mitigation]'s [boundary variable] boundaries where it starts to lose its purpose are at boundaries: [low]; [high]." Two numbers, colon-separated.
7. **Unpack one end.** "When [boundary variable] rises towards [high], it starts to lose its properties, such as [symptom]." Stay in cause language ("such as").
8. **Mirror with "Whereas."** "Whereas when it drops towards [low] [the substance] starts to [opposite symptom]." Same grammar, opposite direction.
9. **Aggregate with "These will."** "These will hamper [the component], leading to less [useful quantity] generated." Loop the useful quantity from step 2.
10. **Close with "Considering this, I hypothesise that."** Pick one independent variable and one dependent variable. The hypothesis must be testable with the equipment list that follows.
11. **(Materials pivot)** For each item: spec → role → material justification ("due to the fact that…") → scope limit ("I will limit my investigation…").
