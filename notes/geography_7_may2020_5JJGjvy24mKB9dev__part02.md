## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**

1. **Claim/context.** "57% of those living in Tower Hamlets are employed, the LB experiences an unemployment rate of 6.7%." This establishes the labor-market baseline; it hands the reader to the next sentence by prompting the question of what is driving these employment conditions — answered by the growth data that follows.

2. **Evidence/mechanism.** "From 1990, Tower Hamlets has seen economic growth, with 200 thousand jobs being available, resulting in 3 jobs being available for every 2 residents." This provides the temporal and quantitative mechanism behind the employment figure; it hands the reader to the next sentence by setting up a contrast — growth in jobs does not yet mean prosperity, which the next sentence reveals.

3. **Contrast/implication.** "Nonetheless, the median income in TH is £30 thousand, compared to the £45 thousand in the City of London." This introduces the income disparity that qualifies the growth narrative; the paragraph closes here, leaving the reader with the tension between job availability and low pay — a tension the next paragraph extends from the labor market into living conditions.

**Paragraph 2**

1. **Transition/claim.** "Simultaneously, 42% of the population lives in social housing, a number higher than the London average." This shifts the lens from employment to housing; it hands the reader to the next sentence by specifying how the housing problem manifests concretely — through repossession orders.

2. **Evidence/specification.** "In 2014, 10 outright orders to repossess property are executed by TH landlords per 1000 renting, though only 5 of these ended in eviction." This quantifies the repossession mechanism and adds a concession (most do not result in eviction); it hands the reader to the next sentence by raising whether the situation is worsening — answered by the temporal trend that follows.

3. **Evidence/trend.** "These orders to repossess are rising; 2003 saw approximately 300 repossession orders, whereas 2015 saw a threefold increase of approximately 900." This shows the trajectory over time; the paragraph closes with an accelerating problem, handing the reader to the next paragraph, which shifts from housing hardship to the commercial landscape.

**Paragraph 3**

1. **Claim/definition.** "88.6% of the enterprises in 2013 employed less than 10 people, making microbusinesses the majority of commercial activity." This defines the enterprise structure of TH; it hands the reader to the next sentence by inviting a closer look at the age profile of these businesses.

2. **Evidence/specification.** "In the same year, 27.8% of businesses were younger than two years, 25% being older than ten years." This adds the temporal dimension of business age; it hands the reader to the next sentence by setting up the dynamic of business turnover — birth versus death rates.

3. **Evidence/implication.** "2012 saw an enterprise birth rate of 17.4% compared to the death rate of 12.3%, suggesting an increase in business numbers." This provides the turnover mechanism and draws an implication; the sentence is cut off at "Businesses are," leaving the reader expecting a continuation of the commercial analysis.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Labor-market baseline** — employment and unemployment rates are stated first to anchor the section in the workforce. This sets up the question of whether employment translates to prosperity.
2. **Economic growth vs. income contrast** — job availability and growth are presented, then immediately qualified by median income disparity. This moves the reader from "are people working?" to "does work pay?"
3. **Housing conditions and repossession trend** — social housing prevalence is introduced as a consequence of low income, then repossession orders quantify the housing crisis, and the temporal trend shows escalation. This moves the reader from income to living security.
4. **Enterprise structure and dynamics** — the section pivots from residential hardship to the commercial landscape: microbusiness dominance, age distribution, and birth/death rates. This moves the reader from housing to the business environment that shapes employment opportunities.

**Why this order:** Each move builds on the previous dimension — labor → income → housing → commerce — creating a layered socio-economic portrait. A student replicating this sequence would first establish the workforce, then qualify it with purchasing power, then show living conditions, then examine the commercial engine driving it all.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / transition:**
- "Simultaneously" — "Simultaneously, 42% of the population lives in social housing" (adds a parallel dimension at the same logical level)

**Contrast / concession:**
- "Nonetheless" — "Nonetheless, the median income in TH is £30 thousand" (signals a counterpoint to the preceding growth claim)
- "though" — "though only 5 of these ended in eviction" (concession that qualifies the severity of repossession orders)
- "whereas" — "whereas 2015 saw a threefold increase" (sets two temporal data points in opposition to show change)

**Cause / consequence:**
- "resulting in" — "resulting in 3 jobs being available for every 2 residents" (shows the direct outcome of economic growth)
- "suggesting" — "suggesting an increase in business numbers" (draws an implication from the birth/death rate comparison)

**Specification / comparison:**
- "compared to" — "compared to the £45 thousand in the City of London" (anchors the median income against a benchmark)
- "higher than" — "a number higher than the London average" (specifies the social housing statistic against a reference point)

**Evidence handling:**
- "saw" — "2003 saw approximately 300 repossession orders" (introduces a dated data point as evidence)

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern:** This section relies on a **statistical anchor → comparative/temporal evidence → contrast or implication** chain. Each idea is introduced with a hard number, supported by either a comparison or a trend, and closed with a contrast or inference that qualifies the initial claim.

**Steps to explain a new idea with the same pattern:**

1. **State the core statistic as an anchor.** Open with a percentage, rate, or quantity that defines the phenomenon, paired with a citation.
2. **Provide temporal or comparative context.** Add a second data point — either from a different time or against a different benchmark — that expands or qualifies the anchor.
3. **Introduce a contrast or consequence.** Use a contrast connective ("nonetheless," "though," "whereas") or a consequence verb ("resulting in," "suggesting") to reveal what the numbers imply or what they fail to show.
4. **Close with the implication.** End the move on the inference the data supports, leaving the reader with a qualified understanding rather than a raw figure.
