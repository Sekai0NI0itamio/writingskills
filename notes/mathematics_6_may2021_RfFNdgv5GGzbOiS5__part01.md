## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (single paragraph, 4 sentences)

**Sentence 1:** *"[I got] surprised by some of the extreme average life expectancies listed in the book, such as 53 for the Central African Republic and 84 for Japan, or 54 for Nigeria and 82-83 for most of the European countries."*
- Move: **personal reaction + illustrative evidence** (past-participle clause continuing from the previous section, then a "such as / or" pair of paired numerical examples).
- Quote: "surprised by some of the extreme"
- Hands to the next sentence by **exposing an unanswered contrast** — the examples show a huge gap, so the reader is set up to expect a "why?".

**Sentence 2:** *"Then I wondered what were the main factors creating such huge gaps between the life expectancy levels of developing and developed countries."*
- Move: **transition + research aim** (anaphoric "Then" picks up the surprise; embedded wh-question states the investigation's driving question).
- Quote: "Then I wondered"
- Hands to the next sentence by **announcing research** — the question's "what were the main factors" signals that data will follow, so the reader expects an evidence-based answer.

**Sentence 3:** *"After doing some research on the World Bank database, I found that the developed countries on average spend $2 000-$10 000 per capita on health, whereas developing countries spend $30-$700 with an exception of some more robust economies spending up to $1 000."*
- Move: **source citation + comparative data + nuance** ("After doing some research" = credibility; "I found that" introduces the finding; "whereas" stages the contrast; the trailing "with an exception" pre-empts over-generalisation).
- Quote: "After doing some research"
- Hands to the next sentence by **presenting raw parallel figures** that the writer has not yet interpreted — the reader is positioned to expect a conclusion drawn from those figures.

**Sentence 4:** *"Just by looking at the values, I noticed that there was a clear correlation between the health expenditure per capita and the life expectancy of countries."*
- Move: **verdict / observation** (cause→inference marker "Just by looking at the values" tells the reader the conclusion is being drawn from the previous sentence's numbers; "clear correlation" is the thesis-style claim).
- Quote: "Just by looking at the values"
- Closes the loop: the surprise → question → data chain resolves into a single correlation claim that the next section will interrogate.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **research-narrative hook / "puzzle → inquiry → data → inference" section**. The ordered moves are:

1. **Personal surprise at a striking real-world contrast** — establishes the writer as a curious observer, not an authority; frames the topic as something the reader can also feel puzzled by.
2. **Concrete paired numerical examples** — anchors the surprise in specifics so it is credible.
3. **Implicit "why" turned into an explicit research question** — converts emotion into a researchable aim; gives the section a job to do.
4. **Source citation + database name** — establishes that the next claim is not opinion.
5. **Quantitative comparison (group A range vs group B range)** — supplies the evidence that answers the question from step 3.
6. **Acknowledged exception / nuance** ("with an exception of…") — pre-empts the objection that the contrast is too neat, signalling academic care.
7. **Inference / verdict drawn directly from the data** — closes the section by answering the question from step 3.

**Why this order:** emotion (1–2) creates motivation; the question (3) converts motivation into aim; the source (4) earns the right to answer; the comparison (5) supplies the answer; the exception (6) defends it; the verdict (7) states it crisply. Each move sets up the next — without the surprise there is no wonder, without the wonder there is no research aim, without the data the verdict is empty, without the exception the verdict looks naive.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / narrative hinge**
- "Then I wondered" — *"Then I wondered what were the main factors creating such huge gaps…"* (carries the paragraph forward from reaction to inquiry).
- "After doing some research on" — *"After doing some research on the World Bank database…"* (marks the move into evidence-gathering).

**Cause / consequence (implicit)**
- "creating such huge gaps" — *"the main factors creating such huge gaps…"* (frames the upcoming variables as causal).
- "Just by looking at the values, I noticed" — *"Just by looking at the values, I noticed that there was a clear correlation…"* (positions the verdict as directly caused by the preceding numbers).

**Contrast / concession**
- "whereas" — *"developed countries on average spend $2 000-$10 000 … whereas developing countries spend $30-$700…"* (stages the group-vs-group comparison).
- "with an exception of" — *"with an exception of some more robust economies spending up to $1 000"* (admits outliers without abandoning the general claim).

**Specification / exemplification**
- "such as" — *"such as 53 for the Central African Republic and 84 for Japan…"* (turns the abstract "extreme values" into named instances).
- "on average" — *"the developed countries on average spend $2 000-$10 000…"* (narrows the claim from any individual country to the typical case).

**Evidence handling / source markers**
- "listed in the book" — *"the extreme average life expectancies listed in the book…"* (attributes the figures).
- "After doing some research on the World Bank database" — *"After doing some research on the World Bank database…"* (attributes the data).

**Reportive / observation verbs**
- "I found that" — *"I found that the developed countries on average spend $2 000-$10 000…"* (introduces the finding).
- "I noticed that there was" — *"I noticed that there was a clear correlation…"* (introduces the inference).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section uses is: **personal hook (concrete contrast) → driving question → source-grounded comparison → correlation claim**. It is *not* a worked calculation, an authority-citation, or a definition→unpack→example pattern; it is a **puzzle-and-data** pattern that mimics the shape of a scientific investigation at miniature scale.

To replicate it on a **new** idea, follow these steps in order:

1. **Pick one striking real-world contrast** in your topic where two named cases sit at opposite ends of a scale (e.g. country A = 53, country B = 84). Hold only that contrast — do not yet explain it.
2. **Write the opener as a past-participle reaction** ("surprised by / intrigued by / struck by …") and feed in **two paired numerical examples** using the syntax "such as X for [Place 1] and Y for [Place 2], or X for [Place 3] and Y for [Place 4]". The "such as … or …" structure is what carries the contrast.
3. **Convert the contrast into an explicit research question** beginning with "Then I wondered" and the wh-word "what" plus a plural noun ("what were the main factors / primary drivers / underlying causes …"). Make sure the noun phrases inside the question echo the two groups you named in step 2.
4. **Cite a real, named database or report** ("After doing some research on the [World Bank / UNESCO / IMF / IEA] database") — this single clause is what makes the next claim admissible rather than opinion.
5. **Give one range for group A and one for group B** separated by "whereas", and ensure the ranges differ by at least an order of magnitude so the contrast is visible in the numbers themselves.
6. **Append a brief exception clause** ("with an exception of some [qualifier] [outlier value]") so the comparison does not read as absolute.
7. **Close with a verdict anchored to the figures**, using the hinge "Just by looking at the values, I noticed that there was a clear correlation between [variable 1] and [variable 2]." Keep the verdict to a single correlation claim — the section's job is to *raise* the link, not yet to *prove* it (proof comes in later sections).
