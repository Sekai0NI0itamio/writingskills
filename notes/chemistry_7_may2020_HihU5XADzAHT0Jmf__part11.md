## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Equipment advantage** — establishes that the setup achieved even thermal distribution. This grounds the reader in physical reliability before moving to analytical reliability.
2. **Analytical method advantage** — introduces the integrated-rate law as a viable tool. This shifts from *hardware* to *software* (data analysis) reliability.
3. **Comparison with alternatives** — justifies the chosen method by contrasting it with a less objective approach (initial-rate method). This answers "why this and not that?"
4. **Concession + verdict** — acknowledges the method's mathematical burden but concludes it yields greater accuracy. This prevents the strength argument from sounding one-sided and creates a balanced tone.
5. **Limitation pivot** — "However" signals a tonal shift from strengths to weaknesses. This is the standard IA rhetorical move: establish credibility, then show self-awareness.
6. **Limitation mechanism** — specifies that repeated titrations were the tracking method. This gives the reader a concrete target for the critique that follows.
7. **Critique intensifier** (incomplete) — "Not only was this method of…" signals an escalating criticism. Generalized sequence: **feature → advantage → justification → concession → pivot → mechanism → critique**. Any student can replicate this by listing what their method does well, defending each choice, then honestly naming what it does poorly.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is: **feature → accumulated advantage → comparative justification → conceded cost + verdict → contrast pivot → limitation mechanism**. This is a "strengths-then-honest-limitations" explanation pattern common in IA discussion sections.

1. **State the feature** — name one thing your method or setup did well, in past tense, with one concrete detail (e.g., "the water bath reached the target temperature within 30 seconds").
2. **Accumulate** — add a second advantage using "Furthermore" or "Additionally," naming a different tool, law, or design choice and what it enabled.
3. **Justify by comparison** — name the alternative method you rejected, then explain *why* it was weaker using a specific flaw (e.g., subjectivity, inaccuracy, time-loss). Use "because" and "therefore" to make the causal chain explicit.
4. **Concede and verdict** — admit one real cost of your chosen method (more time, more math, more expense), then state the net benefit using "While…it probably led to…"
5. **Pivot** — open a new paragraph with "However, despite…" to signal that you are now naming a limitation, which shows self-awareness and balances the argument.
6. **Describe the limitation mechanism** — name the specific step in your procedure that created the problem, using "To [purpose], [action] was carried out" so the reader sees exactly where the weakness entered.
7. **Intensify** (if space allows) — begin a sentence with "Not only was this method of…" to signal that the critique will escalate, adding a second or third drawback.
