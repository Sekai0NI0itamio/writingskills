## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **theoretical / derivation section** that establishes the math the experiment will exploit. The order is:

1. **Show the reaction equation** — gives the reaction the rest of the section depends on.
2. **Practical post-reaction note** — anchors the reaction in lab reality.
3. **Research question** — states the dependent and independent variables.
4. **Validation strategy** — tells the reader how success will be judged (Ea comparison).
5. **Define the validation quantity (Ea)** — gives terms before they are used in equations.
6. **Unpack that definition physically** — "This means that…" so the math later has intuitive meaning.
7. **State the governing named law (Arrhenius)** — anchors the section in established theory.
8. **Specify every constant/variable with units** — closes the door on ambiguity.
9. **Offer a more usable transformed form (log/linearised)** — same law, plot-friendly.
10. **State the graphical/quantitative consequence** — links algebra to a line on graph paper.
11. **Acknowledge experimental gap** — "However, … is needed" → pivots from theory to rate law.
12. **Assert reaction order with citation** — needed before writing the rate law.
13. **Write the rate law** — general form first.
14. **Specialise (c = cA = cB)** — match the rate law to the actual experimental setup.
15. **Reformulate as a differential equation** — bridge to calculus.
16. **Show the integration procedure** — the technical step.
17. **Specify symbols of the integrated result** — label c₀, c, t.
18. **Name the result** — hand the reader a usable equation.

The reason for this order: each move supplies the **symbol, assumption, or justification** the next move needs. Theory must precede application; definition must precede equation; general equation must precede specialised case; algebra must precede integration; symbols must precede naming.

- **Contrast / concession:** "However" ("a method of determining the rate constants… is needed").

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **Theory → Unpack → Specified Equation → Transformed Form → Implication → Experimental Gap** pattern, with a parallel **Rate-Law → Specialisation → Differential Form → Integration → Named Result** derivation pattern chained on.

To explain a NEW idea in this pattern:

1. **Open with the governing law in its raw form.** Name it, cite a textbook (parenthetical: Author, Year, pgs.), and display the equation.
2. **Specify every symbol and constant with units and physical meaning** in a "where…" clause. Use parentheses around numerical values with units.
3. **Offer a transformed form that is more convenient for plotting or computation.** Use "Alternatively, … is often better expressed in its [form]:" to introduce it.
4. **State the graphical or quantitative consequence of the transformed form** ("As such, we can see that a plot of X against Y should result in a straight line of gradient …"). This converts algebra into a method.
5. **Acknowledge what is still needed** ("However, a method of determining … is needed") to pivot to the rate-law derivation.
6. **Begin the rate-law derivation by asserting reaction order with citation** ("Reaction (n) is [order] order (Citation)"). Order is never assumed; it is cited.
7. **Write the general rate law and specify variables** ("where cA and cB are…").
8. **Specialise to the experimental case** ("Considering the case in which c = cA = cB, our rate expression becomes:") and display the simpler equation.
9. **Reformulate as a differential equation** ("Mathematically, … can be expressed as a differential equation as follows:").
10. **Show the integration procedure in two compact steps** ("Separating and integrating both sides, we get:"), ending in a numbered equation.
11. **Define every new symbol** introduced by the integration ("where c₀ is the initial concentration and c is the concentration at time t").
12. **Name the result** ("This is known as the integrated [name] rate law.") so the reader has a label to use downstream.
