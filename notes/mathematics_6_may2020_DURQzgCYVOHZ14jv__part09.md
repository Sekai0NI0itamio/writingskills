## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1:**

1. **Claim** — "number is prime or composite." → States the core decision the theorem addresses. **HAND-OFF:** This binary decision sets up *why* the theorem matters, prompting the next sentence to show a concrete consequence of that proof.

2. **Consequence** — "Because of the proof of this theorem, Euler was able to come up with his phi function…" → Shows a downstream mathematical development caused by the theorem's proof. **HAND-OFF:** The cause→effect chain ("Because of") opens the door for the next sentence to extend that causal logic to further improvements.

3. **Extension** — "Fermat's theorem has also led to new ideas and ways to improve the theorem itself, such as the AKS primality test…" → Introduces a second-order development that builds on the theorem. **HAND-OFF:** The example (AKS test) demands a sentence that explains what it actually does, so the reader is handed a functional description.

4. **Evidence/Function** — "This test allows us to determine whether any given number is prime or composite, just as Fermat's theorem." → Explains the AKS test's purpose and draws an explicit parallel to Fermat's theorem. **HAND-OFF:** The phrase "just as Fermat's theorem" signals that another similar test is coming, prompting the introduction of Rabin-Miller.

5. **Transition** — "A further extension to the theorem and the IA would be the investigation of the Rabin-Miller primality test." → Introduces yet another extension, advancing the sequence of developments. **HAND-OFF:** The word "further" pushes the reader to expect a comparison with what came before, which the next sentence delivers.

6. **Comparison** — "Like Fermat's little theorem, this test allows us to determine whether a number is prime." → Establishes the similarity between Fermat's theorem and Rabin-Miller. **HAND-OFF:** The contrastive word "Compared to" in the next sentence flips from similarity to difference, so the reader is primed for a reliability judgment.

7. **Mechanism/Implication** — "Compared to Fermat, this theorem relies more upon the unproven Riemann hypothesis instead of the modular arithmetic, thus making Fermat's theorem more reliable since the Riemann hypothesis has not been proven yet." → Explains *why* one theorem is more reliable than the other by contrasting their underlying assumptions. **HAND-OFF:** The reliability conclusion leads naturally to the final sentence, which isolates the one remaining weakness.

8. **Verdict/Limitation** — "The only limitation that Fermat's little theorem provides is that it proves to be valid only with prime numbers." → Delivers the sole stated constraint on the theorem. **HAND-OFF:** This closes the paragraph's logical arc from capability → extensions → comparison → limitation.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **States the decision the theorem resolves** (prime vs. composite) — establishes the theorem's core function so the reader has a baseline understanding before developments are introduced.
2. **Shows a downstream consequence of the theorem's proof** (Euler's totient function) — demonstrates that the theorem's value extends beyond its own statement, setting up the idea that it generates further work.
3. **Introduces a named improvement inspired by the theorem** (AKS primality test, with date and institution) — concretises the abstract claim that the theorem "led to new ideas," giving the reader a verifiable example.
4. **Explains what the improvement does and parallels it to the original** — ensures the reader understands the new tool's function and its relationship to Fermat's theorem before moving on.
5. **Proposes a further extension** (Rabin-Miller test) — advances the sequence one more step, showing the theorem continues to generate investigation.
6. **Compares the new extension to the original on a specific criterion** (reliability, based on proven vs. unproven assumptions) — shifts from listing developments to evaluating them, giving the reader a reasoned judgment.
7. **States the theorem's sole limitation** — closes the section by acknowledging a boundary, which prevents the section from appearing one-sided and gives the reader a complete picture.

**Why this order:** Each move builds on the previous one's conclusion — the core function leads to a consequence, the consequence leads to a named example, the example leads to a parallel tool, the parallel tool leads to a comparison, and the comparison leads to a stated limitation. A student replicating this with a different topic would follow: *what it does → what it caused → a named example of that cause → what the example does → another related development → a comparison on a specific axis → the remaining limitation.*

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing:**
- "also" — "Fermat's theorem has **also** led to new ideas" (adds a second development in the sequence)
- "further" — "A **further** extension to the theorem" (advances the sequence to one more item)
- "just as" — "determine whether any given number is prime or composite, **just as** Fermat's theorem" (parallels a new item to the original in the sequence)

**Cause/Consequence:**
- "Because of" — "**Because of** the proof of this theorem, Euler was able to come up with" (explicit causal link from proof to consequence)
- "thus making" — "instead of the modular arithmetic, **thus making** Fermat's theorem more reliable" (consequence of the contrasted mechanism)
- "since" — "more reliable **since** the Riemann hypothesis has not been proven yet" (reason-based consequence)

**Contrast/Concession:**
- "Compared to" — "**Compared to** Fermat, this theorem relies more upon" (explicit comparison frame)
- "instead of" — "relies more upon the unproven Riemann hypothesis **instead of** the modular arithmetic" (substitution showing what is replaced)
- "Like" — "**Like** Fermat's little theorem, this test allows us to" (concession of similarity before introducing difference)

**Specification:**
- "such as" — "led to new ideas and ways to improve the theorem itself, **such as** the AKS primality test" (narrows the category to a named instance)
- "only" — "The **only** limitation that Fermat's little theorem provides" (restricts the scope of the claim to one item)
- "only with" — "it proves to be valid **only with** prime numbers" (restricts the condition under which the theorem holds)

**Evidence handling:**
- "allows us to determine" — "This test **allows us to determine** whether any given number is prime or composite" (functional evidence of what a tool does)
- "has not been proven yet" — "since the Riemann hypothesis **has not been proven yet**" (status claim used as evidence for reliability judgment)

**Explanation verbs:**
- "was able to come up with" — "Euler **was able to come up with** his phi function" (describes the act of derivation)
- "defined as" / "where it becomes possible to" — "where it becomes possible to find the number of integers up to a given integer n that are relatively prime to n" (functional unpacking of what the totient function does)
- "relies more upon" — "this theorem **relies more upon** the unproven Riemann hypothesis" (identifies the foundational mechanism)

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern name:** *Theorem → Consequence → Named Extension → Functional Parallel → Comparative Evaluation → Stated Limitation*

This pattern moves from what a theorem does, through what it caused, to a comparison of related tools on a specific criterion, and closes with its one stated weakness.

**Step-by-step instructions to explain a NEW idea with the same pattern:**

1. **State the core decision or problem the theorem resolves** — open with a brief phrase that names the binary or fundamental question the theorem answers (e.g., "whether a triangle is right-angled").

2. **Name one downstream consequence of the theorem's proof** — identify a major mathematical development that followed directly from proving the theorem, and state who created it and what it enables.

3. **Introduce a named improvement inspired by the theorem** — pick a specific tool, test, or extension that the theorem motivated, and include its name, publication year, and origin institution if available.

4. **Explain what the improvement does and parallel it to the original** — describe the function of the new tool in a sentence that explicitly compares it to the original theorem using "just as" or "like."

5. **Propose a further extension and compare it on a specific criterion** — introduce one more related development, then contrast it with the original theorem on a concrete axis (e.g., what assumption it relies on), and state which one is more reliable and why.

6. **Close with the theorem's sole limitation** — identify the single condition under which the original theorem fails, using "the only limitation" to signal that the evaluation is complete and balanced.
