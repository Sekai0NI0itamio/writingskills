## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **evidence-evaluation/justification section** that uses one method, discards it, and switches to another. The ordered moves are:

1. **Goal statement** — name the hypothesis and what the evidence must show. (Sets the benchmark.)
2. **Present first method's output** — show the data the first tool produced. (Establishes a candidate answer.)
3. **Surface a concrete observation from that data** — give one specific comparison. (Makes the limitation visible.)
4. **Mechanistic explanation of limitation** — explain *why* the data is unreliable. (Justifies rejection.)
5. **Verdict on first method + pivot** — discard and announce the replacement tool. (Forces the reader onto new data.)
6. **Categorization rationale (missing categories)** — explain why a category doesn't exist. (Pre-empts objections.)
7. **Categorization rationale (placement)** — state where the key term was filed. (Defines the unit of analysis.)
8. **Decisive comparative data** — the new tool's numbers, framed as differences. (Delivers the evidence.)
9. **Verdict on hypothesis** — close the loop back to move 1. (Confirms success.)

Why this order works: you cannot reject method 1 without naming what it missed (moves 2–4); you cannot introduce method 2 without first killing method 1 (move 5); you cannot trust method 2's numbers without knowing how its categories were built (moves 6–7); and you cannot deliver a verdict without the numbers (moves 8–9). A student replicating this with a different topic keeps the *spine* of goal → tool → tool's flaw → better tool → better tool's categories → better tool's numbers → verdict, and only swaps the content.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — "Method-pivot paragraph"

**SKELETON:** *"To support [H] the data for [X] must show [place A] having greater attribution to [outcome]. The [tool 1] (figs. [a], [b]) display the [X] that participants said frequently however this does not [group/structure] the words into [categories/units]. '[term 1]' is larger in [A] than [B], and '[term 2]' seems to be the same in both. The limitation being it doesn't consider [synonymous phrasing/equivalent forms], only [exact/raw] words. This presentation of data does not accurately conclude, so [tool 2] (figs. [c], [d]) will be used."*

1. **Slot 1 (goal claim):** statement of what the evidence must demonstrate; full sentence starting "To support H…"
2. **Slot 2 (tool 1 + limitation conjunction):** names the first visualisation, the data source, and a "however" clause flagging its structural weakness.
3. **Slot 3 (raw observation):** a single comparative sentence reporting what tool 1 *can* show — usually one term higher, one term equal.
4. **Slot 4 (mechanistic cause):** a sentence beginning "The limitation being…" explaining the technical reason.
5. **Slot 5 (verdict + pivot):** "This presentation… does not accurately conclude, so [new tool] will be used."

**How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** Pick a hypothesis (H2) about, say, whether Group X shows more empathy than Group Y in interview transcripts. Slot 1: state that transcripts must show X using more empathy-coded language. Slot 2: introduce a frequency word-cloud, then "however" flag that it doesn't group words into themes. Slot 3: report that "supportive" appears more in X and "neutral" the same in both. Slot 4: explain that word-clouds ignore synonyms like "caring"/"kind". Slot 5: announce that a coded thematic table will replace the word-cloud.

**Original filled version:** *"To support H1 the data for adjectives must show JE having greater attribution to being green and clean. The wordles (figs. 22, 23) display the adjectives that questionnaire participants said frequently however this does not group the words into categories. 'Green' is larger in JE than OR, and 'Clean' seems to be the same in both. The limitation being it doesn't consider synonymous phrasing, only exact words. This presentation of data does not accurately conclude, so pie charts (figs. 24, 25) will be used."*

**Demonstration fill (different idea):** *"To support H2 the transcripts must show Group X attributing greater empathy than Group Y. The word-clouds (figs. 4, 5) display the feeling-words participants used, however they do not group synonyms into themes. 'Supportive' is larger in X than Y, and 'neutral' appears the same in both. The limitation being a word-cloud does not collapse synonyms such as 'caring' and 'kind' into one node. This presentation does not accurately conclude, so a coded thematic table (figs. 6, 7) will be used."*

### Skeleton B — "Category-justification mini-sequence"

**SKELETON:** *"A category for [absent term] is not seen as there was not enough frequency to constitute as a category, it is a part of [parent category]. '[key term]' is present in [housing category]."*

1. **Slot 1 (absent-category explanation):** one sentence explaining why a stand-alone category was dropped, framed as a frequency threshold; uses cause "as".
2. **Slot 2 (placement sentence):** one short sentence locating the key term inside an existing category.

**How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** Slot 1: pick a word that didn't appear enough times to stand alone, e.g. "warmth", and explain it now sits inside "comfort". Slot 2: locate "cosy" inside "comfort".

**Original filled version:** *"A category for 'clean' is not seen as there was not enough frequency to constitute as a category, it is a part of 'likeability'. 'green' is present in 'nature'."*

**Demonstration fill (different idea):** *"A category for 'warmth' is not seen as there was not enough frequency to constitute as a category, it is a part of 'comfort'. 'cosy' is present in 'comfort'."*

### Skeleton C — "Decisive-numbers verdict"

**SKELETON:** *"At [place A], [category 1] and [category 2] are greater by [x%] and [y%] than [place B], respectively. Therefore, [data source] support[s] [h]."*

1. **Slot 1 (comparative data):** one sentence using the construction "are greater by X% and Y% than [B], respectively"; matching order to the two named categories.
2. **Slot 2 (verdict):** one sentence beginning "Therefore" that names the data source and ties back to the hypothesis.

**How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** Slot 1: pick two categories (e.g. "safety" and "service") and two locations (e.g. "Hotel A" vs "Hotel B") and assign small percentage differences. Slot 2: name the survey instrument and restate the hypothesis letter.

**Original filled version:** *"At JE, 'likeability' and 'nature' are greater by 1% and 6% than OR, respectively. Therefore, adjective surveys from questionnaires support H1."*

**Demonstration fill (different idea):** *"At Hotel A, 'safety' and 'service' are greater by 3% and 9% than Hotel B, respectively. Therefore, guest-review surveys from TripAdvisor support H3."*

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / method transition**
- *"so pie charts (figs. 24, 25) will be used"* — announces the switch to the replacement tool after rejecting the old one.

**Contrast / concession**
- *"however this does not group the words into categories"* — concedes the visible strength of tool 1 while flagging its structural gap.

**Cause / consequence**
- *"The limitation being it doesn't consider synonymous phrasing"* — names the cause of the failed analysis.
- *"there was not enough frequency to constitute as a category"* — gives the cause for a missing category.
- *"so pie charts … will be used"* — consequence clause tied to the preceding verdict.

**Specification / placement**
- *"it is a part of 'likeability'"* — specifies where a dropped term lives.
- *"'green' is present in 'nature'"* — specifies category membership of the key term.

**Verdict connectives**
- *"Therefore, adjective surveys from questionnaires support H1"* — closes the loop with the verdict.
- *"This presentation of data does not accurately conclude"* — pre-verdict dismissal that licences the pivot.

**Evidence-handling verbs**
- *"display the adjectives that questionnaire participants said frequently"* — introduces raw data.
- *"'Green' is larger in JE than OR"* — comparative evidence reporting.
- *"seems to be the same in both"* — hedges an equality finding.

**Explanation / definition verbs**
- *"is not seen as"* — flags absence of a category.
- *"is present in"* — places a term in a category.

**Quantification pattern**
- *"greater by 1% and 6% than OR, respectively"* — paired-difference reporting that pairs directly with the verdict in the next sentence.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section relies on a **"Tool-A → Tool-A-fails → Tool-B → Tool-B-categories → Tool-B-verdict"** pattern. To explain a *new* idea with the same pattern, follow these steps:

1. **Open with the goal sentence.** State the hypothesis and write down what the evidence must show for it to be supported (e.g. "To support H2 the data must show X attributing more Y than Z").
2. **Introduce the first tool with a single sentence.** Name the tool, say where the data comes from, and immediately append a "however" clause flagging its structural weakness (it groups by frequency but not by category, or it lists items but not themes, etc.).
3. **Give one concrete observation from that tool.** A short comparative sentence showing one item higher, one item equal — the minimum to expose the limitation.
4. **Name the mechanism behind the failure.** A sentence beginning "The limitation being…" that explains the technical reason the tool cannot answer the hypothesis.
5. **Deliver the verdict on the tool and pivot.** "This presentation of data does not accurately conclude, so [new tool] will be used."
6. **Justify any missing categories in the new tool.** One sentence explaining why a stand-alone category was dropped (frequency threshold) and where it was rolled into.
7. **State where the key term lives.** One short placement sentence locating the central term inside an existing category.
8. **Give the decisive comparative numbers.** One sentence using the construction "[categories] are greater by [x%] and [y%] than [comparison], respectively."
9. **Close with the verdict.** A "Therefore" sentence that names the data source and restates the hypothesis letter, signalling that the goal sentence from step 1 has now been met.

The rhythm is *benchmark → first attempt → first attempt's flaw → second attempt → second attempt's definitions → second attempt's numbers → conclusion*, and it works because the reader is never asked to trust a number before the tool that produced it has been justified.
