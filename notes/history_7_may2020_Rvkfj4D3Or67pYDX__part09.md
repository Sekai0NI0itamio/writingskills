## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **single-paragraph analytical move** whose job is to **adjudicate between two competing explanations** (economic vs military) by weighing evidence. The ordered sequence is:

1. **Specific data point** — sets up the empirical ground for the verdict.
2. **Verdict contrasting the two hypotheses** — announces which interpretation wins.
3. **Academic authority backing the winning verdict** — generalises the verdict into a structural motive.
4. **Institutional counter-evidence** — steelmans the losing interpretation so the rebuttal looks fair.
5. **Source critique of the counter-evidence** — disqualifies the counter-authority by exposing vested bias.
6. **Macro-level causal mechanism** — re-seals the verdict with a structural explanation.

**Why that order:** each move only makes sense once the previous one has run. You cannot deliver a verdict (2) without the data (1); you cannot credit an authority (3) without first stating the claim it supports (2); you cannot refute a source (5) without first quoting it (4); and you cannot re-affirm the verdict after the rebuttal without supplying the deeper cause (6). The sequence replicates the classic IB argument-rebuttal choreography: *evidence → claim → expert → counter-claim → discredit counter-claim → structural cause.*

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Verdict / conclusion marker:** *"It is therefore more likely that…"* — converts prior data into a judgment that chooses one interpretation.
- **Contrast (binary narrowing):** *"…, rather than military, means."* — explicitly pits two hypotheses against each other.
- **Authority introduction:** *"Kinzer argues that…"* / *"The Senate Foreign Relations Committee stated that…"* — verb of attribution ("argues," "stated") framed around a named source.
- **Quoted evidence handling:** *"…had the ulterior motive of securing 'markets, resources, and investment potential…'"* / *"'ships of war are the necessary complement…'"* — short italicised quotes sandwiched into the syntax.
- **Pivot / counter:** *"However, the origin of this quote betrays its limitation;"* — signals source-critique and disqualifies the counter-evidence.
- **Causation (structural):** *"[A] highly productive manufacturing sector left America searching for markets…"* — verb "left" frames a sector as a causal agent acting on policy.
- **Specification / concession:** *"violent or otherwise"* — concedes ambiguity inside a single sentence without abandoning the argument.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**The pattern is: evidence → verdict → expert amplification → steelmanned counter-evidence → source discredit → structural mechanism.** It is a *rebuttal-through-source-criticism* pattern, not a straight exposition.

**Steps to replicate with a new idea:**

1. **Open with a quantified micro-fact.** Pick one dated/numbered piece of evidence (a shipment, a budget line, a troop count) and leave it as a fragment that implies the conclusion.
2. **State the verdict as a binary contrast.** Use the formula "It is therefore more likely that [winning hypothesis], rather than [losing hypothesis]." Commit; do not hedge.
3. **Generalise the verdict with a named expert.** Introduce a historian/scholar ("X argues that…") who elevates your specific case into a structural motive. Quote them on a tripartite benefit list (markets/resources/influence, or an equivalent triple).
4. **Steelman the opposing view with an institutional quote.** Find an official committee, senate, or ministry whose language *seems* to support the losing hypothesis. Quote a paired formulation: a general principle ("X is the necessary complement to Y") plus its local application ("[Place] is indispensable as…").
5. **Discredit that institutional source by exposing its author's bias.** Pivot with "However, the origin of this quote betrays its limitation;" then name the chair/drafter and link them, via "who stood to politically gain," to a personal or constituency interest that overlaps with the policy.
6. **Re-seal the verdict with a structural cause.** Invoke a macro-economic or demographic pressure using "X left Y doing Z" so the reader sees that the verdict survives even when the rebutted source is removed.
