## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

The bibliography is presented as a flat bulleted list of twelve entries, not as discursive paragraphs. To trace the LOGIC PATH I group adjacent entries that share a source-type identity; each entry acts as one sentence-level move in the list.

**Paragraph 1 — Primary domestic legislation (entry 1)**

- Move 1 — *anchor in a concrete statute*. Quote: "Assembleia Legislativa do Estado de São Paulo, Lei 10948". Job: opens the bibliography on the most authoritative type of source — a law. Hand-off: because the list begins with a law, the next entry must show how that legal frame is *interpreted* in the public sphere, i.e. domestic news on legal events.

**Paragraph 2 — Domestic news on legal and political events (entries 2–4)**

- Move 2 — *news evidence of a Supreme Court ruling*. Quote: "STF Conclui Julgamento e Enquadra Homofobia". Job: evidences how the courts have acted on the topic. Hand-off: because the judicial branch has just been named, the next entry should pivot to the *executive actor* whose rhetoric provoked that ruling.

- Move 3 — *news evidence on a political figure*. Quote: "Relembre as Polêmicas Da Ministra Damares Alves". Job: shifts from the STF to the executive branch. Hand-off: because a minister who has spoken on the issue has been named, the next entry must move to a *social consequence* of that rhetoric — workplace exclusion.

- Move 4 — *news evidence on social impact*. Quote: "Transexuais São Excluídos Do Mercado De Trabalho". Job: shows the lived consequence on a vulnerable group. Hand-off: because social exclusion has been stated, the next entry must provide a *counter-voice* — an embodied or artistic response.

**Paragraph 3 — Activist response and statistical evidence (entries 5–7)**

- Move 5 — *activist/scholarly artefact*. Quote: "Corpo Livre. Body and Art as Means of Activism". Job: gives a corporeal counter-source to the social exclusion just cited. Hand-off: because an embodied response has been offered, the next entry should move to *quantification* of the underlying prejudice.

- Move 6 — *statistical news source*. Quote: "Um a Cada Quatro Brasileiros é Homofóbico". Job: quantifies the prevalence of homophobia. Hand-off: because prevalence has been measured, the next source must escalate to the *most extreme empirical consequence* — mortality.

- Move 7 — *report on deaths*. Quote: "População LGBT Morta No Brasil". Job: provides the empirical record of fatality. Hand-off: because the data peak (death) has been hit, the next entry must pull back to a *synthesising overview* that contextualises the numbers.

**Paragraph 4 — Overview news and theoretical framing (entries 8–10)**

- Move 8 — *comprehensive news overview*. Quote: "LGBTfobia No Brasil: Fatos, Números e Polêmicas". Job: returns the reader to a synthesising article on the same dataset. Hand-off: because the empirical picture has been laid out, the next entry should introduce the *conceptual lens* needed to interpret it.

- Move 9 — *conceptual authority*. Quote: "State Monopoly on Violence". Job: introduces Max Weber's framework. Hand-off: because the theoretical lens is set, the next entry must *apply* it to a concrete Brazilian ruling.

- Move 10 — *news applying the frame*. Quote: "STF Permmite Criminalização Da Homofobia e Da Transfobia". Job: applies the monopoly-of-violence concept to a real court decision. Hand-off: because a single-state ruling has been theorised, the next entry must *universalise* the role of the state.

**Paragraph 5 — International framing (entries 11–12)**

- Move 11 — *international institutional source*. Quote: "The Role of States". Job: broadens the lens from Brazil alone to states-as-actors in human rights. Hand-off: because the role of states has been generalised, the bibliography must close on the *highest legal authority* on the topic.

- Move 12 — *foundational international document*. Quote: "Universal Declaration of Human Rights". Job: anchors the whole essay in the universal legal bedrock. Closing move: the list ends on the most general, most authoritative source — the conventional signal that the argument has been universalised.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

The ordered moves a bibliography makes in this IB context are:

1. **Primary legislation first** — establishes the legal frame the essay engages with.
2. **Domestic news on legal/political events** — shows how the law is interpreted, enforced, contested.
3. **Activist and statistical sources** — moves from legal/political to social/embodied reality.
4. **Theoretical concept + applied news** — provides a conceptual lens, then re-applies it to a concrete case.
5. **International institutional + foundational legal sources** — universalises the argument at the close.

The order runs **specific domestic statute → public/political discourse → social reality → conceptual theory → universal legal authority**. This is a *funnel-up* sequence: the list opens with one statute and closes on universal human rights. Each move sets up the next because each one answers the implicit question left by the previous one — "what does this law say?" → "how is it being applied?" → "what is its social effect?" → "how do we name that effect conceptually?" → "what higher authority confirms the conceptual claim?" A student replicating this with a different topic should keep the same escalation — local law first, global law last — so the bibliography visibly widens in scope.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — Primary legal source**

"[Legislative body], [Document type] [Number], [Long-form date], available at: [official URL], Accessed on [Long-form date]."

1. *What each slot holds:* Slot 1 = name of legislature; Slot 2 = type (Lei / Law / Act) + identifying number; Slot 3 = "5th of November 2001" form; Slot 4 = full repository link; Slot 5 = access date.
2. *How to fill with a different idea:* pick a statute that created or amended the phenomenon you are investigating; name the body that passed it, its official number, the precise day of promulgation, paste the official government URL, and record when you last opened it.
3. *Original filled version:* "Assembleia Legislativa do Estado de São Paulo, Lei 10948, 5th of November 2001, available at: https://www.al.sp.gov.br/...html, Accessed on August 31st, 2019."
4. *Demo fill (different idea — climate protest law):* "Deutscher Bundestag, Strafgesetzbuch § 125, 1st of January 1872, available at: https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__125.html, Accessed on 12th March 2022."

**SKELETON B — News article**

"[Author surname, given name]. '[Headline stating the topic].' [Outlet], [Parent company], [Long-form date], [URL]. Accessed on [Long-form date]."

1. *What each slot holds:* Slot 1 = surname-comma-given; Slot 2 = headline as a noun phrase + verb of action (enquadra, permite, exclui…); Slot 3 = outlet + parent media group; Slot 4 = "13 June 2019" form; Slot 5 = full URL; Slot 6 = access date.
2. *How to fill:* pick a news article that documents a discrete event relevant to your essay; the headline should already name who did what — that headline-as-claim is the source's contribution; record the parent company to signal reliability.
3. *Original:* "Coletta, Ricardo Della. 'STF Conclui Julgamento e Enquadra Homofobia Na Lei Dos Crimes De Racismo.' Folha De S.Paulo, Folha De S.Paulo, 13 June 2019… Accessed on November 19th, 2019."
4. *Demo fill:* "Bell, Sarah. 'UK Court Rules Climate Protests Law Unlawful.' The Guardian, Guardian Media Group, 18 July 2022, www.theguardian.com/uk-court-climate. Accessed on 20 July 2022."

**SKELETON C — Data / report source**

"[Lead author], et al. [Descriptive noun-phrase title]. [Year], pp. [range], [Publisher]."

1. *What each slot holds:* Slot 1 = lead author + "et al." if co-authored; Slot 2 = noun phrase naming the population measured; Slot 3 = year; Slot 4 = page range consulted; Slot 5 = publisher.
2. *How to fill:* identify a report that quantifies the harm you are investigating; use a noun phrase in the title so the reader sees the population at a glance; cite the page range of the part you actually read.
3. *Original:* "Michels, Eduardo, et al. População LGBT Morta No Brasil. 2018, pp. 1–25, População LGBT Morta No Brasil."
4. *Demo fill:* "Park, Jisoo, et al. Climate Refugees in South Asia. 2021, pp. 4–30, Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre."

**SKELETON D — International authoritative document**

"[International body], [Full formal title], [Long-form date], [Resolution / reference code], available at: [official URL] [accessed Long-form date]."

1. *What each slot holds:* Slot 1 = UN body / treaty body; Slot 2 = full formal title; Slot 3 = adoption date; Slot 4 = reference number (A/RES, 217 A (III)); Slot 5 = repository URL; Slot 6 = access date in square brackets.
2. *How to fill:* close the bibliography with the most universal instrument relevant to your topic; preserve the full UN-style reference code so examiners see the institutional pedigree; use the square-bracket access convention to mark it as a web-cited foundational text.
3. *Original:* "UN General Assembly, Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 10 December 1948, 217 A (III), available at: https://www.refworld.org/docid/3ae6b3712c.html [accessed 29th January 2020]."
4. *Demo fill:* "UN General Assembly, Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, 13 December 2006, 61/106, available at: https://www.refworld.org/docid/45f973632.html [accessed 5th February 2023]."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

A bibliography does not use connectives in the way a discursive paragraph does. Its "vocabulary" is the set of citation gestures that move the reader through the list.

- **Source-type specification:** "Lei 10948", "Universal Declaration of Human Rights" — proper-noun titles that locate each source in a hierarchy of authority (statute → treaty).
- **Authority signalling:** "Universidade De São Paulo", "Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.", "UN General Assembly" — names of institutions attached to the publication venue to mark reliability.
- **Temporal precision:** "5th of November 2001", "Accessed on March 1st, 2020", "accessed 29th January 2020" — long-form ordinal dates rather than numerics, signalling rigour.
- **Title-as-claim (evidence handling):** "STF Conclui Julgamento e Enquadra Homofobia…" — the headline itself encodes the source's argument; the title does the work a "this suggests" would do in prose.
- **Repository marker:** "available at:", "Accessed on", "[accessed" — fixed citation verbs that signal the *use* of the source rather than any commentary on it.
- **Sequencing:** implicit — the list itself is the connective; ordering from primary law to international declaration is the only "firstly/finally" the format allows.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this bibliography relies on is **hierarchy-by-formatting + escalation-by-position**. Each entry is identical in *shape* (author. title. venue, date, URL. access date) but different in *kind* of source, and the position of each entry in the list signals its conceptual role.

To replicate the pattern for any new topic:

1. **Choose the source-type hierarchy** you will need: primary law → domestic news → activist/data → theoretical concept → international legal document. Decide the order so it escalates in authority and scope.
2. **Open with primary legislation.** Write the legislature, the document type + number, and the long-form promulgation date. Add the official repository URL and the access date. This sets the legal frame.
3. **Add domestic news in event-order.** For each event in your topic (court ruling, ministerial statement, social consequence), find one news article whose headline is a noun-phrase claim. Use the same author-headline-venue-date-URL-accessed template for each.
4. **Insert the activist/data pair next.** Move from news to a creative/embodied source, then to a statistical source, then to a fatality/extreme-consequence report. Keep the template identical.
5. **Insert the theoretical concept and a re-application of it.** Pair an encyclopaedia entry on a key concept (e.g. "State Monopoly on Violence") with a news article that applies that concept. This shows methodological self-awareness.
6. **Close with the international source pair.** An institutional page on the general role of the relevant actor (states, corporations, media), followed by a UN-style foundational document with reference code and square-bracket access date.
7. **Lock the formatting across all twelve entries.** Identical punctuation, identical date form, identical "Accessed on" phrase — the visual uniformity is what tells the examiner the student has a *system*, not a list.

The replication rule: *identical formatting, escalating scope*. Every entry looks the same so the reader can see only the *kind* of source changing; every move up the list widens the lens, so the bibliography visibly grounds the essay in concrete Brazilian law and lifts it onto universal human-rights terrain.
