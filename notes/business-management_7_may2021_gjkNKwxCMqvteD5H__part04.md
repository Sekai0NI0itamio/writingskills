## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This section follows a **"parallels + pay-off calculation"** logic — a numeric reasoning pattern.

1. **Industry parallel** (1st): establish legitimacy of the move by citing what a comparable firm did. *Why first:* it gives the reader an external benchmark before numbers are introduced, anchoring scale.
2. **CAPEX/VPX contrast** (2nd): quickly contrast the upfront cost against a small per-unit figure. *Why next:* it primes the reader that the big number is not the whole story.
3. **Break-even volume for option A** (3rd): state the threshold number first. *Why next:* sets up the "but after break-even…" payoff.
4. **Post-break-even payoff for option A** (4th): show the profitability rate via formula. *Why next:* delivers the incentive.
5. **Derived target for option A** (5th): formula yielding a second number. *Why next:* closes the case for option A by quantifying a milestone.
6. **Break-even for option B** (6th): lower threshold number. *Why next:* opens the comparison.
7. **Post-break-even profit for option B** (7th): per-chip profit margin. *Why next:* supplies the metric that will be compared.
8. **Comparison verdict** (8th): declare the better option in the long term. *Why next:* resolves which option wins.
9. **Real-world volume anchor** (9th): cite actual sales to convert the abstract metric into reality. *Why next:* bridges from per-chip math to a company-level payoff.
11. **Payback period formula** (10th): final numeric verdict. *Why last:* it is the headline answer to the whole "is it worth it?" question.

The sequence is **benchmark → cost anatomy → answer-first calc → post-threshold upside → comparison verdict → real-world conversion → final payback**.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / source-flagging**
- "From figure three, we can see" — flags evidence and announces a numeric claim.
- "From figure three, we can see" — reappears to set up each break-even figure.
- "Figure four shows" — short, declarative evidence introduction.

**Cause / consequence**
- "we will be able to calculate the payback period" — names a downstream consequence of an assumption.
- "would take them less than 817,000,000 ÷ … = 6,515,152" — delivers a derived consequence directly as a formula.

**Contrast / concession**
- "however" — "The variable cost, however, in comparison to the fixed cost, is much cheaper…" → flips from big CAPEX to small per-unit figure.
- "But once Huawei breaks even, they will have…" — twice used to pivot from the break-even hurdle to the post-threshold upside.
- "in comparison to" — explicit comparator for the contrast.

**Specification / precision**
- "much cheaper at an estimated $8 per chip" — narrows the contrast to a number.
- "which is $100 more than purchasing from supplier" — adds a delta so the comparison is exact.
- "an estimated $100" — soft qualifier on the assumption, useful for honest IB-style reasoning.

**Evidence handling**
- "According to Forbes.com" — names authority source before a real-world figure.
- "(Takahashi)" / "(Sin)" — inline parenthetical citations signalling source.
- "From figure three, we can see" — uses the student's own diagram as evidence.

**Explanation / definition verbs**
- None in the strict definitional sense; instead, **calculation verbs**: "must do the same," "we can see," "they will be earning," "makes it the better option." The section explains via *arithmetic verbs* (multiply, divide, equal) rather than definitional verbs.
- "estimated to be" — soft framing verb for assumptions.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **"benchmark → answer-first calculation → post-threshold payoff → comparison verdict → real-world conversion → payback formula"** pattern. To replicate with a new idea:

1. **Name one comparable firm that already made the move.** Quote their investment figure and the capability they achieved. This legitimises the move before any maths appears.
2. **Issue the imperative parallel.** "Subject must do the same" — one short sentence, no elaboration.
3. **Contrast fixed vs. variable cost with "however."** Give one small per-unit dollar figure so the reader senses the per-unit economics are not catastrophic.
4. **State the break-even volume first.** Open with "From figure X, we can see that [Subject] must sell [N units] to break-even." Lead with the answer, not the setup.
5. **Pivot with "But once… break even…"** and embed a return-rate formula (large numerator ÷ small denominator = huge %). The point is to show explosive post-threshold upside.
6. **Add a second formula yielding a milestone volume** (e.g. "would take them less than [formula] = [integer]"). This closes the first option's case.
7. **Open the alternative option with a *lower* break-even number.** "Figure Y shows that [Subject] needs to sell [smaller N]…" The lower number primes the comparison.
8. **Give its per-unit profit and an explicit delta** vs. the supplier option ("which is $X more than purchasing from supplier").
9. **Close the comparison with "This significant increase… makes it the better option in the long term."** A decisive verdict sentence is mandatory.
10. **Anchor in real-world volume.** "According to [source], [Subject] had [actual figure] in [year]." This converts the per-unit math into a company-scale claim.
11. **State the assumption as a conditional.** "If we estimate… we will be able to calculate the payback period."
12. **Isolate the final formula as its own line.** "[CAPEX] ÷ [volume × per-unit] = [N] years." Do not add commentary afterwards; the number is the conclusion.

The pattern is **external legitimacy → CAPEX/VPX anatomy → numeric proof of upside → compare alternatives → convert to payback years**, with every transition either answering the previous line or specifying it more precisely.
