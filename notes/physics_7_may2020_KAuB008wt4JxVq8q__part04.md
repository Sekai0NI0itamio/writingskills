## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**

1. **Sentence:** "was rising." — **Move:** *Continuation of prior trend claim*. It hands the reader to the next sentence because a rising trend alone is not yet interpreted; the reader now needs the author to evaluate *what kind* of trend it is.

2. **Sentence:** "This does not mean that the trend was proportional, since there is a straight line, but the line does not pass through the origin, a requirement for proportionality." — **Move:** *Refutation with embedded criterion* — denies a possible interpretation ("does not mean... proportional") and supplies the rule that decides it ("a requirement for proportionality"). It hands the reader to the next sentence by *inviting a check*: if the rule is "must pass through origin," the reader now needs the *actual* intercept.

3. **Sentence:** "The equation for the trendline was 0.55x + 0.31 with an error on slope of +/- 0.06 and an error on the y-intercept of +/- 0.02." — **Move:** *Evidence — the specific equation with uncertainty.* It hands the reader to the next sentence because the constant term (0.31) and its error (+/- 0.02) *answer* the prior criterion and simultaneously leave the slope (0.55) waiting to be converted into a physical quantity.

4. **Sentence:** "This slope led to a value of 1.8 +/- 0.1." — **Move:** *Mechanism / derivation step* — turns the mathematical slope into a physically meaningful parameter (the exponent n). It hands the reader to the next sentence because a derived quantity demands an *overall uncertainty* before it can be compared to theory.

5. **Sentence:** "The overall error is 8.35%." — **Move:** *Single-statistic uncertainty summary.* It hands the reader to the next sentence by *finishing the numeric package*: with value (1.8) and error (8.35%) in hand, the only thing left is to *compare to what was predicted*.

6. **Sentence:** "In my original hypothesis, I predicted that there would be a cubed relationship, or that n = 3 because dipoles should have a cubed relationship, but I got something closer to n = 2, meaning a squared relationship, closer to what was expected for monopoles." — **Move:** *Comparison — hypothesis vs. result, grounded in physical theory.* Two parallel "because… should have" clauses supply the theory on each side. The sentence terminates the logical chain by *verdict*: the result sits between two competing physical models.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **results-interpretation / discussion** section. The ordered content moves are:

1. **State the qualitative trend** (what the data was doing) — so the reader knows what is being judged.
2. **Test the simplest mathematical form first** (proportionality) using its defining criterion (origin passage) — so the strongest claim is disposed of before nuance.
3. **Quote the fitted equation with uncertainties** — so the reader can independently verify the prior test and the next derivation.
4. **Convert the slope into a physical quantity** (here, the power-law exponent n) — so the abstract number gains physical meaning.
5. **Aggregate the error into one percentage** — so the result is comparable to theory as a single value.
6. **Compare against hypothesis, then against theory**, naming an alternative model that the result resembles — so the result is *positioned* on the theoretical landscape rather than merely labelled "wrong."

The order works because each move supplies exactly what the next requires: the trend supplies nothing interpretable without a rule; the rule demands the equation; the equation yields a physical parameter; the parameter needs an aggregated error; and only then can the value be matched against a prediction and a competing model.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — "Proportionality Test by Origin Criterion"**

> Trend statement. **This does/does not mean that the trend was proportional, since [shape criterion], but [counter-criterion], a requirement for proportionality.** The equation for the trendline was [a]x + [b] with an error on slope of +/- [s] and an error on the y-intercept of +/- [i].

1. *Slots:*
   - Slot 1: the observed behaviour (verb-ing clause or adjectival phrase).
   - Slot 2: a yes/no verdict on proportionality.
   - Slot 3: the geometric/algebraic criterion that defines it (straight-line, linear, monotonic…).
   - Slot 4: the property that fails the criterion (passes through origin, slope equals one…).
   - Slot 5: trendline equation in *y = mx + c* form with two errors.
2. *How to fill with a different idea:* Pick a measured quantity whose proportionality you want to test. State the observed direction of change. Decide whether your data *should* have been proportional. Identify the one defining property of proportionality (zero intercept, slope = 1, ratio constant). State explicitly which property your data violates. Then give the actual fitted line with the two uncertainties on slope and intercept — the intercept value must numerically demonstrate the violation.
3. *Original filled version:* "This does not mean that the trend was proportional, since there is a straight line, but the line does not pass through the origin, a requirement for proportionality. The equation for the trendline was 0.55x + 0.31 with an error on slope of +/- 0.06 and an error on the y-intercept of +/- 0.02."
4. *Demonstration fill:* "This does not mean that the current was proportional to the applied voltage, since the graph was linear, but the gradient was not equal to one over the resistance, a requirement for Ohm's law in this configuration. The equation for the trendline was 1.42x + 0.08 with an error on slope of +/- 0.03 and an error on the y-intercept of +/- 0.01."

**SKELETON B — "Derive a Physical Quantity, Then Aggregate the Error"**

> **This slope led to a value of [n] +/- [e].** The overall error is **[X]%.**

1. *Slots:*
   - Slot 1: a forward-pointing demonstrative ("this slope / this gradient / this gradient-to-length ratio…").
   - Slot 2: the mathematical quantity just calculated.
   - Slot 3: the absolute uncertainty on that quantity.
   - Slot 4: a single percentage summarising the *total* propagated uncertainty across the experiment.
2. *How to fill with a different idea:* Take the numerical slope you just reported. Name the derived physical quantity it represents (an index of refraction, a spring constant, a power-law exponent). Give that quantity with its propagated uncertainty. Then collapse the whole experiment's error budget into one percentage, written as "The overall error is X%."
3. *Original filled version:* "This slope led to a value of 1.8 +/- 0.1. The overall error is 8.35%."
4. *Demonstration fill:* "This gradient led to a value of 0.0042 +/- 0.0001 mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹. The overall error is 4.7%."

**SKELETON C — "Hypothesis vs. Result, Bracketed by Two Competing Models"**

> **In my original hypothesis, I predicted [prediction A] because [theory A] should have [prediction A], but I got something closer to [prediction B], meaning [interpretation of B], closer to what was expected for [theory B].**

1. *Slots:*
   - Slot 1: a backward-pointing phrase ("In my original hypothesis…").
   - Slot 2: the predicted relationship or value, restated as a number or named form.
   - Slot 3: the theoretical family supporting that prediction.
   - Slot 4: the experimental outcome, phrased as "closer to…"
   - Slot 5: a re-statement of the outcome in plain physical language.
   - Slot 6: a competing theoretical family that the result instead resembles.
2. *How to fill with a different idea:* Look back at your hypothesis paragraph and lift the specific prediction. Identify which physical theory supported it. Identify which *other* physical theory would have predicted a different value close to what you actually got. Build the sentence with "because" linking prediction to theory A and "closer to what was expected for" linking result to theory B.
3. *Original filled version:* "In my original hypothesis, I predicted that there would be a cubed relationship, or that n = 3 because dipoles should have a cubed relationship, but I got something closer to n = 2, meaning a squared relationship, closer to what was expected for monopoles."
4. *Demonstration fill:* "In my original hypothesis, I predicted that the period would be independent of amplitude, or that T ≈ constant because small-angle SHM should be isochronous, but I got something closer to a slight positive dependence, meaning the restoring force was not perfectly linear, closer to what was expected for a real pendulum near 30°."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing:** "In my original hypothesis, I predicted…" (frames what came before as prior-in-time)
- **Cause / consequence:** "This slope led to a value of…" (treats the slope as the *cause* of the derived n); "because dipoles should have a cubed relationship" (causal grounding of prediction)
- **Contrast / concession:** "but the line does not pass through the origin" (refutes an implication); "but I got something closer to n = 2" (refutes a prediction)
- **Specification / restatement:** "or that n = 3" (gives the predicted number); "meaning a squared relationship" (translates the number into words)
- **Evidence handling:** direct quotation of the trendline equation with errors "The equation for the trendline was 0.55x + 0.31 with an error on slope of +/- 0.06 and an error on the y-intercept of +/- 0.02"; single-statistic summary "The overall error is 8.35%"
- **Explanation verbs:** "a requirement for proportionality" (defines the test); "This slope led to" (causal conversion); "closer to what was expected for" (theory-mapping verb)

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **trend → rule-based rejection → equation → derived quantity → aggregated error → two-model comparison.**

To explain a *new* idea with the same pattern:

1. **Open with a one-clause statement of what the data was doing** (verb-ing or adjectival — no interpretation yet).
2. **State the simplest mathematical form and reject it via its defining criterion** (proportionality ↔ origin passage; linearity ↔ constant gradient; Ohm's law ↔ V = IR exactly; etc.). Use "This does/does not mean… since [criterion], but [counter-criterion], a requirement for [form]."
3. **Quote the fitted equation with both uncertainties** ("The equation for the trendline was [a]x + [b] with an error on slope of +/- [s] and an error on the y-intercept of +/- [i].") — this must show the criterion check numerically.
4. **Convert the slope into a physical quantity** with its propagated uncertainty ("This slope led to a value of [n] +/- [e].") — name what the number *means* in the next slot.
5. **Aggregate the experiment's error into one percentage** ("The overall error is X%.") so the reader has a single comparison handle.
6. **Compare to the hypothesis, then map the result onto an alternative physical model** using "In my original hypothesis, I predicted [A] because [theory A] should have [A], but I got something closer to [B], meaning [plain-language restatement], closer to what was expected for [theory B]." — always supply *both* theories so the result is positioned, not merely labelled wrong.
