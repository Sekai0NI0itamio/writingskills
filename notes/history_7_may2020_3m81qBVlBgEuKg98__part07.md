## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **causal-reframing / source-critique move** typical of historiographical analysis. The sequence is:

1. **Surface-cause hypothesis first** (the film prompted the Klan's moralistic focus). This is necessary because the student must acknowledge the intuitive reading before overturning it.
2. **Evidence for the surface cause** (specific film sequences + audience reaction). Without this, the rebuttal reads as ungenerous.
3. **Contrast pivot** ("However") that signals the writer is moving from the weaker to the stronger explanation.
4. **Stronger structural cause** (pre-existing concerns among the target audience). This is the analytical payload.
5. **Reframing as supply-to-demand** ("to whom... attempted to cater"), which neutralises the original film-as-cause claim by showing both cultural products were *responding to* the same audience.

The ordering matters: you cannot rebut a cause you have not first stated and supported. The reader is carried from the obvious reading into the more sophisticated one only because the obvious reading is shown to be grounded.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — "Conceded cause → stronger structural cause"

**SKELETON:** "[Group]'s focus on [issue] may also have been prompted in part by [stimulus] — its [specific feature] were met with [response] from [agent]. However, it is more likely that these [issues] were already prevalent amongst [target audience], to whom [stimulus] (and [group]) attempted to cater."

1. **Slot 1 — Claim of partial causation** (noun phrase + "may also have been prompted in part by"): names the institution/group, its behaviour, and proposes a stimulus. Grammatical shape: subject + possessive + noun + modal passive + "by"-phrase.
2. **Slot 2 — Em-dash mechanism** (possessive + content noun + "were met with" + reception noun + "from" + agent): shows *how* the stimulus plausibly caused the behaviour. Shape: noun clause of evidence linked by em-dash.
3. **Slot 3 — Contrast pivot** ("However, it is more likely that…"): concedes Slot 1 and reframes. Shape: single concessive adverb + comparative modal.
4. **Slot 4 — Stronger cause + audience** (passive "were already prevalent amongst" + audience noun + relative clause "to whom… attempted to cater"). Shape: stative passive + prepositional phrase + purpose-tinged relative clause.

**How to fill with a different idea:** Pick any pair of cultural products/groups and the social concern they are accused of amplifying (e.g. tabloid press and anti-immigration sentiment; evangelical radio and moral-panic topics; a video game and youth aggression). The skeleton works whenever one surface agent (the text/product) is being distinguished from the underlying social demand both it and a movement serve.

**Original filled version (verbatim):** "The Klan's focus on moralistic issues may also have been prompted in part by the film — its sequences concerning the purity of women and moral corruption were met with particularly emotive responses from audiences. However, it is more likely that these social concerns were already prevalent amongst white Protestants, to whom the film (and the Klan) attempted to cater."

**Demonstration fill (different idea):** "The tabloid press's fixation on immigration may also have been prompted in part by government rhetoric — its headlines framing asylum seekers as 'floods' were met with sharp readership spikes during election cycles. However, it is more likely that these anxieties were already prevalent amongst working-class voters, to whom the press (and political parties) attempted to cater."

### Skeleton B — "Hedged compound cause with parenthetical alignment"

**SKELETON:** "[Group A] (and [Group B]) attempted to cater to [audience], amongst whom [concern] was already prevalent."

1. **Slot 1 — Coordinated agents** ("[A] (and [B])"). Shape: parenthetical coordination that visually equates two usually-distinct actors.
2. **Slot 2 — Catering verb** ("attempted to cater to"). Shape: volitional past + "to"-infinitive signalling supply-side responsiveness.
3. **Slot 3 — Audience + prevalence clause** ("amongst whom [concern] was already prevalent"). Shape: relative "whom" + stative past ("was already prevalent") that locks the concern into the past — the audience *already* held it before the stimulus arrived.

This mini-skeleton captures the final reframing sentence specifically and can be reused whenever two cultural products need to be re-presented as twin responses to one demand.

**Original filled version:** "to whom the film (and the Klan) attempted to cater."
**Demonstration fill:** "to whom the printing press (and pamphleteers) attempted to cater, amongst whom Protestant grievance was already prevalent in the 1630s."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Hedging / probability ranking**
- *"may also have been prompted in part by"* — Modal + partial-causation marker, used to float a cause without committing: "The Klan's focus on moralistic issues may also have been prompted in part by the film."

**Cause / mechanism markers**
- *"prompted in part by"* — Partial-cause verb phrase; assigns agency to a stimulus.
- *"were met with particularly emotive responses"* — Reception verb ("were met with") + intensifier ("particularly") + noun of affect ("emotive responses"): explains *how* the stimulus acted on viewers.

**Contrast / concession pivots**
- *"However, it is more likely that"* — Single-word sentence adverb plus comparative modal: the engine of the rebuttal.

**Pre-existence / audience-attribution markers**
- *"already prevalent amongst"* — Stative past + adverb of anteriority ("already") + locative preposition ("amongst"): locates the cause in the audience before the stimulus.
- *"to whom… attempted to cater"* — Relative-clause catering verb: re-codes both the film and the Klan as responsive suppliers.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern used:** *Conceded surface cause → stronger structural cause with audience reframing.* This is a "demand-side, not supply-side" re-explanation. Replicate it as follows:

1. **State the surface/intuitive cause.** Pick the explanation a reader would jump to first (the visible text or product caused the behaviour). Hedge it (*"may also have been prompted in part by"*) so you retain room to overturn it.
2. **Give one piece of grounding evidence for the surface cause.** Quote or describe a specific feature of the stimulus and the response it produced. Use the verb pair *"were met with"* to attach reception to content.
3. **Pivot with a contrast adverb.** Open the next sentence with *"However"* and a comparative modal (*"it is more likely that"*) — this explicitly tells the reader the first cause is being downgraded, not discarded.
4. **State the stronger cause as something that already existed.** Use *"already prevalent amongst"* + the relevant social group. The adverb *already* is doing real work: it predates the stimulus chronologically.
5. **Reframe both the stimulus and the original group as parallel responders.** Use *"to whom [X] (and [Y]) attempted to cater"* — the parenthetical coordination visually equates the two cultural actors and converts the analysis from "A caused B" into "A and B both answered C."
6. **Footnoting.** Place a citation marker immediately after the surface-cause claim and after the audience-reception evidence so the rebuttal's strength is anchored in sourced observations, not free speculation.
