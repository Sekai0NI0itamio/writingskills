## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **back-matter definitional glossary** sitting alongside an apparatus page (captions + bibliography). The ordered content moves are:

1. **Citation/authority block first** — the section opens with full bibliographic entries (Derry/Connor/Jordan, NIST paper, Stretton, Vedantu). *Why first:* in a research-paper appendix, sources must be locatable before the reader can verify any definition that follows; placing them up top lets a reader cross-check the glossary terms against the cited literature.

2. **Figure captions / appendix labels second** — "Appendix 1: Propanol, Butanol and Pentanol Isomers," then numbered sub-captions ("1-propanol (n-propanol)," "2-propanol (iso-propanol)"). *Why second:* these visually anchor the isomers that the upcoming definitions will need to *refer to* (item 2 in the glossary will say "structural isomers," and the reader must already have seen the isomers on the page).

3. **Glossary header third** — "Important Definitions of terminology." *Why third:* the header is a transition move; it tells the reader "what you just saw (images) and what you are about to read (definitions) belong to the same reference layer."

4. **Glossary items fourth, in dependency order** — combustion enthalpy → structural isomers → homologous series → functional group → primary/secondary/tertiary alcohols. *Why this order:* each later term *uses* an earlier one. "Isomers" requires the reader already to trust "molecular formula." "Homologous series" requires "functional group." "Alcohols classification" requires both "functional group" and "isomers." So the order is **least-dependent → most-dependent**, letting each new definition borrow vocabulary the reader just learned.

**Replicable sequence for another student:** (a) source list, (b) labelled visuals that the terms will refer back to, (c) a single transition header, (d) glossary ordered from simplest/most-independent concept to most-complex/most-dependent concept, with each definition phrased so it can re-use the term defined immediately above it.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### SKELETON A — The glossary-item skeleton (used for items 1–4)
`[Term] is / are [category noun] [that relative clause: shared property]. [Optional second sentence: elaboration with a bracketed example or qualification].`

1. **Slot holds:** Slot 1 = the technical term (noun phrase, often capitalised, sometimes with a symbol in parentheses); Slot 2 = a generic category noun ("enthalpy change," "compounds," "series"); Slot 3 = a *defining* relative clause whose head is the property that makes the term what it is; Slot 4 = an optional parenthetical or dash-clause that gives a concrete instance (a unit, a formula, a sub-class).
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Pick a course concept that can be stated as "X is a Y that Z." State the category generically (don't smuggle the answer into the category), then put the discriminating property in the relative clause. Add the parenthetical only if a standard unit, formula, or example is part of the syllabus definition.
3. **Original filled version:** "**Standard enthalpy change of combustion (ΔHc) is the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burnt in oxygen under standard conditions.**" (The "(usually –CH2–)" parenthetical in item 3 is the same slot filled differently.)
4. **Demonstration fill (different subject, same skeleton):** "**Rate constant (k) is the proportionality constant that relates the rate of reaction to the concentrations of reactants raised to their partial orders in the rate law.**"

### SKELETON B — The "same X, different Y" contrast skeleton (items 2 and 3)
`[Plural noun phrase] are [count] or more [items] that have the same [property A] but different [property B] – [restatement of the contrast in plain words].`

1. **Slot holds:** Slot 1 = the class name in the plural; Slot 2 = a quantifier ("two or more," "a series of"); Slot 3 = property A (what members share, often *formula* or *group*); Slot 4 = property B (what differs, often *arrangement* or *unit*); Slot 5 = a dash-clause that re-says the contrast in non-jargon.
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Choose a concept defined by *invariance plus variation* — something where the shared feature is the formal identity and the differing feature is the structural or behavioural one. State both halves of the contrast explicitly; finish by paraphrasing the contrast in everyday language so a non-specialist reader can check they understood.
3. **Original filled version:** "**Structural isomers are two or more compounds that have the same molecular formula but different structural formulas – the atoms are joined together in different ways.**" (Item 3 uses the same skeleton with the contrast moved inside the sentence: "Each member differs from the next by a common structural unit.")
4. **Demonstration fill (different subject):** "**Enantiomers are two molecules that have the same molecular formula and the same connectivity of atoms but different three-dimensional arrangement in space – the mirror images cannot be superimposed."**

### SKELETON C — The bibliography-entry skeleton

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / enumeration**
- "**Important Definitions of terminology**" — opens the list block and signals an ordered run.
- Numbered items "1." / "2." / "3." / "4." / "5." — carry the reader through the dependency chain.

**Specification / narrowing**
- "**under standard conditions**" (from "Standard enthalpy change of combustion … under standard conditions") — narrows the definition to a constrained case.
- "**(usually –CH2–)**" (from "common structural unit (usually –CH2–)") — flags the *typical* instance while leaving room for exceptions.

**Contrast / comparison**
- "**same molecular formula but different structural formulas**" — the section's core contrast pair; everything else in the glossary rests on it.
- "**Each member differs from the next by**" — comparative move linking one member to the next in a series.

**Definition / explanation verbs**
- "**is the enthalpy change when**" — builds the definition as a *process* ("when one mole … is completely burnt").
- "**are two or more compounds that**" — builds the definition as a *class with a property*.
- "**is a series of compounds that have**" — definition by class membership.
- "**is the atom or group of atoms**" — definition by *referent* (points at the thing itself, not a process).
- "**are described as**" — definition by *classification* (the verb used in the cut-off item 5).

**Evidence handling (in the apparatus around the glossary)**
- "**Research Paper. October 2020.**" — citation form that lets the reader *check* the source.
- "**accessed <date>**" pattern (e.g. "18 October 2020," "November 2020") — marks the source as time-stamped web material.
- Superscripted footnote marker "**12**" attached to the header — links the glossary back to a numbered note elsewhere in the paper.

**Restatement / paraphrase (the dash clause)**
- "**– the atoms are joined together in different ways**" — re-says the technical contrast in plain language; the dash signals "in other words."

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **ordered-glossary / dependency-stack** pattern: it does *not* argue or narrate; it lays down a chain of definitions in which each new term can re-use the term defined immediately before it. To reproduce this pattern for a new idea:

1. **Pick a single technical concept you want the reader to be able to use.** (Here it is "alcohol classification.")
2. **List every term a reader would need to recognise in order to understand that concept.** (Combustion enthalpy, isomer, homologous series, functional group, primary/secondary/tertiary.)
3. **Sort that list from least-dependent to most-dependent.** A term goes *earlier* if it does not require any other term on the list; a term goes *later* if it borrows vocabulary from at least one earlier term. (Combustion enthalpy is standalone; "alcohols" borrows "functional group" which borrows "homologous series.")
4. **Write each entry in a fixed sentence shape** — `X is the Y when Z` for process terms, `X are [count] [items] that have the same A but different B` for class terms, `X is the [thing] that gives the molecule its [property]` for component terms. Using a *repeatable shape* signals to the reader that the items belong to one reference layer.
5. **Open each entry with the term itself, capitalised, optionally with a symbol in parentheses.** This lets the reader scan the page and jump to the entry they need.
6. **Add one dash-clause at the end of the entry that restates the technical point in plain words.** This is the reader's comprehension check — if they can read the plain restatement and nod, the definition has worked.
7. **End the last entry with the *hook back to the body of the paper*** (here, item 5 begins "Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Alcohols:" — the colon signals that the body of the paper will now apply the whole stack). The glossary's job is finished the moment the body of the paper can begin using the terms without re-defining them.

This pattern — *stand-alone term first, dependent term last, fixed sentence shape, plain-language dash-clause, terminal hook to the body* — is the move that makes a glossary a working tool rather than a vocabulary list.
