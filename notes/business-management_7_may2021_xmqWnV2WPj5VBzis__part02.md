## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **results/data-presentation section**, not a discursive one. The ordered content moves are:

1. **Variable 1 (independent factor 1) with raw scores + mean.** Sets up the first data pillar.
2. **Variable 2 (independent factor 2) with raw scores + mean.** Sets up the second data pillar in the *same shape* so the eye can compare columns, averages, and spreads.
3. **Variable 3 (dependent/outcome variable) with raw scores + mean.** This is the payoff: the reader was given two contributing factors, then is shown the outcome that those factors were supposed to influence.

**Why this order works:** the section imitates a mini-experiment in the order components → outcome. The first two tables establish *what was measured* on the input side; the third answers *did it matter?* by reporting retention. Each table is self-contained (raw + average) so the reader never has to flip back, and identical formatting is the mechanism that makes comparison effortless — the eye reads straight down the "Average" cell of all three tables to extract 7.33 → 7.33 → 8.33.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

Because this section is tabular, "expressing ideas" happens through *structural* vocabulary — the labels and connecting words that frame numbers as argument.

- **Topic-framing labels** (name what the numbers mean): "Employees satisfaction with training:" / "Employees satisfaction with salary:" / "Degree of encouraging employees' retention with above improvements:" — these are the section's only "claims"; they tell the reader how to read the grid.
- **Causal-linking phrase** (wires one table to the next): "with above improvements" — turns Table 3 from an isolated score into a *consequence* of the prior two.
- **Aggregation verb** (turns raw rows into a verdict): "Average" — the single word that compresses fifteen rows into one defensible number.
- **Sequencing numerals** (track block order so comparison is possible): "Table 1" / "Table 2" / "Table 3" — the only "sequencing" device in the section; functions like "firstly/secondly/finally" for data.
- **Scale anchor** (sets the interpretive frame): "Rating/10" — defines the maximum so the reader can judge whether 7.33 is high, mid, or low.
- **Implicit contrast operator** (no word, but structural): the *identical format* of all three tables is the device that lets the reader compare "7.33 → 7.33 → 8.33" without being told to. The repetition *is* the connective.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern here is **parallel-comparison-of-components-leading-to-an-outcome**. It explains an idea by *showing the same measurement applied to several inputs and then to the resulting output*, letting the numbers do the arguing.

Step-by-step replication for a new idea:

1. **Pick one outcome you want to explain** (e.g. "why customers churn," "why exam scores rose," "why a plant grew faster"). This becomes your *dependent variable* — it will sit in the final table.
2. **List 2–4 component factors that plausibly drive that outcome.** Each factor becomes its own table. Keep the number of factors small (two is the minimum that makes comparison meaningful; four is the practical maximum before the reader loses the thread).
3. **For each component, build a table in this exact order:** (a) component header naming the factor; (b) two-column schema with an ID column and a fixed-scale rating column (pick one scale, e.g. /10, and use it in *every* table); (c) a body of paired scores split into two visual halves; (d) a single mean/median cell aligned under the rating column; (e) a "Table N" caption.
4. **Use the *same* scale and *same* number of respondents in every table.** Identical format is the mechanism that makes comparison automatic — if the formats drift, the reader cannot compare and the section collapses.
5. **Write the final table's header to *link back* to the previous blocks** using a phrase such as "with the above improvements," "given the prior conditions," or "as a result of the above factors." This single phrase is what turns the last table from a third data point into the *answer*.
6. **Number tables sequentially** (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3) so the reader always knows where they are in the argument.
7. **Resist adding prose between tables.** The pattern's power comes from letting the reader's eye drop the "Average" cell straight down the column to read the argument as a number sequence (7.33 → 7.33 → 8.33). Any sentence between the tables breaks that drop. Save the interpretation for the section that follows.
