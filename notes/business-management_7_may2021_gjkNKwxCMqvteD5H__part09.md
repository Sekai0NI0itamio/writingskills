# Idea Flow Notes: business-management_7_may2021_gjkNKwxCMqvteD5H — TSMC has already halted taking new orders for chips from Huawei, the Nikkei

## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1 (2 sentences)**

- **Sentence 1 — attribution framing.** "Asian Review reported on Monday, citing unnamed sources." This establishes that what follows is *someone else's claim*, delivered with a date and a publication name. It hands the reader to sentence 2 by creating an open slot: *what did they report / what does the source say?*
- **Sentence 2 — official rebuttal (evidence).** "It is market rumor, and TSMC does not disclose customers' order details" — a spokesperson for the company said. This is the *counter-evidence*, delivered via a quoted official who denies the rumour. It closes the rumour-loop opened in sentence 1 and pivots the reader away from "did TSMC confirm this?" toward "if so, what does Huawei do instead?" — a *consequence pivot*.

**Paragraph 2 (2 sentences)**

- **Sentence 1 — proposed alternative (claim).** "Huawei could look to Samsung's chip manufacturing business to make the semiconductors required as an alternative to TSMC." A *speculative solution* is introduced, framing Samsung as the fallback. It hands the reader to sentence 2 by raising an immediate *question*: *would Samsung actually cooperate?*
- **Sentence 2 — obstacle to the alternative (concession + mechanism).** "However, Samsung is 'currently Huawei's biggest competitor … rather push a domestic supplier than a rival company'" — Shah told CNBC. The *however* directly answers the raised question with a *reason why this won't work* (competitor status, geopolitical alignment). This is a *failure of the plan*, which forces the reader forward into a verdict.

**Paragraph 3 (1 sentence)**

- **Sentence 1 — verdict (implication).** "That leaves Huawei in a very tough position." A *transitional sentence* that compresses the failed alternatives into a judgment. Its job is not new evidence — it is *to convert the previous two moves (rumour-blocked, Samsung-blocked) into a single readable consequence*, handing the reader forward to *why* and *how* tough that position is.

**Paragraph 4 (2 sentences)**

- **Sentence 1 — quoted expert diagnosis (authority + specification).** "So from Huawei's perspective, it will help to quickly look to diversify its supply chain further into a non-U.S. tech-based supplier list" — Shah said. This *answers the verdict* by saying what Huawei *must* do, with a quoted expert giving the move authority. It hands the reader to sentence 2 by *raising the difficulty*: *why is this hard?*
- **Sentence 2 — mechanism of the difficulty (evidence + unpack).** "Most of the companies which supply advanced components have some or the other form of (U.S.) equipment or technology being indirectly used" — Shah said. This *answers the difficulty question* by exposing the structural reason: U.S. tech is everywhere in the supply chain. It is the closing move — there is no further question to ask; the section ends in *inescapability*.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **source-commentary section**: the writer is not making a personal argument but *mediating between outside sources* and *extracting the logical implication*. The move sequence is:

1. **Attribution of the news event** — name outlet, date, sources, so the rumour is on the record. *Sets up:* a contested claim that needs a response.
2. **Official response / rebuttal** — quote an insider who pushes back. *Sets up:* the rumour is destabilised, so the writer must move to consequences rather than facts.
3. **First proposed alternative** — state who the affected party *could* turn to. *Sets up:* a "but does that work?" gap.
4. **Obstacle / counter-reason to the alternative** — explain *why* the fallback fails (geopolitics, competition). *Sets up:* a verdict.
6. **Compressing verdict** — a short authorial summary sentence collapsing the failed routes into one judgment. *Sets up:* the explanation of *how* the situation is tough.
7. **Expert diagnosis of the difficulty** — quote an analyst saying what the affected party must attempt. *Sets up:* the reader wants to know *why this is hard*.
8. **Structural mechanism of the difficulty** — explain the underlying reason (U.S. tech is embedded globally). *Closes the loop:* the reader now sees a self-reinforcing trap, not a solvable problem.

The *why this order*: each move answers the unspoken question the previous move raised. The section never lets the reader sit on an unanswered "so what?" — the rebuttal raises "what now?", the alternative raises "will it work?", the obstacle forces a verdict, the verdict forces a mechanism.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Rumour + Rebuttal Pair**

SKELETON:
> [Outlet] reported on [time cue], citing [source type]. "[Quoted denial / official pushback]," [spokesperson for X] said.

- *Slot 1 (noun phrase + reporting verb, past tense):* Name the publication, the timing ("on Monday", "this week"), and how sourced ("citing unnamed sources", "according to people familiar"). Grammar: reporting clause + optional participial phrase.
- *Slot 2 (direct quote + attribution):* A short spokesperson denial that *denies the report* and/or cites confidentiality. Grammar: quote → comma → "a spokesperson for [organisation] said" or "[name] told [outlet]."
- **How to fill with a different idea:** Pick any corporate rumour (a merger, a product launch, a layoff). Slot 1 = the news outlet framing it; slot 2 = the company's official "we don't comment / that's speculation" line.
- **Original fill:** "Asian Review reported on Monday, citing unnamed sources." + "'It is market rumor, and TSMC does not disclose customers' order details,' a spokesperson for the company said."
- **Demo fill with new idea:** "Reuters reported on Tuesday, citing people familiar with the talks." + "'No deal has been signed and we do not comment on speculation,' a spokesperson for Vertex Logistics said."

**Skeleton B — Fallback + Obstacle Pair**

SKELETON:
> [Party] could look to [alternative actor] to [do the thing required] as an alternative to [blocked option]. However, [alternative actor] is "[reason the alternative won't work — competitor / rival / ideological mismatch]," [analyst] told [outlet].

- *Slot 1 (modal + infinitive, present speculation):* The blocked party could turn to someone specific to fill the gap.
- *Slot 2 (direct quote + analyst attribution, embedded reason):* An expert quote explaining *why* the alternative actor is structurally unwilling or unable, framed as a single block of cause.
- **How to fill with a different idea:** Pick any scenario where Plan A is blocked. Slot 1 = the obvious Plan B. Slot 2 = the expert reason Plan B is hostile (competitor, rival ideology, regulation).
- **Original fill:** "Huawei could look to Samsung's chip manufacturing business to make the semiconductors required as an alternative to TSMC." + "'currently Huawei's biggest competitor and so it would rather push a domestic supplier than a rival company which is South Korean and technically a U.S. ally,' Shah told CNBC."
- **Demo fill with new idea:** "The startup could look to BorrowFlow to extend the bridge loan as an alternative to CreditLane." + "'currently BorrowFlow's biggest competitor and so it would rather refer the client to its own partner bank than fund a rival's deal,' Mehta told Bloomberg."

**Skeleton C — Compressing Verdict**

SKELETON:
> That leaves [party] in a very [adjective] position.

- *Slot 1 (demonstrative + present tense):* A single short sentence that *sums up* what the previous moves have shown, using "That leaves" to chain back.
- **How to fill with a different idea:** Use this whenever a series of blocked alternatives needs to collapse into one judgment. The verb "leaves" forces the reader to read backward and forward at once.
- **Original fill:** "That leaves Huawei in a very tough position."
- **Demo fill with new idea:** "That leaves the council in a very awkward position."

**Skeleton D — Expert Diagnosis + Mechanism**

SKELETON:
> So from [party]'s perspective, it will help to quickly [proposed action] further into a [specifier] list. Most of the [companies / actors] which [supply / do X] have some or the other form of [constraint] being indirectly [used / present], [analyst] said.

- *Slot 1 (expert prescription, quoted):* What the affected party *should* do, framed as a strategic imperative ("quickly look to diversify").
- *Slot 2 (mechanism of why it's hard, quoted):* The structural reason the prescription is near-impossible — the obstacle is *embedded everywhere*, not at one node.
- **How to fill with a different idea:** Slot 1 = an expert's strategic recommendation (diversify, switch supplier, change law). Slot 2 = the systemic reason it's nearly impossible (every alternative is contaminated by the same constraint — tech, regulation, capital, ideology).
- **Original fill:** "'it will help to quickly look to diversify its supply chain further into a non-U.S. tech-based supplier list which is a humongous ask in the short-to-mid-term. Most of the companies which supply advanced components have some or the other form of (U.S.) equipment or technology being indirectly used,' Shah said."
- **Demo fill with new idea:** "'it will help to quickly rebuild its customer base further into a non-state-bid pipeline which is a huge ask in the short term. Most of the agencies which place advanced orders have some or the other form of government procurement rule being indirectly applied,' Park told the FT."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / framing:** "reported on Monday, citing unnamed sources" — slots a piece of evidence into time and source-credibility space.
- **Sequencing / move markers:** none used — the section advances by *quoted content*, not by numbered signposting.
- **Causal / consequence:** "leaves … in a very tough position" — collapses prior moves into consequence; "it will help to quickly look to diversify" — frames the prescription as causally required.
- **Contrast / concession:** "However, Samsung is 'currently Huawei's biggest competitor'" — the single explicit concession that kills the alternative.
- **Specification:** "as an alternative to TSMC" — narrows the function of Samsung; "in the short-to-mid-term" — bounds the difficulty in time.
- **Evidence handling:** "a spokesperson for the company said" — attribution of rebuttal; "Shah told CNBC" / "Shah said" — attribution of expert analysis.
- **Explanation verbs:** none used as formal definitions; the section uses *naming* ("competitor", "domestic supplier", "rival company") rather than definitional verbs.
- **Embedded connectives inside quotes:** "and so it would rather push … than a rival company" — paired comparative "rather … than"; "which is South Korean and technically a U.S. ally" — relative-clause specification; "have some or the other form of (U.S.) equipment or technology being indirectly used" — exhaustive universal ("some or the other") signalling inescapability.
- **Transition signal:** "That leaves" — demonstrative summary move that chains backward and forward.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **"blocked alternative → compressing verdict → embedded-obstacle diagnosis" pattern**. The replication is:

1. **State the news event as an attributed rumour.** Give outlet, time cue, and source-type hedge. Do not yet assert it as fact.
2. **Quote an official rebuttal.** Make it a denial + a confidentiality reason in the same short quote. This destabilises the rumour so the rest of the section is about *what happens now*, not whether the news is true.
3. **Name the obvious fallback.** Use "could look to [X] to [do Y] as an alternative to [blocked option]." This is the reader's first guess — surface it so you can kill it.
4. **Kill the fallback with a single concession.** Open with "However," and put the structural reason in a quoted expert voice ("competitor", "ally", "rival"). One sentence is enough; the kill must feel *closed*, not negotiable.
5. **Compress with a verdict sentence.** "That leaves [party] in a very [adjective] position." This is the only authorial (non-quoted) sentence in the pattern. Its presence is what tells them the section has stopped weighing options and is now diagnosing.
6. **Quote the expert prescription.** Frame it from the affected party's perspective ("So from [party]'s perspective, it will help to quickly [verb] …"). The word "quickly" signals urgency.
7. **Quote the mechanism of impossibility.** End on a *universal-quantifier* sentence ("Most of the [actors] which [do X] have some or the other form of [constraint]"). The point of this sentence is to make the reader feel the constraint is *structural*, not situational — there is no obvious exit. Stop here. Do not offer a solution; the pattern's job is to show the trap is closed.
