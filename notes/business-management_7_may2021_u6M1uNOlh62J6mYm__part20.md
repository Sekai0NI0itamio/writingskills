## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1 (transitional tail + interview Q&A)**

1. **[Context/quote from previous]** — "locked up in the same gym for 6 months" → hands off via **cause → expected remedy**: the change mentioned next is framed as the obvious positive fix for that long confinement.
2. **[Implied verdict/elaboration on previous speaker]** — "new stuff and ideas will possibly excite… staying" → hands off via **contrast**: the hopeful future flips the reader into a new speaker's pragmatic constraints.
3. **[Transition/framing move — interviewer prompt]** — "Student: Is there anything else… like to add?" → hands off via **invitation**: this question opens the floor, so the next sentence must be the respondent's substantive reply.
4. **[Main claim of new speaker]** — "The biggest issue we currently have right now is the facility" → hands off via **specification**: "facility" needs to be unpacked, so the next sentence narrows to winter context.
5. **[Context/cause for the claim]** — "and space during the winter as the national school was introduced recently" → hands off via **contrast pivot** ("however" coming): a setup about adequacy is about to be undermined.
6. **[Counter-evidence to soften the critique]** — "For the amount of teachers I believe we're okay" → hands off via **specification**: "okay" gets quantified in the next clause.
7. **[Evidence/data]** — "We have 3 PE teachers, scheduled… 50 kids… around 16 for each teacher" → hands off via **evaluative verdict**: the data now needs a judgment word.
8. **[Evaluative comment on data]** — "which is a very generous amount" → hands off via **contrast pivot ("however")**: generosity gets immediately undercut by the spatial problem.
9. **[Restated main complaint with concrete image]** — "having 50 students in a single gym is just too much" → hands off via **new instance**: a specific sub-problem is introduced.
10. **[Specific example of facility misuse]** — "Having a swimming pool and not being allowed to use it" → hands off via **exaggeration/comparison**: the absurdity is escalated by an extreme comparative claim.
11. **[Hyperbolic comparative claim]** — "I think we're the only PE department in the world which is not allowed to teach swimming" → hands off via **cause chain**: the ban is now explained via a policy.

13. **[Consequence of the rule + reason]** — "and since we don't have a beginner's swimming pool, we're not allowed to teach them at all" → terminal: ends the paragraph in a final restriction, leaving no further move.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **transitional/interview-excerpt section** that bridges two voices. The ordered moves are:

1. **Closing tail of previous speaker** (a condition + implied remedy) — sets up that some change is expected.
2. **Neutral interviewer prompt** ("Is there anything else?") — hands the floor cleanly, signaling a new voice.
3. **Headline claim of the new speaker** ("the biggest issue is X") — anchors the reader with the central complaint.
4. **Contextual cause** ("because Y was introduced") — supplies the *why now* that justifies the claim.
5. **Concession / counter-evidence** ("but on the other metric we're okay") — shows balance, builds credibility before the critique returns.
6. **Quantification of the conceded point** ("3 teachers / 50 kids / 16 each") — turns the concession into data.
7. **Verdict on the data** ("very generous") — bridges to the "however" turn.
8. **Contrast pivot back to the critique** ("however, the space is the problem") — the structural *turn*.
9. **Concretization of the spatial complaint** ("50 kids in one gym") — anchors the abstract "facility" in an image.
10. **Anecdotal example of absurdity** ("pool exists but unused") — escalates with a vivid case.
11. **Hyperbolic comparison** ("only department in the world…") — rhetorical intensification.
12. **Causal mechanism** ("policy says…") — explains *why* the absurd is the case, rather than leaving it as opinion.
13. **Final consequence of the mechanism** ("so we can't teach them at all") — closes with a hard, concrete result.

**Why this order**: the speaker must first *earn the right* to complain (concession + numbers), then *pivot* back to the real problem, then *escalate* with a vivid example, then *justify* it with a mechanism. Skipping the concession kills credibility; skipping the mechanism leaves the complaint sounding like opinion.

**SKELETON**: "[Topic]'s biggest issue is [A] because [recent change]. For [metric B] we're okay: [data/quantification], which is [positive verdict]. However, [A restated with concrete image]."

2. **Slot 2 (Concession with quantification):** pick *one* metric where the situation is actually fine, then back it with numbers. 3. **Slot 3 (Positive verdict on the data):** one evaluative phrase ("generous", "comfortable", "sufficient"). Fill with an adjective that you'd defend with the numbers above.
4. **Slot 4 (Pivot + restatement of the headline problem with image):** use "however" and re-state the problem in a concrete physical scene. Fill: pick a number + a room/location to make it visual.

**SKELETON**: "[Concrete absurd situation]. I think we are the only [group] in the world that [does/doesn't do X]. [Mechanism/rule] states that [condition], and since [missing prerequisite], we [cannot/never] [action]."

1. **Slot 1 (Vivid absurd example):** a real resource that is present but unusable. Grammatical shape: "Having [thing] and not being allowed to use it." Fill: name a facility/tool that exists but is locked down.
2. **Slot 2 (Hyperbolic comparative claim):** "I think we're the only [category] in the world which [isn't allowed to do X]." Fill: pick a category (department, school, lab) and an action they obviously should do.
3. **Slot 3 (Mechanism — the policy):** name the rule. Grammatical shape: "We have a policy which states that [condition]." Fill: write the policy in plain if-then language.
4. **Slot 4 (Consequence + missing-prerequisite reason):** "since [we lack Y], we are not allowed to [do X] at all." Fill: identify the small missing piece that the rule uses to block everything.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / framing**
- "as the national school was introduced recently" — anchors a claim in time.
- "right now" — emphasizes present-tense urgency.
- "currently have right now" — doubled present-marker to stress immediacy.

**Concession / contrast pivot**
- "For the amount of teachers I believe we're okay" — admits a strength before the critique.
- "however, having 50 students in a single gym" — the structural turn back to the problem.

**Specification / quantification**
- "We have 3 PE teachers, scheduled for every lesson" — concrete count + scope.
- "spread amongst 50 kids, which is around 16 for each teacher" — derives a ratio.
- "which is a very generous amount" — evaluative label on the ratio.

**Anecdote / vivid example**
- "Having a swimming pool and not being allowed to use it" — present-anchored absurd image.
- "because it is not 'safe'" — scare-quotes signal skepticism of the stated reason.

**Hyperbolic comparison**
- "I think we're the only PE department in the world which is not allowed" — escalation device.

**Mechanism / cause explanation**
- "We have a policy which states that" — names a rule as causal agent.
- "since we don't have a beginner's swimming pool" — supplies the missing prerequisite that drives the consequence.
- "we're not allowed to teach them at all" — final hard consequence.

**Interview-handling vocabulary**
- "Is there anything else that you would like to add?" — the open prompt that signals a new voice.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **Concession-then-pivot-then-amplify-then-mechanism** pattern. Steps to replicate with any new idea:

1. **Open with the headline claim** as a single noun-phrase problem ("The biggest issue is X").
2. **Supply a temporal/contextual cause** so the reader understands *why now* ("because Y was just introduced").
3. **Make a concession** on a *different* metric — pick a sub-area where you are genuinely fine, and say so plainly ("For Z, we're okay").
4. **Quantify the concession** with at least one count and one derived ratio (number of staff : number of users).
5. **Label the data** with a one-word verdict ("generous", "adequate", "comfortable").
6. **Pivot with "however"** and restate the headline problem in a concrete image (a number + a physical room).
7. **Add a vivid anecdotal example** that escalates the absurdity — something that exists but is unusable.
8. **Push to hyperbole** with a comparative claim ("the only [group] in the world…").
9. **Reveal the mechanism** — name the policy/rule that produces the absurdity.
10. **State the consequence** triggered by that rule, plus the missing prerequisite that makes the rule bite ("since we lack Y, we are not allowed to Z at all"). End there.
