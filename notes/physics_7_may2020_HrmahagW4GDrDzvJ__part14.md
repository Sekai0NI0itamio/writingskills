## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (closing the linear-model evaluation)
- **S1** — Evidence specification + evaluative verdict. *"uncertainties in the gradients, at 35% for the large ball and 60% for the small ball; an aspect which hinders the strength of the linear relationships."* Hands to S2 by: delivering the **bad news first** (high gradient uncertainty weakens the trend) — this primes a contrast with what is numerically safer.
- **S2** — Contrast pivot + comparative quantification. *"On the other hand, the uncertainties in the intercepts are significantly lower at about 2%."* Hands off the paragraph by: closing the evaluation with the **good-news counterpart**, so the reader leaves with a balanced ledger of weaknesses/strengths before the next model is introduced.

**Paragraph 2** (Figure 5 caption — treated as a paragraph because it carries instructional prose)
- **S1** — Definition + setup. *"showing a quadratic relationship between salt concentration, c, and viscosity, η, using a small steel ball (red) and a larger one (blue)."* Hands to S2 by: telling the reader **what to look at** (relationship, variables, colour key) before pointing at the evidence inside it.
- **S2** — Evidence pointer. *"The equations and the r2 values are given."* Hands off the paragraph by: directing attention to the **numeric outputs** that the next paragraph will interpret.

**Paragraph 3** (introducing the quadratic model)
- **S1** — Addition / transition. *"In addition to the linear graph, quadratic models have been plotted."* Hands to S2 by: introducing the quadratic as a **new sibling** to the linear, so the reader expects a parallel description next.
- **S2** — Parallel property claim. *"Similarly, the models pass through all the data points."* Hands to S3 by: establishing the **shared trait** (both fit the data) so the next sentence can pivot to the **difference** (goodness of fit).
- **S3** — Tentative superiority claim + caveat. *"At first glance, the quadratic models seem to fit better than the linear ones, due to the slightly higher r2 values, however this is a point of discussion."* Hands off the paragraph by: raising a **verdict but refusing to commit**, which hands the unresolved tension straight into the conclusion.

**Paragraph 4** (Conclusion opener)
- **S1** — Context (RQ) + headline claim. *"In response to my research question 'How does the salinity of brine affect its viscosity (Pas)?', the data obtained seems to support my hypothesis that increasing salinity increases viscosity; a positive correlation."* Hands to S2 by: answering the RQ with a **direction-of-effect claim**, which invites a summary of *how* the claim was established.
- **S2** — Evidence summary / enumeration. *"Two different models have been found, linear and quadratic."* Hands to S3 by: naming the **competing candidates** so the next sentence can begin to adjudicate between them.
- **S3** — Concession / qualifier (truncated). *"Although the statistical analyses have showed that the quadratic…"* Hands forward by: opening an **although-construction** that forces the reader to expect a "but" — the conclusion's verdict is being deferred but staged.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Close out the prior model** with a paired uncertainty verdict (high gradient uncertainty → contrast → low intercept uncertainty). This gives the linear trend its final credibility score before introducing any rival.
2. **Caption the new figure** so the reader can decode colour, variables, and what numbers are visible. Sets up the comparison by anchoring the reader visually.
3. **Introduce the alternative model** ("In addition to… quadratic models"). Names the rival before judging it.
4. **Assert the shared property** ("Similarly, the models pass through all the data points"). Establishes parity so the later contrast is meaningful.
5. **Stage a tentative superiority verdict** with evidence ("due to the slightly higher r² values") followed immediately by a deferral ("however this is a point of discussion"). This is the key IB-grade move — never let a claim stand unqualified.
6. **Open the conclusion** by echoing the RQ, restating the direction-of-effect, then listing the candidate models, and finally beginning a concession about which model wins. Order matters: RQ → answer → methods summary → adjudication.

**Why this order:** the section must *retire one model fairly* (1), *position the reader visually* (2), *introduce the rival on equal footing* (3–4), *flag the disagreement* (5), and only then *deliver a hedged verdict* (6). Skipping any step breaks the evidential chain.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — "Paired Uncertainty Verdict"
**Template:**
"[Quantity A] are at [high value] for [case 1] and [higher/lower value] for [case 2]; an aspect which [negative verb] the strength of the [relationship]. On the other hand, the [Quantity B] are significantly [comparative adjective] at about [low value]%."

- **Slot 1** — bad metric + two numeric values + negative-impact verb. Grammatical shape: noun phrase with two parallel numbers ("at X% for … and Y% for …") + relative clause with evaluative verb.
- **Slot 2** — "On the other hand" pivot + good metric + much smaller value. Grammatical shape: contrastive adverbial + noun + comparative adverb + single number.
- **Fill with different idea:** pick **two derived quantities** from your experiment (e.g. control variance vs. treatment variance, response-time spread vs. accuracy spread). State the weaker one numerically first, then pivot.
- **Original fill:** *"uncertainties in the gradients, at 35% for the large ball and 60% for the small ball; an aspect which hinders the strength of the linear relationships. On the other hand, the uncertainties in the intercepts are significantly lower at about 2%."*
- **Demonstration fill (different subject):** "Standard deviations in the control reaction times reached 1.4 s for Trial 1 and 1.7 s for Trial 2; an aspect which weakens any claim of a cognitive-load effect. On the other hand, the mean differences between conditions are markedly tighter at roughly 0.2 s."

### Skeleton B — "Alternative Model Introduction with Tentative Verdict"
**Template:**
"In addition to [first model], [second model] have been plotted. Similarly, the [second model] [shared property]. At first glance, the [second model] seem[s] to [verb] better than the [first model], due to [reason with metric], however this is a point of discussion."

- **Slot 1** — addition pivot + new model. "In addition to" + prior model + new-model noun phrase in passive ("have been plotted / fitted / proposed").
- **Slot 2** — parallel-property claim. "Similarly" + subject + verb mirroring slot 1's behaviour.
- **Slot 3a** — tentative superiority. "At first glance" + comparison verb + "due to" + metric.
- **Slot 3b** — deferral. "however this is a point of discussion."
- **Fill with different idea:** pick **two competing model families** (linear vs. exponential, deterministic vs. stochastic, Type I vs. Type II fit). Pair them and force the second to look marginally better on one metric.
- **Original fill:** *"In addition to the linear graph, quadratic models have been plotted. Similarly, the models pass through all the data points. At first glance, the quadratic models seem to fit better than the linear ones, due to the slightly higher r² values, however this is a point of discussion."*
- **Demonstration fill (different subject):** "In addition to the exponential decay, a logistic curve has been fitted. Similarly, the logistic curve passes through every data point. At first glance, the logistic curve appears to capture the plateau better than the exponential, due to its bounded asymptote, however this is a point of discussion."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / addition**
- "In addition to" — *"In addition to the linear graph, quadratic models have been plotted."* (used to introduce a rival alongside the prior model)
- "Similarly" — *"Similarly, the models pass through all the data points."* (used to assert a shared property before pivoting to a difference)

**Contrast / concession**
- "On the other hand" — *"On the other hand, the uncertainties in the intercepts are significantly lower"* (numeric contrast pivot)
- "At first glance" — *"At first glance, the quadratic models seem to fit better"* (tentative-seeming marker)
- "however" — *"however this is a point of discussion."* (flag that the apparent verdict will be re-examined)
- "Although" — *"Although the statistical analyses have showed that the quadratic…"* (concession that defers the final call)

**Causal / evidentiary**
- "due to" — *"due to the slightly higher r² values"* (attributing the apparent fit advantage to a specific metric)
- "seems to support" — *"the data obtained seems to support my hypothesis"* (hedged claim verb)
- "have been found" — *"Two different models have been found, linear and quadratic."* (evidence-summary verb, present perfect)
- "have showed" — *"the statistical analyses have showed that the quadratic…"* (results-handling verb)

**Specification / comparison adverbs**
- "significantly lower" — *"the uncertainties in the intercepts are significantly lower"* (intensifier marking the magnitude of the gap)
- "an aspect which hinders" — *"an aspect which hinders the strength of the linear relationships"* (noun + relative clause that **evaluates** rather than just states)

**Stance verbs for hedging**
- "seem to" — *"the quadratic models seem to fit better"* (epistemic softener)
- "appears to" / "seems to" — both used to keep the reader aware the claim is provisional

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section's dominant pattern is: **Evaluate previous model → introduce alternative → assert shared property → flag apparent superiority with metric → defer verdict → restate in conclusion with concession.**

To replicate it for a new idea, take these steps in order:

1. **Close the previous model on its own terms.** Give one specific number (or pair of numbers) for the model's weakest measurement, then use a negative-impact verb (hinders / weakens / undermines) to evaluate what that number does to the conclusion. This is your "exit verdict."
2. **Add the contrasting measurement with "On the other hand."** Use a single, much smaller number for the model's strongest measurement, and an intensifier ("significantly," "markedly," "substantially").
3. **Introduce the alternative model with "In addition to [prior model], [new model] have been [plotted / fitted / proposed]."** State the new model in the passive voice so the focus is on the model, not the doer.
4. **Mirror one shared property with "Similarly."** Show that the new model does at least one thing the old model also did — this is what makes the comparison fair.
5. **Deliver a hedged apparent superiority with "At first glance … seems to …, due to [specific metric]."** The hedge ("seems," "at first glance") is non-optional; it is what keeps the move 6 alive.
6. **Defer the verdict with "however this is a point of discussion."** This sentence is the engine of the conclusion — without it, the section has no tension to resolve later.
7. **Open the conclusion by quoting your RQ verbatim**, then echo the direction-of-effect ("a positive/negative correlation"), list the competing models, and begin an "Although the [analysis type] have showed that the [preferred model]…" construction to stage the final adjudication.

The through-line is **claim → metric → hedge → defer → re-stage in conclusion**. A reader who follows all seven steps will see the logic move from evidence to verdict without ever feeling that the verdict was sprung on them.
