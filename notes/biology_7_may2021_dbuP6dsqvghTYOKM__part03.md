## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **concession → mechanistic chemistry partition** block. The ordered moves are:

1. **Concede the downside of the recommended substance.** Names the negative effect first so the reader cannot accuse the writer of one-sided advocacy. *Sets up:* the need for chemical detail that explains the magnitude of the risk.
2. **Generalise the chemistry across a category.** State the partition (organic vs inorganic) for the broad group. *Sets up:* a contrast with one species.
3. **Quantify the dominant species.** Give the % share of the dominant iodine form in the general case. *Sets up:* the rule that will be broken.
4. **Contrast with one specific species.** Use "However" to introduce Kombu / *Laminaria japonica* as the exception. *Sets up:* a re-quantification at species level.
5. **State the species-level form.** Quote the reduced-form identity (iodide). *Sets up:* the solubility claim that quantifies it.
6. **Quantify solubility.** Give the % that is water-soluble. *Sets up:* the right to break that soluble fraction into forms.
7. **Quantify the form inside the soluble fraction.** Give the % that is I⁻ within the soluble share. *Sets up:* the residual category.
8. **Define the residual by subtraction.** What is left after the quantified form is "considered as" the other category.

**Why this order:** concession must precede detail (credibility), general must precede specific (frame of reference), and each percentage must precede the percentage that *partitions* it (soluble % before the form-within-soluble %). The "However" between general and specific is the hinge — without it the reader would not register the deviation.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Concession paragraph (claim → named harm)**
```
SKELETON: "However, [the recommended substance] may also [verb] negative
effects, such as '[quoted technical harm] due to [mechanism phrase]' ([author
et al. page])."
```
1. **Slot 1** ("However"): single-word concession pivot.
2. **Slot 2** (substance): noun phrase naming the thing just defended.
3. **Slot 3** (verb): a neutral action verb ("have", "carry", "produce").
4. **Slot 4** (quoted harm): a noun phrase inside quotation marks naming one specific adverse outcome.
5. **Slot 5** ("due to …"): causal fragment explaining the harm's mechanism.
6. **Slot 6** (citation): parenthetical author + page.

**How to fill with a different idea:** Pick the substance or intervention you have just advocated. Identify *one* recognised downside. Find a source that names both the harm and its mechanism. Slot the harm in quotation marks, then attach the mechanism as "due to [quote fragment]".

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Concession / pivot:** "*However*, excessive intake of iodine may also…" — used to redirect from praise to cost.
- **Cause / consequence:** "*due to* persistent stimulation by thyroid-stimulating…" — attaches a mechanism to a named harm.
- **Specification / example:** "such as 'iodine-induced goitre…'" — narrows an abstract claim to one named instance.
- **Category framing:** "*In marine algae*, 'iodine exists as a combination…'" — situates a chemistry claim inside a class.
- **Form / state verb:** "is present in both inorganic and organic forms"; "*exists as* a combination of…" — partitions a substance into named forms.
- **Evidence-quoting pattern:** "with 'more than 60% of total soluble iodine [being] I-'" — sandwich structure: `with '…[being]…' (citation)`.
- **Contrast within a category:** "*However, in Kombu (Laminaria japonica)*…" — same connector, but redirected to one species as the exception.
- **Cumulative evidence:** "*Furthermore*, it was found that…" — adds a second, independent measurement rather than restating.
- **Definition-by-subtraction:** "The remaining insoluble residue would be considered as organic iodine." — uses "*would be considered as*" to assign the leftover fraction a label.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **concession → quantified partition** pattern. To replicate it on a new idea:

1. **State the concessive counter-effect.** Open with "However," then name one documented downside of the substance/intervention you have just defended. End the sentence with a quote that names the harm.
2. **Attach the mechanism to the harm.** Inside the same quote, add "due to [biological/chemical process]". This converts the harm from a label into an explanation.
3. **Move to the broad category.** Start a new paragraph with "In [category]," and state the partition: the substance "exists as a combination of [form A] and [form B]".
4. **Quantify the dominant form in the general case.** Add "with '[X%] of total [medium] [substance] [being] [form A]' (citation)." Use "[being]" inside the brackets so the grammatically broken quote still reads naturally.
5. **Pivot to the specific exception.** Begin a sentence with "However, in [species/example]," and restate that the substance is present in both forms — signalling that this case will diverge from the general ratio.
6. **Specify the dominant form for the exception.** Quote a phrase identifying which reduced/oxidised/protein-bound form dominates in this species, and cite it.
7. **Add a second, independent measurement with "Furthermore."** Quote a percentage establishing a sub-property (solubility, bioavailability, etc.) of the same species, and cite it.
8. **Nest the form-percentage inside that sub-property.** Add "with '[Y%]' (citation) of the [sub-property noun] being [form A] coupled with low [form B] values." This stacks a percentage inside the one just given.
9. **Close with a one-sentence remainder.** Write "The remaining [sub-property] residue would be considered as [form B]." Use "*would be considered as*" so the definition sounds inferred rather than asserted.
