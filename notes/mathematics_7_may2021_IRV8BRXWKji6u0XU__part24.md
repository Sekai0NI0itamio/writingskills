## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **"reflective generaliser"** section — its job is to convert a finding established earlier into an open theoretical question.

1. **Inherited context tail** — picks up the prior section's last phrase ("error of the approximation"). Sets up the bridge so the new paragraph reads as a continuation, not a restart.
2. **Pattern observation** — flags a regularity already implicit in earlier calculations ("a certain pattern forming here"). Sets up the next move: a question is the natural consequence of any observed regularity.
3. **Informal question** — names the loose inquiry ("what would happen to the efficiency…"). Sets up the pivot to formal language by leaving the question deliberately vague.
4. **Formal restatement** — the pivot "Or rather more formally" upgrades the loose inquiry into a precise comparison (more lines vs. minimum). Sets up symbolisation because a formal question demands named quantities.
5. **Symbolisation with inequality (eq. 16)** — assigns variable M and constants c₁ < c₂, states the claim as a limit comparison. Sets up generalisation because writing the two-constant case begs the n-constant case.
6. **Generalisation to i constants (eq. 17)** — extends to a strict chain, leaving the question open. Ends the section on an unresolved question so a future section can address it.

The order matters: observation must precede question; question must precede formalisation; a two-constant inequality must precede the i-constant chain. Skipping any step breaks the rhetorical engine — a formalisation without an observation feels unmotivated; a generalisation without a base case feels groundless.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / chaining the reader forward**
- "So we may actually spot" — opens the observation chain by chaining onto prior context: "So we may actually spot a certain pattern forming here, where we continuously add…"
- "So infact here" — chains the question onto the observation: "So infact here the question of what actually would happen…"
- "So if we were to denote" — chains the symbolisation onto the formal question: "So if we were to denote the number grid lines by M…"

**Cause / consequence (used lightly, mostly as rhetorical softeners)**
- "So" repeated three times — functions less as logical consequence here and more as connective glue between successive reframings of the same underlying point.

**Transition / register-shifting**
- "Or rather more formally" — the single pivot that flips from informal question to formal restatement: "Or rather more formally, would adding more and more grid lines…"
- "Which could also be generalized" — the second pivot that flips from two-constant case to i-constant case: "Which could also be generalized to the question of whether or not…"

**Question-posing verbs/phrases**
- "could be posed" — flags the inquiry as something raised, not yet answered: "the question of what actually would happen…could be posed"
- "would it be true that" — flags the conjecture as conditional, not asserted: "would it be true that for some constants c₁ < c₂…"
- "Or rather more formally, would…" — interrogative form used to frame an open investigation.

**Specification / hedging**
- "a certain pattern" — soft claim-warrant, asks the reader to accept the regularity provisionally.
- "some constants" — quantifier-soft, leaves the choice of constants open.

**Formalisation verbs / connectives**
- "denote … by" — assigns a symbol to a quantity: "if we were to denote the number grid lines by M"
- "such that" — defines the ordering among indices: "such that cᵢ > cᵢ₋₁, cᵢ₋₂, …, c₂, c₁"

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section relies on the **observation → informal question → formal restatement → symbolisation → generalisation** pattern. The aim is to take something already established and re-present it as an open theoretical question, in escalating layers of formality.

Step-by-step replication for a NEW idea:

1. **Inherit the previous section's closing phrase.** Begin your paragraph with the last few words of the prior paragraph (e.g. "error of the approximation"). Do not start a brand-new sentence. This signals continuity to whoever is reading.

2. **Claim a pattern out loud.** Open a sentence with "So we may actually spot a certain pattern forming here, where…" and describe one concrete repetition (the pattern's evidence). This is a *soft claim* — "a certain pattern" — and you are inviting the reader to take it on trust for now.

3. **Raise the loose follow-up question.** Begin the next sentence with "So infact here the question of what actually would happen to [the quantity you care about] as [you vary something] could be posed." Keep this question *deliberately vague*; you are about to sharpen it.

4. **Pivot to formality.** Use the exact hinge "Or rather more formally," and convert the loose question into a *binary comparison*: "would [more of X], yield [better Y] than [minimum X]?" End with a question mark — this is the moment the reader sees you are framing an investigation, not asserting a result.

5. **Symbolise.** Begin a new sentence with "So if we were to denote [quantity] by [single capital letter]…" Assign that letter to the thing you are varying, then write the formal inequality with two specific index values (e.g. c₁ < c₂). Number it as an equation in parentheses, and append a domain condition ("at some finite N").

6. **Generalise.** Open the final sentence with "Which could also be generalized to the question of whether or not for some [general index] ∈ [domain], such that [strict ordering chain]…" Replace the two index values with a chain of i indices, number it as the next equation in parentheses, and leave the question *open*. End the section there — do not resolve the question yourself; that is the job of whatever follows.
