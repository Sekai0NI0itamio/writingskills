## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **two-part explanation block** that moves from **culinary consequence → biological mechanism**, in that order, for a specific pedagogical reason:

1. **Establish real-world stake first.** Begin with the practical situation (food/cooking/liquid) so the reader understands *why* the biochemistry matters. Without this, T3/T4 feels abstract.
2. **Name the process and its actors.** Introduce the chain of participants (organs/glands/agents) as a single quoted unit so the citation carries the structural overview for you.
3. **Zoom into the critical reaction.** Mark it with superlative language ("most important") to license selectivity — you do not need to describe every reaction.
4. **Identify the molecular products and their destination.** Use parallel construction ("X and Y") and trace their physical path (diffuse into blood).
5. **Assign physiological function.** State what the products *do* in the body.
6. **Reveal the regulatory mechanism.** Pivot from action to control — name the feedback principle.
7. **Unpack the feedback concretely.** Give the trigger (fall in X), the responder (gland), and the corrective release. End on the closing of the loop.

The logic: **practical stake → process overview → key reaction → product/destination → function → regulator → feedback loop closure.** Each move earns the next: stake earns interest; overview earns permission to select; selection earns focus on products; products earn the function question; function earns the control question; control earns the loop's completion.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is a **"real-world consequence → quoted process overview → superlative zoom → named paired products → joint function → feedback announcement → trigger → glandular response → corrective release"** chain. To replicate on a new idea, follow these steps:

1. **Open with the practical stake.** State a real situation where the substance leaves its carrier (cooking, brewing, soaking, rinsing). Name the carrier, the substance, and the verb of loss.
2. **Deliver the "Therefore, unless…" verdict.** Make the loss of benefit conditional on the carrier being used up.
3. **Give the physical reason for the loss** using "as it would have [dissolved/degraded/oxidised] into [the medium]."
4. **Transition with "In the [domain] of [X] in [system],"** to move from the kitchen/lab into the body.
5. **Quote a source for the whole chain of actors** — four participants in a single parenthetical citation. This buys you structural credibility in minimal words.
6. **Use "The most important reactions from the [process]"** to license selecting one branch of the pathway.
7. **Name two paired products in parallel** with parentheses for abbreviations (T3 and T4, PTH and calcitonin).
8. **Add a second citation for the products' movement** — a quoted verb of transport ("diffuse into," "enter the," "are carried by").
9. **State joint function** with "Both [A] and [B] [agents] are responsible for [function]."
10. **Announce the regulator** with "; this relies on a feedback mechanism."
11. **Specify the trigger** — "A fall in the levels of [A] or [B]."
12. **Close the loop** — "would stimulate the [gland]'s secretion of [signaller], leading to the release of [corrective output] from the [source organ] into [destination]."

The mechanism works because each step narrows the reader's attention: world → body → pathway → key step → molecules → function → control → loop closure. No sentence asks the reader to make a leap larger than the previous one prepared.
