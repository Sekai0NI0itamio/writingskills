## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **analysis-with-uncertainty-discussion** move set. The sequence is:

1. **Dual-trend claim + paired equations** (sets up that two variables share a trend, and gives their numeric forms so percentages can be applied).
2. **Single-variable error focus** (picks the noisier equation — "the latter" — and quantifies its error).
3. **Worst-case implication** (asks: if we propagate the error, what alternative trend becomes possible?). This sets up tension with theory.
4. **Theory reconciliation** (dismisses the propagated alternative because it breaks known science).
5. **Pivot to the more reliable variable** (Ecell now gets the same error treatment but with a smaller percentage).
6. **Robustness verdict** (the trend survives the error margin; only magnitude changes).
7. **Forward pointer into supporting data table** (the next sub-point, signalled but cut off).

**Why this order:** the writer must establish the headline claim *before* dismantling parts of it; must quantify error on the worst variable *before* discussing what that error could imply; must derive the worst-case trend *before* rejecting it with theory; and must contrast the unreliable variable with the reliable one *before* issuing a final verdict. Skip any step and the verdict loses its grounding.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / pointing:**
- "On the latter equation, for 𝐾𝑐 against temperature" — directs attention to a specific member of a pair.
- "From the percentage uncertainties in table 11" — source-locator introducing new evidence.

**Cause / consequence:**
- "As a result, the minimum line propagated suggested" — derives worst-case outcome from the quantified error.
- "suggesting that the true value's deviation from the measured value is not unexpected" — paraphrases what a large error margin means causally.

**Contrast / concession:**
- "Though percentage error shows that this could be the case" — concedes that the data permit an alternative trend.
- "there is no scientific theory to corroborate this trend … and in fact it contradicts scientific theory" — hard rejection.

**Specification / quantifier pairing:**
- "extremely high (over 100%)" — bracketed number qualifying an adjective.
- "saw a 13.9% uncertainty in its gradient" — error attributed to a single statistic.

**Evidence-handling verbs:**
- "suggesting that" — tentative interpretation of evidence.
- "propagated suggested" — derived from the data set.
- "shows" / "saw" — declares what the numbers evidence.

**Verdict / robustness:**
- "Regardless of the extent of uncertainty" — sweeping qualification before a claim.
- "seemingly remains the same … it is only … that changes" — restrictive verdict isolating what does and does not vary.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is **paired trend claim → error quantification → worst-case propagation → theoretical rejection → pivot to reliable variable → robustness verdict**. Replicate it with a new idea in six numbered steps:

1. **State the dual trend in one sentence**, naming two related quantities and the shared independent variable, and give both fitted equations with their percentage errors inline. (Establishes the headline and the noise profile of each equation in one move.)
2. **Pick the noisier equation** (signal it with "On the latter equation" or equivalent) and quantify its error — name the percentage and bracket the number next to a qualitative adjective.
3. **Interpret that error as a propagated range** (use "As a result, the minimum/maximum line propagated suggested … could be [opposite]") — show what alternative trend the error band permits.
4. **Reject the alternative against theory** (use a "Though X, there is no Y to corroborate … and in fact it contradicts Y" construction) — anchor the rejection in an established principle the reader accepts.
5. **Pivot to the more reliable variable** ("[Var A] against [condition] saw a [smaller error] in its gradient") — set up the contrast that justifies the verdict.
6. **Issue a robustness verdict** ("Regardless of the extent of uncertainty, the [original direction] seemingly remains the same … it is only [magnitude] that changes") — tell the reader what survives the error analysis and what does not.
