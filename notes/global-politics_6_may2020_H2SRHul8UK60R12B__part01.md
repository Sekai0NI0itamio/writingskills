## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** — *Problem framing + policy + concession*

1. **Evidence/statistic move** — "A recent audit of the plastics industry, done by the federal government, showed that about 90% of all plastics produced in Canada ended up in landfills, the equivalent of 29,000 tonnes, or 2.3 billion plastic water bottles."
   → Hands to S2 by *amplification material* (raw statistic + equivalence conversion), which S2 needs as the launching pad for "Mix this with…"

2. **Causal amplification / compounding move** — "Mix this with the increased production of single-use plastics, and there is a huge environmental concern."
   → Hands to S3 by *cause→response logic*: once the problem is established as "huge," the reader expects a policy response, which S3 delivers.

3. **Context/policy move (with specification)** — "Fortunately, the federal government… has announced that they will go ahead with a national ban (effective as early as 2021) on single-use plastics, which will include cutlery, straws and drink stirrers."
   → Hands to S4 by *tension release followed by contradiction* — "Fortunately" sets the reader up to expect unqualified approval, which S4 undercuts.

4. **Concession + transition move** — "Although this is a great sign for the environment, small businesses and large chains alike have voiced their concerns."
   → Hands to Paragraph 2 by *stakeholder pivot*: because opposition has been flagged, the next paragraph must defend a position against that opposition (hence the personal stance in P2).

**Paragraph 2** — *Personal ethos + verdict + comparative evidence*

1. **Personal claim / stance move** — "Personally, I consider myself to be an environmentalist as I strive to efficiently use the Earth's natural resources."
   → Hands to S2 by *definition→demonstration logic*: "I am X" obligates the writer to show what X looks like.

2. **Evidence/example move (triadic list)** — "Whether it is going paperless, walking and cycling more as a mode of transportation, or turning the heat down, I do whatever is in my power to put less strain on the environment."
   → Hands to S3 by *accumulation→verdict logic*: three examples of personal action license a generalized judgment.

3. **Verdict / opinion move** — "And so, in my opinion, this ban has been long overdue."
   → Hands to S4 by *opinion→support logic*: the verdict is the writer's own, so S4 backs it with external (international) evidence rather than repeating personal examples.

4. **Comparative evidence move** — "Many other countries, even developing countries like Kenya and Bangladesh, have implemented bans similar to this and are already seeing the positive effects."
   → Hands to Paragraph 3 by *external authority → pivot* — having defended the verdict, the writer now signals a shift toward the dissenting stakeholders flagged in P1.

**Paragraph 3** — *Counter-perspective pivot*

1. **Concession / transition move** — "However, it is important to look at the issue from the perspectives of all stakeholders." (followed by the fragment "Even…")
   → Hands off-section by *scope-expansion logic*: after arguing one side, the writer announces a turn toward opposing stakeholders, setting up the analysis that follows.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **argumentative introduction that establishes position before analysis**. The ordered moves are:

1. **Stat hook** — opens with a quantified audit result to anchor the discussion in measurable harm. *Sets up:* amplification.
2. **Amplification / problem intensification** — combines the stat with a related trend to escalate to "huge concern." *Sets up:* a policy response.
3. **Policy announcement (the proposed solution)** — names the actor, the instrument, the date, and the specific items covered. *Sets up:* a concession, because a one-sided celebration would look naive.
4. **Concession / counter-flag** — acknowledges the affected stakeholders' opposition without yet engaging it. *Sets up:* a personal defense of position.
5. **Personal ethos / self-positioning** — declares identity ("environmentalist") to license the verdict. *Sets up:* examples.
6. **Triadic personal examples** — three lifestyle actions that prove the ethos claim is lived, not just claimed. *Sets up:* the verdict.
7. **Verdict ("long overdue")** — the personal position is delivered. *Sets up:* external evidence.
8. **Comparative international evidence** — uses other nations (including "even developing countries") to validate the verdict by analogy. *Sets up:* a pivot.
9. **Pivot to stakeholder analysis** — explicitly broadens scope to "all stakeholders," handing the reader over to the next section.

**Why this order:** stat → amplification → policy → concession → ethos → examples → verdict → international comparison → pivot. Each move earns the next: the stat licenses concern; the concern licenses the ban; the ban invites objection; objection invites defense; defense requires credibility; credibility requires lived examples; examples license a verdict; the verdict needs external validation; validation is complete when the writer pivots to the opposition.

**Replication logic:** another student could use the same skeleton to open on any contested policy (e.g. junk-food advertising bans, e-scooter regulation, AI in classrooms) — open with a quantified harm, pair it with a worsening trend, introduce the official response, flag opposition, declare personal stake, list three lived actions, deliver the verdict, cite two or three international analogues, then pivot to stakeholder cost.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / compounding**
- **"Mix this with"** — "Mix this with the increased production of single-use plastics, and there is a huge environmental concern." → used to fuse two evidence points into one intensified conclusion.

**Cause / consequence**
- **"has announced that they will go ahead with"** — "Fortunately, the federal government… has announced that they will go ahead with a national ban" → presents the policy as a direct response to the stated problem.

**Contrast / concession**
- **"Although this is a great sign for the environment,"** — "Although this is a great sign for the environment, small businesses and large chains alike have voiced their concerns." → concedes merit before pivoting to opposition.
- **"However,"** — "However, it is important to look at the issue from the perspectives of all stakeholders." → signals a full retreat from the just-stated verdict.

**Specification / definition**
- **"which will include cutlery, straws and drink stirrers"** — "national ban… which will include cutlery, straws and drink stirrers" → converts an abstract policy into a concrete list so the reader can picture the ban.
- **"(effective as early as 2021)"** — parenthetical that pins the abstract policy to a date.

**Evidence handling**
- **"A recent audit of the plastics industry, done by the federal government, showed that"** — frames the statistic as third-party, authoritative, and recent.
- **"Many other countries, even developing countries like Kenya and Bangladesh, have implemented bans similar to this and are already seeing the positive effects."** — uses comparative case studies with an "even X" intensifier to strengthen the claim.

**Explanation / stance verbs**
- **"I consider myself to be"** — "Personally, I consider myself to be an environmentalist as I strive to efficiently use the Earth's natural resources." → identity claim with embedded reason.
- **"have voiced their concerns"** — neutral reporting verb that introduces opposition without endorsing it.
- **"has been long overdue"** — verdict phrased as duration claim to dramatise delay.

**Position markers**
- **"Personally,"** and **"in my opinion,"** — bracket the verdict ("Personally… in my opinion, this ban has been long overdue") so the reader sees the claim is owned, not merely reported.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **"evidence-to-position-to-counter-pivot"** pattern. The replication steps are:

1. **Open with a quantified authority source.** Find a recent audit, report, or dataset from a credible body. Convert the figure into a tangible equivalence (tonnes, bottles, hours, dollars) so the reader can visualise it.
2. **Compound the evidence.** Pair the statistic with a second trend that makes the problem worse, and name the resulting harm in an evaluative noun ("huge environmental concern").
3. **Name the official response.** Identify the actor, the policy verb, the effective date, and a concrete list of what is included. This converts abstract policy into something the reader can picture.
4. **Concede the opposite view in one sentence.** Use "Although" + a positive characterisation of the policy + a stakeholder group who objects. This prevents the position from looking naive and licenses the defence that follows.
5. **Declare personal ethos.** Open with "Personally, I consider myself to be…" and tie the identity to a domain verb so it is acted on, not just claimed.
6. **Give three parallel examples of that ethos in practice.** Use "Whether it is A, B, or C" so the reader sees a range, not a single anecdote.
7. **Deliver the verdict.** Use "And so, in my opinion," + a duration or evaluation phrase ("long overdue") to dramatise the position.
8. **Back the verdict with international analogy.** Name 2–3 comparable cases, including one counter-intuitive one introduced by "even" (e.g. "even developing countries"), to show the verdict is not idiosyncratic.
9. **Pivot to the opposition.** Close with "However, it is important to look at the issue from the perspectives of [plural stakeholders]" so the next section can analyse the counter-view already flagged in step 4.

Following these nine steps reproduces the section's logic path on any contested policy topic.
