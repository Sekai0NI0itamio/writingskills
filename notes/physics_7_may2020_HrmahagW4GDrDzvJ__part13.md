## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **evaluation of experimental results** section. The ordered moves are:

1. **Technical clarification of the data display** (units/shift) — first because the reader needs to trust the visual representation before judging the numbers.
2. **Precision claim backed by statistical evidence** (r² values) — sets up a positive evaluation that the concession can then push back against.
3. **Definition of the statistical tool used** — because the metric just cited must be explained before the reader can evaluate it.
4. **Unpacking the metric's scale** (what 1 vs 0 means) — turns the definition into evaluable benchmarks.
5. **Concession pivot** to accuracy — establishes tension: precision is high but accuracy is questioned. This tension is the engine of the section.
6. **Comparison against a known reference value** — supplies concrete evidence for the accuracy concern, itemised across both balls.
7. **Secondary anomaly observation** (gradient comparison + theoretical expectation) — adds a second, independent line of doubt.
8. **Final qualifier** (error bars) — opens the last evaluative angle to complete the picture.

Why this order: the writer moves from *visual integrity* → *statistical strength* → *metric explanation* → *counter-evidence*. Defining the tool AFTER citing it (moves 2→3) works because the reader is already curious about the numbers; defining before would be abstract. The concession (move 5) must come AFTER the positive claim is fully justified, otherwise it has no force.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section relies on the **"metric → define metric → benchmark metric → evaluate data against metric → concede a weakness → prove weakness with external reference"** pattern. Steps to replicate:

1. **Cite the metric from your data.** State the numerical values of a statistical or quantitative measure (e.g. r², percentage error, p-value) for each trial/condition side by side.
2. **Name and define the metric.** Use "The [X] assesses [Y]." — declare what the metric quantifies so the reader can interpret the values just cited.
3. **Benchmark the metric at its extremes.** Give the meaning of the maximum value (ideal outcome), restate in plain terms with "i.e.", then contrast with "whereas" to give the minimum/null meaning.
4. **Render a verdict on the data.** Use "reflecting [judgment]" to convert the metric value into a qualitative claim (e.g. precision, reliability).
5. **Pivot with a concession.** Begin a new sentence with "In spite of" + the just-stated strength, then switch evaluative axis to a new dimension (e.g. precision → accuracy).
6. **Supply external evidence for the weakness.** State the accepted/theoretical reference value, introduce your values with "however", and rank the two using "although [A] is closer … than [B]".
7. **Flag a deeper anomaly with "Interestingly".** Identify a second, independent disagreement between conditions; explain with "which is strange since [theoretical reason], regardless of [the variable]".
8. **Open the final evaluative angle with "Although".** Set up a contrast whose resolution lies in the next section, signalling one more line of evaluation.
