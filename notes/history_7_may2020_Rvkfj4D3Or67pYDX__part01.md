## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Event/question framing** — names the historical event and signals the research question; this sets the scope and directs the reader toward the method.
2. **Method declaration** — states that primary sources from stakeholders will be consulted; this tells the reader *how* the question will be answered.
3. **Source identification (Source 1)** — names the first source and its relevance; this builds the evidentiary foundation.
4. **Source identification (Source 2)** — names the second source and its relevance; this completes the source map in parallel structure.
5. **Value claim for Source A** — asserts that the source's value lies in origin and content; this establishes the evaluative lens.
6. **Credibility context (two layers)** — establishes the author's unique position and connections; this builds the case for trustworthiness before evidence is extracted.
7. **Evidence extraction and unpacking** — quotes the author's rhetorical claim and interprets it; this demonstrates *how* the source supports a historical argument.
8. **Limitation — vested interest** — introduces the first counterweight to credibility; this adds critical depth.
9. **Limitation — published format** — specifies a second, structural limitation; this deepens the critique.
10. **Implication** — states the practical effect of the limitations; this concludes the evaluation with a qualified judgment.

**Generalised sequence for any topic:** Frame event/question → declare method → identify Source 1 with relevance → identify Source 2 with relevance → assert value criterion → establish credibility (origin + connection) → extract and unpack evidence → state limitation 1 → state limitation 2 → state implication. Another student could replicate this with, for example, industrial revolution sources: frame the Luddite movement → declare parliamentary records and worker diaries → evaluate each for origin, content, vested interest, and bias.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing:**
- "The first of these sources is… The second source is…" — identifies sources in order, establishing a parallel structure that guides the reader through each source systematically.

**Cause/consequence:**
- "it is relevant because it was the premier newspaper…" — states relevance with a causal clause explaining *why* the source matters.
- "it is relevant as it is from a memoir authored by…" — states relevance using "as" to link origin to justification.
- "It therefore indicates that…" — draws a consequential conclusion from the stated limitations.

**Contrast/concession:**
- "However, the origin and purpose of this source demonstrate the source's limitations" — pivots from credibility claims to critical counterweight using "However."
- "An additional limitation of the source is in its purpose as a published memoir" — extends the contrast with "An additional" to introduce a second, layered critique.

**Specification:**
- "within the passage, Liliuokalani posits that…" — narrows the scope to the specific text being analysed.
- "in its purpose as a published memoir" — specifies the exact dimension of the limitation.

**Evidence handling:**
- "Liliuokalani posits that the reasoning behind American expansion into Hawaii was based on nationalistic principles" — attributes a claim to the source and unpacks its meaning.
- "this statement furthers the claim that Hawaiian annexation was done for nationalistic reasons" — interprets the evidential weight of the quoted statement.

**Explanation verbs:**
- "bolster the credibility of the extract's content" — explains how personal circumstances function as credibility support.
- "demonstrate the source's limitations" — explains how origin and purpose function as critical evidence.
- "would incentivise her to portray the American government negatively" — explains the behavioural consequence of a vested interest.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern name:** Authority establishment → evidence extraction → critical qualification (a two-phase evaluation pattern).

**Step-by-step instructions to explain a new idea with this pattern:**

1. **State the evaluative value claim** — open by declaring what criterion makes the source or idea valuable (e.g., "the value of X lies in its origin and content").
2. **Establish credibility context, layer 1** — describe the unique personal position of the author/subject that grants them knowledge (e.g., "As the only person who witnessed…, she had extensive knowledge on…").
3. **Establish credibility context, layer 2** — describe a second factor (connections, role, access) that compounds the credibility case (e.g., "As [role], she also had close connections with [institution]").
4. **Transition with a credibility bridge sentence** — use a sentence that links the credibility contexts to the evidential content (e.g., "These circumstances bolster the credibility of the content").
5. **Extract and unpack the evidence** — quote or paraphrase the author's specific claim, then interpret its historical implication (e.g., "the author posits that…; this furthers the claim that…").
6. **Introduce the first limitation with contrast** — pivot using "However" and state a vested interest or bias that undermines full credibility (e.g., "the author had a vested interest in…, and this would incentivise…").
7. **Specify a second, structural limitation** — identify a limitation inherent in the source's format or purpose (e.g., "An additional limitation is in its purpose as a…").
8. **State the implication** — conclude with a consequence clause showing the practical effect of the limitations on the source's reliability (e.g., "It therefore indicates that… the agenda could have coloured…").
