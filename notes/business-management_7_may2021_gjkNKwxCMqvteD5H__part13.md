## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **business/tech-news analysis paragraph block** (running portion of an article), so the sequence is:

1. **Setup of the strategic action** — what the company did (here: porting designs). Sets up the *why does this matter* frame.
2. **Authority quote on competitive position** — an analyst's verdict comparing the new vs. old situation. Justifies the action as worthwhile.
3. **Risk/benefit balance** — concedes downsides (capacity strain), then concedes the upside (engineering simplification). This rhythm is what gives analytical journalism its evenhanded tone.
4. **Second authority quote on underlying mechanism** — explains the *root cause* behind the decision (here: Globalfoundries' cost problem). Deepens the analysis from "what happened" to "why it had to happen."
5. **Pivot to adjacent news** — shifts register to signal the article continues elsewhere.

The order matters: action → endorsement → trade-off → root cause → new item. A reader needs the action on the table before the endorsement makes sense; the endorsement makes the trade-off legible; the trade-off makes the root-cause quote feel earned (you're now sophisticated enough to receive the deeper "why"); only then do you exit. To replicate: never lead with the analytical quote — earn it with the move first.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Strategic action context**
SKELETON: "[Company] went to the trouble to make sure that [its products] could be [operate/made] in [new partner]'s [facilities/platform] as [easily/cleanly] as they could be [made/run] in [old partner]'s [facilities/platform]."

- *Slot 1 (Company)*: noun phrase naming the firm.
- *Slot 2 (its products)*: plural noun phrase, the thing being designed/produced.
- *Slot 3 (operate/made)*: past-participle verb of compatibility.
- *Slot 4 (new partner)*: proper noun, the partner being adopted.
- *Slot 5 (facilities/platform)*: infrastructure noun.
- *Slot 6 (made/run)*: verb that names the old method.
- *Slot 7 (old partner)*: proper noun, prior partner.

**How to fill with a different idea:** Pick a firm that recently migrated between two providers (cloud, supplier, foundry, OS). Name the artifact in slot 2, the destination in slot 4, and the legacy in slot 7. Use past participle for the compatibility verb.

*Original fill:* "AMD… went to the trouble to make sure that its processor designs could be manufactured in TSMC's factories as easily as they could be built in Globalfoundries' factories."

*Demo fill (different idea):* "Spotify went to the trouble to make sure that its recommendation engine could be deployed on Google Cloud's infrastructure as reliably as it could be hosted on Amazon's infrastructure."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / framing transitions**
- "In other news" — opens the personnel-news pivot, *"In other news, the head of AMD's computing and graphics division"* — signals a topic shift within running prose.

**Concession / contrast pivots**
- "But the switch" — concessive counterweight after risk sentence, *"But the switch will likely simplify AMD's engineering work"* — flips from downside to upside.
- "But Gwennap didn't see" — concessive reservation that interrupts the personnel fact, *"But Gwennap didn't see the latest"* — softens an unconfirmed implication.

**Cause / consequence markers**
- "as TSMC already makes" — causal connective explaining WHY the move simplifies, *"as TSMC already makes AMD's Radeon graphics chips"* — gives the enabling prior fact.
- "had to make a call" — modal-of-necessity framing, *"AMD had to make a call to keep pace"* — forces the strategic move.

**Specification / equivalence markers**
- "in the same class as" — equivalence comparator, *"TSMC 7-nanometer to be in the same class as Intel 10-nanometer"* — equates specs across vendors.
- "and thus" — inferential connector, *"and thus better than Intel 14-nanometer"* — derives the ranking from the class claim.

**Authority / evidence handling**
- "said Gwennap" — attribution tag after block quote.
- "said Kevin Krewell, analyst at Tirias Research" — attribution tag with credentials.
- "I'd guess" — speculative authority hedge inside the quote, *"I'd guess Globalfoundries just couldn't scale"* — signals analyst inference not hard fact.

**Explanation / mechanism verbs**
- "will have to scramble to ensure" — mechanism verb of strained capacity, *"TSMC will have to scramble to ensure enough capacity"* — names the operational pressure.
- "simplify AMD's engineering work" — outcome verb, *"the switch will likely simplify AMD's engineering work"* — names the strategic benefit.
- "couldn't scale big enough to pay for" — failure-mechanism verb phrase, *"Globalfoundries just couldn't scale big enough to pay for"* — names the cost constraint.

**Rhetorical verdict**
- "Who would have thought?" — editorial reaction phrase, *"Who would have thought?' said Gwennap"* — closes the authority quote with surprise.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is: **ACTION → AUTHORITY ENDORSEMENT → TRADEOFF → ROOT-CAUSE QUOTE → PIVOT**.

Steps to replicate with a new idea:

1. **Open with the strategic action** as a setup sentence. State plainly what the company did and the dual-context complication (e.g., making it work in two places). This grounds the reader.
2. **Insert an authority quote that compares** the new situation to a legacy competitor, using "in the same class as" and "and thus better/worse than," ending with a rhetorical question to dramatize the shift. This gives the action credibility.
3. **Write a three-sentence risk/benefit paragraph**: sentence 1 names a risk ("there is some risk in"), sentence 2 explains the mechanism of that risk (capacity/scale strain), sentence 3 pivots with "But" to the upside and supports it with "as [partner] already does [adjacent work]."
4. **Insert a second authority quote that explains the root cause** — use "had to make a call to" + "I'd guess [actor] just couldn't [verb] big enough to [cost mechanism] — possibly even with [Company] as a customer." This converts the analysis from "what" to "why."
5. **Pivot with "In other news"** to an adjacent item (executive move, product launch, earnings note). End mid-thought to suggest more to come.

The logic engine: each move earns the next. The action creates the question "is it worth it?" The first quote answers "yes." The trade-off tempers that answer with realism. The second quote explains the deeper structural reason. The pivot releases the reader from the analytical frame. Replicating the rhythm means never front-loading the deepest analysis — build to it.
