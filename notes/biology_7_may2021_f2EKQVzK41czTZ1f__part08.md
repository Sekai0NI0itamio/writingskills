## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Geographic spread of the pest.** Sets up the reader to believe the threat is wide-ranging, so the next beat (biology) is consequential.
2. **Biology of the pest (morphometric + reproductive figures).** Why second: only after the reader believes the threat is broad does "why it persists" matter; numerical specs give the mechanism teeth.
3. **Feeding mechanism + visible damage.** Why third: with identity and persistence established, the reader is ready for the *consequence* of the pest on the plant.
4. **Origin hypothesis.** Why fourth: shifts from biology-on-plant to biology-of-population, the next investigative step once damage is shown.
5. **Impact on the experiment's data reliability.** Why fifth: reframes the pest from a botanical curiosity into a methodological threat.
6. **Attempted remedy + reason for abandonment.** Why sixth: shows the student's initiative was constrained, which earns honesty points and motivates the uncertainties section.
7. **Uncertainties header.** Why seventh: the abandoned control leaves methodological gaps, which the uncertainties section will now enumerate.

The unifying logic: **identity → mechanism → damage → source → impact on data → failed mitigation → flagged limitations.** Each move hands the reader a question that the next move answers.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / continuation:** none present in this fragment — the moves are driven by cause and consequence, not by ordinal markers.

**Cause / consequence:** "due to their small size" (causal attribution); "causing the deformities shown in fig. 7" (present-participle result clause); "was abandoned due to a low availability of the extract" (causal blocker); "affecting the reliability of the data measured" (consequence attached to impact claim).

**Contrast / concession:** "but was abandoned due to" — single coordinating conjunction used to flip from attempted remedy to its abandonment.

**Specification / evidence handling:** bracketed numerical evidence "(0.2 to 0.3 mm) short life span (13 days producing 30- 70 offspring)" — figures used inline to substantiate the abstract claim "easily spreadable and persistent"; cross-reference to figure "shown in fig. 7."

**Explanation verbs (defining / attributing cause):** "are easily spreadable and persistent due to" (adjectival properties tied to mechanism); "consume soft tissue motley found at" (mechanistic action verb).

**Hedging / probability markers:** "probably originated from" — modal hedge marking an origin claim that is inferred, not proven; characteristic IB move of calibrated certainty.

**Impact / verdict intensifiers:** "completely devastating" — adverb + adjective pair used to escalate a verdict in one beat.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **identity → mechanism → consequence on host → origin → consequence on experiment → failed remediation → flagged limitation**. Replicate with a different idea as follows:

1. **Open with the agent's reach.** State in present tense the geographic/agricultural/human contexts where the phenomenon occurs. This justifies why it matters.
2. **Name two biological or structural properties of the agent.** Join them with "and" and attribute them with "due to" so the reader sees cause attached to property, not a bare adjective.
3. **Cite numeric evidence inline, in parentheses.** Insert at least three figures (size, lifespan, output) so the cause in step 2 has a mechanism behind it.
4. **State the mechanistic action on the host.** Use a verb of consumption/interaction ("consume," "pierce," "colonise") and a body-part location ("soft tissue … at the lower parts").
5. **Attach the visible result with "causing."** Use a present-participle clause and cross-reference a figure ("causing the deformities shown in fig. X").
6. **Hypothesise origin with a probability marker.** Use "probably originated from" + a plausible vector (another crop, imported stock, a neighbouring setup). This signals calibrated certainty.
7. **Deliver the impact verdict on the experiment.** Use an extreme adjective ("devastating") and target a specific measured quantity ("reliability of the data measured") so the consequence is operational, not vague.
8. **Describe the abandoned remedy.** Use the template "An attempt to combat X with the use of [treatment] was thought of, but was abandoned due to [practical blocker]." This shows initiative that was constrained, not laziness.
9. **Open the uncertainties block with a labelled noun-phrase list.** Format: "Uncertainties: uncertainties in [measured quantity] as well as flaws in [method step]" so the reader knows the bullets that follow are categorised.
10. **Maintain one idea per sentence and let each sentence's tail (its last clause) set up the next sentence's head.** This is the load-bearing rule: the cause clause at the end of sentence N is the question sentence N+1 answers.
