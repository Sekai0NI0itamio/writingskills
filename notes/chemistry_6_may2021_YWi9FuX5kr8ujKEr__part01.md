## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This section is the **investigation rationale / origin-of-research** section. The ordered content sequence is:

1. **Personal entry hook** — a generic statement that connects the writer's interest to the chosen knowledge area. *Why first:* it frames the writer as personally invested, satisfying IB expectations of authenticity.
2. **Specific classroom trigger** — narrows down to a precise lecture/method studied. *Why second:* it converts vague interest into a concrete academic source.
3. **Personal/local curiosity arising from the trigger** — a real-world question the method could answer. *Why third:* it bridges classroom theory to a personal observation, justifying the investigation's relevance.
4. **Choice of investigation (primary variable)** — declares what will actually be measured. *Why fourth:* it answers the curiosity raised in sentence 3 with a measurable plan.
5. **Choice of investigation (secondary variable)** — adds a second dependent/independent variable. *Why fifth:* it broadens scope and shows critical thinking beyond a single measurement.
6. **Cited scientific fact that links to the local context** — a literature value with citation. *Why sixth:* it supplies the quantitative threshold needed to convert observation into a testable prediction.
7. **Observation + hypothesised value** — applies the cited threshold to the observed site and states a numerical hypothesis. *Why last:* this is the logical payoff; every prior move was needed to justify it.

Generalised sequence another student could replicate: **interest → classroom moment → real-world question → primary aim → extended aim → cited scientific fact → observation-driven hypothesis**.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section uses an **interest → academic trigger → personal question → measurable aim → cited quantitative threshold → observation-driven numerical hypothesis** pattern. To explain a NEW idea with the same pattern:

1. **Open with personal investment** in one trailing clause: phrase a general interest in the broad knowledge area so the topic feels chosen, not assigned.
2. **Pin a specific academic moment** — name the lesson, lab, or reading and the exact technique/principle studied.
3. **Convert that moment into a real-world question** about a personally familiar place or situation.
4. **State the primary aim** as a measurable quantity taken from that specific place/sample, and label it with a figure reference.
5. **Extend the aim** with "as well as investigate how, if at all, [variable A] would affect [variable B]." This signals scope and critical thinking.
6. **Insert a cited quantitative fact** with a full in-text citation and page range that provides a numerical threshold relevant to the phenomenon.
7. **Bridge to a local observation** using "hence" — refer to photographic or figure evidence that matches the cited fact.
8. **Close with a hedged numerical hypothesis** using "has made me hypothesise that [quantity] must be [comparison to threshold]" so the claim is testable and tied back to both the citation and the observation.
