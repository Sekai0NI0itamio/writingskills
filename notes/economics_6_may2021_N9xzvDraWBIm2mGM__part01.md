## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **reaction-and-impact section**. The ordered content moves are:

1. **Residual uncertainty about the event** — establishes that the threat is open-ended, framing the reaction as proportionate.
2. **Identification of the first responder + emotional label** — names a named official and tags the reaction with a short quoted feeling-word.
3. **Categorical denial of the alleged wrongdoing** — the official's verbatim refusal of the accusation.
4. **Positive-reputation justification** — popularity/quality evidence that underpins the denial.
5. **Switch to a second authority** — a trade minister introduces the economic consequence frame.
6. **Human-scale emotional close** — the producers' effort and distress personalised the impact.

*Why this order:* uncertainty (1) sets a question that reaction (2) answers; the reaction's tone (2) is unpacked into the denial (3); a denial alone is hollow, so justification follows (4); once the moral case is made, the section pivots to the material cost (5); and that material cost is humanised (6) so the reader feels the consequence on real people. A student replicating this on a different controversy (e.g., a fishery ban, a tech export restriction) should preserve **ambiguity → emotional header → denial → justification → second-source economic verdict → human consequence**.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Authority denial with reputation justification**

> SKELETON: "[Authority figure] categorically rejects any allegation that [subject group] are [alleged wrongdoing]," [he/she] said. "[Subject] is [positive attribute] both in [location A] and across the globe due to [reason], and we are confident that a full and thorough investigation will confirm this."

- *Slot 1 — Authority figure:* proper noun + political title; "the agriculture minister".
- *Slot 2 — Subject group:* noun phrase naming those accused; "our wine producers".
- *Slot 3 — Alleged wrongdoing:* gerund/verb of the contested behaviour; "dumping product".
- *Slot 4 — Positive attribute:* adjectival reputation claim; "hugely popular".
- *Slot 5 — Locations:* two places (domestic + foreign); "in China and across the globe".
- *Slot 6 — Reason:* causal phrase explaining the attribute; "due to its high quality".
- *Original filled:* "The Australian government categorically rejects any allegation that our wine producers are dumping product into China," he said. "Australian wine is hugely popular both in China and across the globe due to its high quality and we are confident that a full and thorough investigation will confirm this."
- *Demo fill (different idea — pharmaceutical industry):* "The Indian government categorically rejects any allegation that our generic-drug manufacturers are copying patented formulas," she said. "Indian pharmaceuticals are widely trusted both at home and across the globe due to their rigorous testing standards, and we are confident that a full and thorough audit will confirm this."

**Skeleton B — Second-source economic impact + human consequence**

> SKELETON: "[Country]'s trade minister [Name] said the new [measures] make [product] unviable and unmarketable in [market]. "This is a very distressing time for many hundreds of [industry actors], who have built, in good faith, a [adjective] market in [market]," [he/she] said.

- *Slot 1 — Minister + name:* proper noun + title.
- *Slot 2 — New measures:* plural noun for the policy instrument; "tariffs".
- *Slot 3 — Product/industry:* affected export; "Australian wine".
- *Slot 4 — Market country:* proper noun; "China".
- *Slot 5 — Quantity of actors:* numeric phrase; "many hundreds of".
- *Slot 6 — Industry actors:* noun for the workers/firms; "wine producers".
- *Slot 7 — Adjective on market:* describes quality of the market built; "sound".
- *Original filled:* "Australia's trade minister Simon Birmingham said the new tariffs make Australian wine unviable and unmarketable in China. 'This is a very distressing time for many hundreds of Australian wine producers, who have built, in good faith, a sound market in China,' he said."
- *Demo fill (different idea — lumber exports):* "Canada's trade minister Mary Ng said the new tariffs make Canadian lumber unviable and unmarketable in the United States. 'This is a very distressing time for many hundreds of Canadian lumber workers, who have built, in good faith, a reliable market in the United States,' she said."

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is **EVENT-RESIDUE → AUTHORITY-DENIAL → JUSTIFICATION → ECONOMIC IMPACT → HUMAN CONSEQUENCE** (an *event → reaction → rebuttal → evidence → impact → human close* chain).

1. **Open with the unresolved edge of the event.** State what the imposing party did *not* clarify (e.g., "The ministry did not specify how long the measures would last for.") — this seeds the sense of an open-ended threat.
2. **Name the responding official + medium + emotional label.** Identify the officeholder, the channel of response, and a short emotional headline word in single quotes (e.g., "'Extremely disappointed'").
3. **Expand the label into a categorical denial.** Have that official reject the specific allegation in direct speech using the verb *rejects* paired with an intensifier like *categorically*.
4. **Justify the denial with a positive reputation + vindication clause.** Cite cross-border popularity, quality, and the expectation that "a full and thorough investigation will confirm this."
5. **Pivot to a second authority on economic impact.** Introduce the trade minister and assert that the measures render the product "unviable and unmarketable" in the named market.
6. **Close with a human-consequence quote.** Use "This is a very distressing time" + the producers' "good faith" effort + an evaluative adjective on the market ("sound") — the section ends on an emotional, earned-effort note.
