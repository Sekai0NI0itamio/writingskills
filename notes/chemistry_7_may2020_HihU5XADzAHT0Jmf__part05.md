## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (safety and disposal for chemicals and equipment):

1. **Sentence 1 (fragment):** *"the experiment site."* — Context marker; **CONTINUATION** carry-over from a prior sentence about where work occurred. Hands the reader to S2 by **anchoring location** so the disposal action that follows is rooted somewhere physical.
2. **Sentence 2:** *"small amounts of ethyl ethanoate were flushed down a sink, but with"* — **Procedure with qualifier**; states what was done to the chemical, then "but" introduces a **restraining condition** (large water volume). Hands to S3 by **naming the chemical and the disposal act**, which triggers the need for a hazard-based justification.
3. **Sentence 3:** *"Due to its flammability, care was needed"* — **Cause-based precaution**; "Due to its flammability" supplies the **hazard property** that retroactively legitimises the "but with a large quantity of water" qualifier in S2. Hands to S4 by **shifting chemical** — the paragraph has finished justifying ethyl ethanoate and now pivots to a new substance.
4. **Sentence 4:** *"Sodium hydroxide is corrosive, so care was needed"* — **Hazard → precaution chain**; mirrors the structure of S3 ("Due to…care was needed" / "is corrosive, so care was needed"), establishing a **parallel template**. Hands to S5 by **promising a concrete protective action** that the "care" must cash out.
5. **Sentence 5:** *"As such, safety glasses and gloves had to be worn"* — **Verdict / instantiation of precaution**; "As such" is the **consequence link** that converts abstract "care" into named PPE. Hands to S6 by **completing the chemical-hazard block** and signalling an addition ("Furthermore").
6. **Sentence 6:** *"Furthermore, some of the temperature intervals were very hot, e.g. 60℃, therefore it was"* — **New hazard with numeric example + consequence**; "Furthermore" **adds a third hazard category** (thermal, not chemical), "e.g. 60℃" anchors it with a **specific value**, and "therefore it was important not to directly touch the test tubes" closes with a **behavioural verdict**. Hands to the next paragraph by **exhausting hazard categories**, so what remains is the ethical wrap-up.

**Paragraph 2** (ethical and disposal closure):

1. **Sentence 1:** *"There were no notable ethical or environmental issues"* — **Absence claim**; asserts that the hazard discussion above did not generate ethical/environmental problems. Hands to S2 by **promising an example** that demonstrates the absence is not an oversight.
2. **Sentence 2:** *"All remaining chemicals from the experiment were returned to the technician rather than being disposed of"* — **Evidence via contrast**; "rather than" sets up **proper action vs. improper alternative**, supplying the concrete behaviour that backs the absence claim in S1. Hands to the next section (Results) by **closing the safety discussion** so the reader is cleared to move to data.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A **safety-and-disposal sub-section of a procedure write-up** makes moves in this fixed order:

1. **Disposal protocol for the most hazardous reagent** — sets the safety tone by tackling the riskiest substance first.
2. **Property-based justification of that disposal choice** — converts the procedure into reasoned action (property → precaution logic).
3. **Introduction of the next reagent with its hazard property** — moves sequentially through each chemical used.
4. **Property-to-precaution chain** — repeats the same "X is Y, so Z" structure so the reader recognises a pattern.
5. **Concrete PPE verdict** — the abstract "care" is finally named (glasses, gloves, etc.).
6. **Non-chemical hazards** — adds thermal/physical risks the chemicals alone do not cover, with a numeric anchor.
7. **Ethical/environmental absence claim** — signals to the examiner that broader impacts have been checked.
8. **Evidence of responsible practice** — demonstrates the absence claim is real, not glossed over.
9. **Hand-off to Results** — the section is closed so data can begin.

**Why this order:** each chemical hazard sets up the next by exhausting one category before moving on; the PPE verdict only makes sense after at least one hazard has been stated; the ethical line only lands *after* all hazards are listed (otherwise it would feel premature); and the hand-off exists to **release the reader** into the data tables that follow.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — Chemical Disposal with Qualifier
**SKELETON:** *"[Chemical] is [hazard adjective], therefore [disposal action], but with [restraining condition]."*

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: a specific chemical name (noun phrase).
   - Slot 2: a hazard adjective — grammatically an adjective or "highly + adjective" (e.g., flammable, toxic, reactive).
   - Slot 3: a passive disposal verb phrase in past tense (e.g., "were flushed down a sink").
   - Slot 4: a safety-restraining condition (a quantity, dilution, or distance phrase).
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** pick the riskiest reagent in your experiment; state its hazard in one adjective; describe how you actually disposed of it; then add the qualifier that made the disposal safe.
3. **Original filled version:** *"When disposing of it, small amounts of ethyl ethanoate were flushed down a sink, but with a large quantity of water."* (with "Due to its flammability" supplying the hazard adjective in the prior sentence).
4. **Demonstration fill:** *"When disposing of it, residues of bromine water were poured into a labelled waste beaker, but with a loosely fitting lid to prevent vapour escape."*

### Skeleton B — Hazard-to-Care-Verdict Chain
**SKELETON:** *"[Reagent] is [property], so care was needed in order to prevent [harm]."*

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: a chemical or apparatus name.
   - Slot 2: a hazard property as a predicative adjective ("corrosive", "irritant", "sharp").
   - Slot 3: a body-part or contact target ("eye or skin contact", "inhalation", "cuts").
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** list each remaining chemical in your experiment in order; for each, name the harm it actually poses (not a generic one); name the body/contact target that the lab manual specifies.
3. **Original filled version:** *"Sodium hydroxide is corrosive, so care was needed in order to prevent eye or skin contact."*
4. **Demonstration fill:** *"Concentrated hydrochloric acid is irritant, so care was needed in order to prevent inhalation of fumes."*

### Skeleton C — PPE / Behavioural Verdict
**SKELETON:** *"As such, [specific equipment or action] had to be [used/carried out] at all times."*

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: a connective of consequence ("As such", "Hence", "For this reason").
   - Slot 2: named PPE or a single behavioural rule (plural noun + optional modifier).
   - Slot 3: a modal of obligation in past tense ("had to be worn", "had to be performed").
   - Slot 4: a frequency/condition phrase ("at all times", "before each trial").
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** after at least one hazard chain, name the PPE your lab actually required and the duration rule attached to it; "at all times" is the safest filler if no duration was specified.
3. **Original filled version:** *"As such, safety glasses and gloves had to be worn at all times."*
4. **Demonstration fill:** *"As such, a fume cupboard had to be used whenever volatile solvents were open."*

### Skeleton D — Non-chemical Hazard with Numeric Anchor
**SKELETON:** *"Furthermore, [category of risk] were [adjective], e.g. [specific value], therefore [precaution]."*

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: additive connective ("Furthermore", "In addition").
   - Slot 2: a measurable risk category ("temperature intervals", "voltages", "pressures").
   - Slot 3: an intensifying adjective ("very hot", "extremely high").
   - Slot 4: a concrete numeric example with units ("60℃", "250 V").
   - Slot 5: a behavioural consequence ("it was important not to [verb] the [object]").
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** identify the non-chemical extreme in your procedure (heat, voltage, pressure, sharp edges), find its maximum value from your method, and write the avoidance behaviour that maximum demands.
3. **Original filled version:** *"Furthermore, some of the temperature intervals were very hot, e.g. 60℃, therefore it was important not to directly touch the test tubes in the water bath."*
4. **Demonstration fill:** *"Furthermore, some of the power-supply readings were very high, e.g. 12 V, therefore it was important not to touch the terminals while the circuit was live."*

### Skeleton E — Absence Claim with Contrastive Evidence
**SKELETON:** *"There were no notable [ethical/environmental] issues. [Responsible action performed] rather than [improper alternative]."*

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: absence claim with "no notable" + a category the examiner expects you to address.
   - Slot 2: a concrete responsible action in past tense.
   - Slot 3: the contrasting improper action introduced by "rather than".
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** only use this skeleton if you genuinely had no significant ethical/environmental concerns; pair it with one observable behaviour that demonstrates responsibility, and explicitly name the alternative you avoided.
3. **Original filled version:** *"There were no notable ethical or environmental issues. All remaining chemicals from the experiment were returned to the technician rather than being disposed of."*
4. **Demonstration fill:** *"There were no notable ethical issues. All animal specimens were returned to the laboratory store rather than being discarded after use."*

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / addition:**
- *"Furthermore, some of the temperature intervals"* — adds a new hazard category after the chemical hazards.

**Cause / consequence:**
- *"Due to its flammability, care was needed"* — property → precaution cause chain.
- *"Sodium hydroxide is corrosive, so care was needed"* — embedded cause-effect with "so".
- *"As such, safety glasses and gloves had to be worn"* — closes a cause chain with a named consequence.
- *"therefore it was important not to directly touch"* — numeric example → behavioural consequence.

**Purpose:**
- *"in order to prevent eye or skin contact"* — names the *target* of the precaution, not just the precaution itself.

**Contrast / qualifier:**
- *"small amounts… but with a large quantity of water"* — "but" restrains a risky action with a safety condition.
- *"returned to the technician rather than being disposed of"* — "rather than" sets proper vs. improper disposal.

**Temporal / conditional:**
- *"When disposing of it, small amounts…"* — frames the disposal action in a temporal-conditional clause.

**Evidence handling:**
- *"e.g. 60℃"* — numeric anchor proving the abstract "very hot" claim.

**Absence / scope:**
- *"There were no notable ethical or environmental issues"* — explicit denial-claim pattern used to pre-empt examiner concerns.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section does **not** explain a scientific concept; it explains a **safety rationale**. The pattern is **hazard-identification → property-based justification → named precaution**, replicated once per risk. To apply the same pattern to any new lab safety write-up:

1. **List every distinct risk source in your experiment** (chemicals first, then apparatus/conditions, then broader ethical/environmental scope).
2. **For the riskiest chemical, open with the disposal action you actually performed**, then immediately restrain it with a safety condition introduced by "but" or "with".
3. **Follow the disposal sentence with a "Due to / is…so" sentence** that names the chemical's hazard property and links it to a *specific* harm you were preventing — not a generic one.
4. **Move to the next chemical using the same "X is Y, so care was needed to prevent Z" template** so the reader recognises the structure.
5. **Close each chemical block with an "As such, [PPE/action] had to be [verb] at all times" verdict** that converts the abstract "care" into something the examiner can tick off.
6. **Pivot to non-chemical hazards with "Furthermore"**, anchor the claim with a numeric example ("e.g. 60℃"), and write a behavioural consequence ("therefore it was important not to…").
7. **Add an absence-claim sentence** ("There were no notable ethical or environmental issues") only if true, then back it with **one** observable responsible action introduced by "rather than".
8. **End the section so the reader can move into Results** — the safety discussion must not bleed into the data.
