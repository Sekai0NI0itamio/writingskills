## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**
1. **Claim (mechanism):** "lower interest rates fuel house price inflation through a sharp pick-up in credit." — States the causal chain linking rates to prices via credit. *Hand-off:* the mechanism raises the question of whether this is actually happening, which the next sentence answers.
2. **Evidence (data):** "Housing lending over the four months to September has grown at an annualised pace of 52 per cent, while property prices in Melbourne and Sydney are growing at an annualised 20 per cent." — Supplies two quantified metrics confirming the mechanism is active in specific cities. *Hand-off:* the concrete domestic evidence transitions naturally to the authority's response, which the next paragraph provides.

**Paragraph 2**
1. **Context (authority citation):** "Treasurer Josh Frydenberg said the OECD's assessment highlighted global risks to the Australian economy." — Frames the OECD as the relevant authority and positions the concern as global, not just domestic. *Hand-off:* the mention of the OECD's assessment invites the specific evidence the next sentence supplies.
2. **Evidence (direct quote):** "We do face headwinds, with the OECD in its November Economic Outlook pointing out that the global outlook is 'fragile' with 'trade policy tensions since May taking an increasing toll on confidence and investment,' Mr Frydenberg said." — Unpacks the authority's diagnosis with direct language and a temporal cause (trade tensions since May). *Hand-off:* the "fragile" outlook and "toll on confidence and investment" set up the quantified GDP consequence in the next paragraph.

**Paragraph 3**
1. **Specification (quantified outcome):** "Globally GDP growth has now been downgraded to 2.9 per cent for this year and projected to remain around 3 per cent for 2020-21." — Converts the qualitative "fragile" assessment into precise figures and a forward projection. *Hand-off:* the downgrade demands a causal explanation, which the next paragraph provides.

**Paragraph 4**
1. **Cause (attribution):** "The OECD blamed 'persistent high policy uncertainty and weak trade and investment' for its downgrade" — Identifies the specific causes behind the GDP downgrade. *Hand-off:* having named the causes, the sentence moves to the prescribed remedy.
2. **Implication (prescription with contrast):** "and said fiscal policy was needed for 'unaddressed structural changes more than any cyclical shock'." — Prescribes fiscal policy and contrasts structural needs against cyclical fixes. *Hand-off:* the call for fiscal action leads into the broader call for bold action in the next paragraph.

**Paragraph 5**
1. **Claim (urgent prescription):** "'There is scope and an urgent need for much bolder policy action to revive growth,' the OECD said." — Escalates from fiscal policy specifically to bolder action generally. *Hand-off:* the claim of "scope" and "urgent need" invites the conditional mechanism showing how that action would work.
2. **Mechanism (conditional recommendation):** "'Should the outlook deteriorate more than we project, co-ordinated fiscal and monetary action across the G20, even allowing for the limited policy space some central banks have, could efficiently avert a recession.'" — Specifies the condition (deterioration), the actors (G20), the tools (coordinated fiscal and monetary), and the constraint (limited policy space). *Hand-off:* the conditional averting of recession sets up the final warning against complacency.

**Paragraph 6**
1. **Verdict (cautionary claim):** "'The current stabilisation at low levels of economic growth, inflation and interest rates does not warrant policy complacency.'" — Delivers a negative verdict: stability is not safety. *Hand-off:* rejecting complacency opens the door to the positive action the next sentence proposes.
2. **Transition + Example (concrete proposal):** "It has also backed the idea of creating new government investment vehicles. 'The creation of national investment funds, focused on..." — Shifts from warning to a specific institutional remedy, naming national investment funds as the vehicle.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **State a domestic causal mechanism with supporting data** — establishes the phenomenon (rates → credit → prices) so the reader has a concrete starting concern.
2. **Introduce an authority's framing of global risk** — elevates the concern from domestic to international by citing the OECD through a government spokesperson.
3. **Quote the authority's specific evidence** — unpacks the authority's diagnosis in their own words, giving the claim credibility and temporal specificity.
4. **Quantify the global outcome** — converts the qualitative risk into hard GDP figures, making the threat measurable and time-bound.
5. **Attribute the causes behind the outcome** — identifies what drove the downgrade (uncertainty, weak trade/investment), completing the cause chain.
6. **Prescribe a remedy with a contrastive justification** — names fiscal policy and contrasts structural need against cyclical inadequacy, sharpening the recommendation.
7. **Escalate to a bolder, coordinated prescription with a conditional mechanism** — broadens the remedy to G20-level coordinated action and specifies the condition under which it would activate.
8. **Deliver a cautionary verdict against complacency** — warns that current stability is deceptive, creating urgency for the final move.
9. **Propose a concrete institutional solution** — names national investment funds as the specific vehicle, ending on an actionable note.

**Why this order:** each move supplies the reader with what the next move needs — the mechanism raises the question of evidence, the evidence invites an authority's framing, the framing leads to quantified scope, the scope demands a cause, the cause justifies a prescription, the prescription needs a conditional mechanism, and the conditional mechanism earns a caution that opens onto a concrete proposal. A student replicating this sequence with any topic would follow: phenomenon + data → authority framing → quoted evidence → quantified impact → causal diagnosis → specific remedy → broader/conditional remedy → caution → concrete proposal.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing:**
- "over the four months to September" — temporal marker specifying the evidence window (P1).
- "since May" — temporal marker anchoring the cause in the quoted evidence (P2).
- "for this year and projected to remain… for 2020-21" — temporal specification of the quantified outcome (P3).

**Cause / Consequence:**
- "fuel" — causal verb linking rates to prices via credit (P1): "lower interest rates **fuel** house price inflation."
- "taking an increasing toll on" — consequence phrase showing escalating damage (P2): "'trade policy tensions since May **taking an increasing toll on** confidence and investment.'"
- "blamed… for its downgrade" — direct attribution of cause to effect (P4): "The OECD **blamed** 'persistent high policy uncertainty and weak trade and investment' **for its downgrade**."

**Contrast / Concession:**
- "more than any cyclical shock" — contrastive justification prioritising structural over cyclical (P4): "fiscal policy was needed for 'unaddressed structural changes **more than any cyclical shock**.'"
- "even allowing for the limited policy space some central banks have" — concession acknowledging a constraint before asserting the recommendation (P5): "co-ordinated fiscal and monetary action across the G20, **even allowing for the limited policy space** some central banks have."

**Specification:**
- "in Melbourne and Sydney" — geographic specification of the price data (P1): "property prices **in Melbourne and Sydney** are growing."
- "across the G20" — scope specification of the coordinated action (P5): "co-ordinated fiscal and monetary action **across the G20**."

**Evidence handling:**
- "Treasurer Josh Frydenberg said the OECD's assessment highlighted" — authority-mediated evidence introduction (P2): "**Treasurer Josh Frydenberg said the OECD's assessment highlighted** global risks."
- "the OECD blamed" — direct institutional evidence attribution (P4): "**The OECD blamed** 'persistent high policy uncertainty.'"
- "the OECD said" — direct institutional claim attribution (P5): "**the OECD said** 'There is scope and an urgent need.'"

**Explanation / Judgment verbs:**
- "does not warrant" — evaluative verb delivering a verdict against complacency (P6): "The current stabilisation… **does not warrant** policy complacency."

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern name:** Mechanism → Evidence → Authority Diagnosis → Prescription → Cautionary Conclusion.

**Step-by-step instructions to explain a NEW idea with the same pattern:**

1. **State the causal mechanism as a single claim** — name the driver, the outcome, and the intermediate channel in one sentence (e.g., "X fuels Y through Z").
2. **Support the mechanism with two quantified data points** — give a metric and rate for the intermediate factor and a metric and rate for the outcome, each with a time frame and location.
3. **Introduce an authority's framing of the broader concern** — cite a named spokesperson attributing the concern to a recognised institution, and state the scope (domestic, global, sectoral).
4. **Quote the authority's own diagnostic language** — include a direct or closely paraphrased quote that names the specific causes using the institution's terminology, with temporal or conditional language.
5. **Quantify the outcome the authority describes** — convert the qualitative assessment into a precise figure with a current measurement and a forward projection.
6. **Attribute the causes behind the outcome** — have the institution name the specific factors responsible, using quoted language.
7. **Prescribe a remedy with a contrastive justification** — state what policy is needed and explicitly contrast it against an alternative it supersedes.
8. **Escalate to a broader, conditional recommendation** — broaden the remedy to a wider coalition or scope, specify the condition that would trigger it, and name any constraint that limits it.
9. **Deliver a cautionary verdict and close with a concrete proposal** — warn that current conditions do not justify inaction, then name a specific institutional vehicle or mechanism as the positive next step.
