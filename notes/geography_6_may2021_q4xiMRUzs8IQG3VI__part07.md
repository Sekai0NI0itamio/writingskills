## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (sentences 1–8)

1. **Contextual limitation (carry-over):** "multiple factors, a bar graph would not show individual discrepancies" — picks up a reasoning chain from the previous sentence about why the bipolar survey is needed; hands the reader into a concrete figure reference so the justification now needs a demonstration.
2. **Evidence claim:** "Fig.22 shows that OR has a greater EIA than JE" — moves from abstract justification to a specific figure claim; forces the next sentence to define what "greater EIA" means.
3. **Definition/unpack:** "A higher EIA score means less environmental impact" — unpacks the abbreviation just used; hands the reader to a comparison statement by clarifying the scale direction.
4. **Concession + specification:** "Although there may have been multiple smaller discrepancies, opportunities for waste disposal is the main difference" — concedes multiple causes but narrows to one; hands the reader to an explanation of why that factor mattered.
5. **Purpose explanation:** "This factor was included to provide an understanding of the potential for litter" — names the rationale behind the factor; hands the reader to a causal chain linking it to the score.
6. **Mechanism (cause→effect):** "Because less waste disposal opportunities lead to littering, effectively increasing the EI" — provides the logical chain that links factor to score; hands the reader to a competing theoretical claim to set up a test.
7. **Theory/authority claim:** "Theory suggests that because of JE's lower rent, there will be less littering" — introduces a hypothesis to test; hands the reader to a verdict sentence by directly inviting comparison.
8. **Verdict:** "This data disproves that" — collapses the theory against the data; hands the reader into photographic evidence because a verbal claim alone needs visual backing.

**Paragraph 2** (sentences 9–14)

9. **Evidence introduction:** "There is photographic evidence to support this data" — signals a new type of evidence (visual); hands the reader to methodological detail for credibility.
10. **Methodological detail + evidence claim:** "Randomly selected photos… show that opportunities for waste are present in OR and not JE" — adds procedural legitimacy then states the observation; hands the reader to a formal verdict linking back to hypothesis.
11. **Verdict + implication:** "This has visually shown that the EIA for OR is greater than JE, which does not support H1" — restates the figure result and names the hypothesis outcome; hands the reader to a statistical caveat that could overturn the verdict.
12. **Contrast/counter:** "However, a Mann-Whitney test (fig.21) conducted suggests that there is statistical insignificance present" — pivots to a conflicting statistical reading; hands the reader to an interpretation of what that significance level means.
13. **Implication:** "Meaning this discrepancy in value may have happened by chance" — unpacks the statistical jargon into plain consequence; hands the reader to numerical justification.
14. **Final evidence + closing:** "Because 32 and 33 are similar (fig.22), it seems possible that their difference can be attributed to chance" — uses raw values to reinforce the statistical conclusion; closes the section by attributing the difference to randomness rather than a real effect.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **discrepancy-analysis + hypothesis-test section** following this ordered logic:

1. **Justify the analysis type** (why a bar graph was insufficient → motivates the bipolar/survey approach).
2. **State the observed result** from a specific figure (Fig.22).
3. **Define the metric** so the reader can interpret the direction.
4. **Isolate the dominant factor** behind the discrepancy (concede others, then specify).
5. **Explain why that factor was included** in the original design (purpose).
6. **Walk through the mechanism** linking the factor to the score (cause → effect).
7. **Introduce the theoretical prediction** that should hold under the mechanism.
8. **Issue a verdict** comparing the data against the theory.
9. **Add a second, visual evidence type** to back the claim, including method detail for credibility.
10. **Connect the visual back to the formal hypothesis** (H1).
11. **Introduce a statistical counter-evidence** (Mann-Whitney) to qualify the verdict.
12. **Translate the jargon** into a plain-chance implication.
13. **Reinforce with raw numerical closeness** (32 vs 33) as final support.

The order matters because each step sets up tension that the next resolves: metric definition enables the comparison; factor isolation enables the mechanism; mechanism enables the theory test; verdict needs visual backing; visual backing needs statistical qualification.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / transition:** "Although there may have been" — concession-then-isolate move.
- **Cause / consequence:** "Because less waste disposal opportunities lead to littering" — chained cause; "Because 32 and 33 are similar" — numerical-consequence trigger; "effectively increasing the EI" — consequence adverb.
- **Contrast / concession:** "However, a Mann-Whitney test (fig.21) conducted suggests" — pivots the reader away from the visual verdict.
- **Specification / narrowing:** "opportunities for waste disposal is the main difference" — isolates one factor from many.
- **Evidence handling:** "Fig.22 shows that OR has a greater EIA" — figure-led claim; "Randomly selected photos (using a random number generator) show" — method + observation; "There is photographic evidence to support this data" — evidence type announcement.
- **Definition / explanation verbs:** "A higher EIA score means less environmental impact" — defines direction; "This factor was included to provide an understanding of" — explains purpose; "Meaning this discrepancy in value may have happened by chance" — unpacks statistical jargon.
- **Verdict / framing verbs:** "This data disproves that" — collapses a theory; "which does not support H1" — verdict against hypothesis; "it seems possible that their difference can be attributed to chance" — softened final verdict.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the pattern: **claim → define metric → isolate factor → mechanism → theory → verdict → visual evidence → hypothesis link → statistical counter → plain implication → numerical reinforcement.**

To replicate with a new idea:

1. **State the visible result from a specific figure** (e.g., "[Fig.X] shows that A has a higher Y than B"). Always anchor in a figure number.
2. **Immediately define the direction of the metric** so the reader knows whether higher means better or worse.
3. **Concede that multiple variables exist, then isolate one factor** as the main driver. Use "Although… [factor] is the main difference."
4. **Explain why that factor exists in your design** ("This factor was included to provide an understanding of [X]").
5. **Build a cause→effect chain** ("Because [cause] leads to [effect], effectively [outcome]").
6. **Introduce a competing theoretical prediction** that should hold under your mechanism.
7. **Issue a verdict** against the theory ("This data disproves that" or equivalent).
8. **Add a second, visual/qualitative evidence type** to support the verbal claim, naming the method you used to obtain it.
9. **Re-link the observation to the formal hypothesis label** (H1, H2…) and restate the metric's meaning in the same sentence.
10. **Pivot with "However"** to a statistical test that qualifies your claim, naming the specific test.
11. **Translate the statistical jargon** into a plain "Meaning…" sentence.
12. **Reinforce with raw numbers** ("Because [n1] and [n2] are similar, it seems possible that their difference can be attributed to [chance/other factor]").

The discipline is: every claim carries a figure or method tag; every term gets defined before it's used; every verdict is immediately paired with a counter-evidence move before the section closes.
