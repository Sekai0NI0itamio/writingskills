## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Personal insight/claim** — anchors the argument in the writer's own analytical observation, making the section reflective rather than purely descriptive. This sets up *why* the writer is examining bias at all.
2. **Source-context evidence** — establishes credibility and scope (70-year span, varying beliefs). This provides the *raw material* that makes the bias problem tangible.
3. **Contrasting named examples** — two historians with opposing standpoints concretize the abstract diversity claim. This moves the reader from *general* to *specific*, making the mechanism visible.
4. **Mechanism unpack** — explains the causal chain: perspective → selective data use → disregard of counter-data. This is the analytical core that transforms observation into argument.
5. **Resolution/model** — shows how the named historians themselves attempt to correct their bias through integration. This provides a *working example* of the solution before stating the rule.
6. **Normative verdict** — prescribes transparency as the historian's imperative. This closes by elevating the analysis from descriptive to prescriptive.

**Why this order:** each move supplies the premise the next move needs — the claim needs evidence, the evidence needs examples, the examples need unpacking, the unpacking needs a resolution, and the resolution needs a final rule. A student replicating this with a different topic would follow: *state your insight → show your evidence base → give contrasting cases → explain the mechanism → show how the cases address it → state the principle*.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / new point:**
- "Another insight that I had into" — introduces a fresh analytical point following prior reasoning.

**Contrast:**
- "while" — sets opposing standpoints side by side ("while Stephen Kinzer's work focuses on American actions").

**Cause / consequence:**
- "means that" — signals a logical consequence of the preceding evidence ("means that both authors need to select data").
- "in turn" — indicates a downstream effect ("in turn supported by different data").
- "As... allows for" — establishes a causal condition ("As having high quantities of evidence allows for the existence of differing, valid historical narratives").

**Evidence handling:**
- "I consulted" — signals personal engagement with sources ("the journal articles and books that I consulted").
- "These historians have addressed" — reports on how named actors handled a problem ("These historians have addressed the limitations of their worldview").

**Specification / mechanism:**
- "that fits their portrayal of history" — specifies the selective criterion ("select data that fits their portrayal of history").
- "will inevitably disregard" — names the unavoidable consequence of the mechanism ("will inevitably disregard other pieces of data").

**Prescription / verdict:**
- "it is the historian's imperative to" — elevates to a normative duty ("it is the historian's imperative to be upfront").

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern name:** *Insight → Evidence Range → Contrasting Cases → Mechanism → Resolution → Prescription*

**Step-by-step instructions to explain a NEW idea with this pattern:**

1. **State your personal insight as a claim** — open with "Another insight I had into the challenge of [topic] is on [specific aspect]." This anchors the explanation in your own analytical position.
2. **Establish the scope of your evidence** — describe the range, diversity, or spread of your sources or observations using concrete descriptors (e.g., time span, number, variety). This shows *why* the challenge exists.
3. **Name two contrasting cases** — identify two specific actors, texts, or instances with opposing positions or approaches, and state each one's defining orientation. Use "while" to set them in contrast.
4. **Unpack the mechanism** — explain *how* the contrast between the two cases produces a specific problem or dynamic (e.g., selection bias, disregard, framing). Use "means that... need to... and will inevitably..." to show the causal chain.
5. **Show the resolution** — describe how the named cases themselves attempt to correct the problem (e.g., through integration, acknowledgment, balancing). Use "have addressed... through..." to name the corrective action and its structural result.
6. **State the normative prescription** — close with a general principle using "As [condition] allows for [outcome] — it is [actor]'s imperative to [prescriptive action]." This elevates your specific analysis into a transferable rule.
