## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A Materials & Apparatus section in IB Chemistry IA performs these content moves in this order:

1. **Procedure sub-heading(s)** — so each list is clearly bounded and the reader can locate items later when reading the method.
2. **Replicate-defining number** (`5 ×`) — locks in the experimental design (number of trials) before any single item is named, so every later volume/glassware count can be cross-checked against this number.
3. **Sample/substrate** — names the biological or chemical starting material first so the reader understands *what* the rest of the apparatus acts on.
4. **Bulk solvent** (usually distilled water) — supplies the universal medium that almost every bench procedure needs.
5. **Timing tool** (stopwatch) — appears before heating/cooling items because timed steps come early in most procedures.
6. **General glassware** (beakers, flasks, watch glasses) — the everyday containers, listed before specialised glassware.
7. **Heat/energy source** (hot plate, heating mantle) — paired with whatever step it powers.
8. **Filtration/transfer pair** (funnel + cotton) — bridges extraction and measurement phases.
9. **Precision measurement instruments** (balance, pipette, burette, volumetric flasks) — grouped together because they share an uncertainty logic (± value).
10. **Specialised apparatus** (separating funnel, centrifuge) — listed after general glassware because they serve a single procedural step.
11. **Named reagents in order of use** (acid → oxidiser → reducer → indicator), each with chemical formula in parentheses — the reader can mentally re-walk the titration or extraction by re-reading this sequence.
12. **Drying agents / finishing reagents** (MgSO₄) — placed at the end of the extraction list because they act on the final organic layer.
13. **Alternative/back-up tools** (heating mantle after hot plate) — appended last so the reader sees the primary route first.

The logic of this order is **procedural dependency**: each item is introduced only when the previous one has created a need (a substrate needs a vessel; a vessel needs a solvent; a solvent needs a timing tool if heated, etc.). A student replicating this sequence must place each item *as soon as the method would logically call for it*, not alphabetically or by category.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Procedure naming (header noun phrase):** "Dichloromethane Solvent Extraction:" / "Iodometric Back Titration:" — a noun phrase of [technique] [modification] that tells the reader which chemistry will be used.
- **Replicate multiplier:** "5 ×" — front-loaded onto almost every list item so the reader can see trial replication at a glance.
- **Uncertainty phrasing (instrument-defined):** "± 0.001g" paired with "Electronic balance ± 0.001g" — attaches precision to the tool, not the reading.
- **Uncertainty phrasing (measurement-defined):** "± 10 mL" paired with "250 ± 10 mL beakers" — attaches tolerance to the stated capacity.
- **Word + parenthetical formula (definition move):** "(H2O)", "(H2SO4)", "(Na2CO3)", "(CH2Cl2)", "(MgSO4)", "(Na2S2O3)" — six instances of word-name followed by bracketed chemical formula; serves as in-line definition for the reader.
- **Concentration/percentage specification (composition detail):** "(5% iodine, 10% potassium iodide)" paired with "Lugol's iodine solution" — specifies composition inline so the reagent identity is unambiguous.
- **Active-component callout (sample detail):** "(22mg caffeine)" paired with "Lipton Black Tea tea bags" — flags the analyte mass inside the substrate.
- **Logical grouping marker (implicit "then"):** the line break itself functions as a temporal conjunction — each new line item is read as "next, you need…" without an explicit connective word.
- **No explicit contrast/cause connectives** appear because no causal claim is being made in a list section; logic is carried by *order*, not by connectives.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **procedural-dependency enumeration**: items appear in the order the experimental hand will need them, and each item hands the reader to the next by *creating a procedural requirement*.

Steps to replicate for a NEW apparatus list:

1. **Write the procedure name as a noun-phrase header ending with a colon** so the reader can bind every later item to one technique.
2. **State the replicate count first** (e.g. 5 ×) and let it prefix every item that comes in multiple copies, so the reader instantly sees the experimental design.
3. **Name the sample/substrate before anything else**, with the active-component mass in parentheses, so the reader knows what is being acted on.
4. **List the bulk solvent second**, because every bench procedure starts with a liquid medium.
5. **Introduce a timing tool immediately after any step that is heated or rate-limited**, because time-dependence must be flagged before the heat source is named.
6. **Pair each container with its cover or closure in adjacent lines** (beaker → watch glass; flask → funnel), so the reader sees the sub-assembly as a unit.
7. **Attach ± uncertainties directly to the capacity or to the instrument**, not to the procedure, so precision logic is consistent throughout.
8. **Group precision tools (balance, pipette, burette, volumetric flask) consecutively** so the reader can audit calibration in one block.
9. **List specialised apparatus (separating funnel, centrifuge) after general glassware**, because they serve one specific phase.
10. **Order reagents by sequence of use** (acid → oxidiser → reducer → indicator), each written as [word name] ([formula]), so the reader can mentally re-walk the chemistry by re-reading the list.
11. **Append drying agents and back-up tools last**, after the primary reagent, so the primary route is read before the alternatives.
12. **Use line breaks, not words, to carry the "next" logic** — every new line is implicitly "and then you need…", which is the connective mechanism of this section.
