## What This Section Does (content sequence)
This is the **counter-argument / opposing-factor** section of an IB History argument. The standard replication sequence is:

1. **Restate the opposing cause** (1st move): present *the* alternative explanation the source group had for annexation. Sets up the rebuttal stage.
2. **Complicate the cause with timing/contingency** (2nd move): show the cause was real but only realised under specific political conditions. Forces the reader to see it as necessary-not-sufficient.
3. **Introduce a second competing cause with "Alternatively"** (3rd move): the first cause having been complicated, introduce a structurally different cause (here, strategic location). This is the section's structural hinge.
4. **Establish contemporary context to make the cause plausible** (4th move): situate the cause in the period's dominant event so it doesn't sound anachronistic.
5. **Narrow the discourse with "However"** (5th move): move from abstract cause to the *specific framing* contemporaries used, sharpening the claim.
6. **Cite a named authority with full credentials** (6th move): primary-source quotation from someone with formal relevance (chairman of foreign affairs committee) — meets IB expectation of named evidence.
7. **Begin verdict on the *nature* of the cause** (7th move): "the pure intent of…" frames the cause as instrumental/imperial rather than genuinely strategic.

**Why this order:** each move *narrows and substantiates* the previous one — first the cause, then the political precondition, then an alternative cause, then its historical plausibility, then its contemporary narrowing, then its proof, then its characterisation. The reader is never asked to accept a bare claim; every claim is either contextualised, evidenced, or qualified.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — "Contingent cause" paragraph**
SKELETON: "[Adversary]'s [motivation noun] were stymied by [obstacle A]; although [adversary]'s fundamental goal was [core aim], these plans were only realised after [condition B]."

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: an agent and a noun of intention (e.g. "Committee's initial plans").
   - Slot 2: a blocking political actor/context (e.g. "an isolationist Cleveland administration").
   - Slot 3: restatement of the agent's aim (grammatically parallel to slot 1's noun).
   - Slot 4: the enabling condition that finally made it happen (typically a new leader or regime shift).
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Pick a historical actor whose main scheme failed once but worked later. State the failed attempt (past passive), name the blocking figure, restate the aim in concessive form ("although X wanted Y"), then name the enabling trigger (a new policy / president / law).
3. **Original fill:** "However, the Committee's initial plans were stymied by an isolationist Cleveland administration; although the Committee's fundamental goal was American annexation, these plans were only realised after the inauguration of an expansionist president."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "However, the Suffragettes' direct-action campaign was hampered by a Liberal government wary of civil disorder; although the movement's fundamental aim was the franchise, parliamentary momentum only built after Asquith's replacement by Lloyd George."

**Skeleton B — "Alternative cause with context" paragraph**
SKELETON: "Alternatively, the [event] could have been due to [factor]. This argument was topical in a political climate dominated by [contemporaneous event]; [mechanism sentence specifying how the contemporary event created the need]. However, the discourse preceding [event] focused on [narrower framing]. [Authority, with title], said that '[quoted claim connecting the factor to the event].'"

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: topic + alternative causal factor (noun phrase).
   - Slot 2: a contemporaneous big event the reader already knows.
   - Slot 3: mechanism — how the big event created a *logistical/functional* need for the factor.
   - Slot 4: narrow-down ("However") specifying the exact framing contemporaries used.
   - Slot 5: named authority with formal position + quoted claim tying factor to event.
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Pick a historical outcome with multiple causes. Use "Alternatively" to flag this as the second cause. Anchor it to a *different* contemporaneous big event, then explain the logistical link, then narrow to contemporary discourse, then quote someone whose job makes them credible.
3. **Original fill:** "Alternatively, the annexation could have been due to Hawaii's strategic location as a military base. This argument was topical in a political climate dominated by the 1898 Spanish-American War… However, the discourse preceding annexation focused on using Hawaii to aid the war effort. Congressman Robert Hitt… said that 'it is not possible to send support…'"
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "Alternatively, the naval base at Scapa Flow could have been built due to its deep-water anchorage. This argument was topical in a political climate dominated by the 1916 Battle of Jutland; Britain's dreadnought losses required a northern repair haven beyond German U-boat range. However, the discourse preceding the 1918 expansion focused on convoy assembly for Atlantic supply lines. Admiral Jellicoe, First Sea Lord, said that 'no squadron can sortie from the eastern coast in winter without sheltered coaling at Scapa.'"

**Skeleton C — "Antithesis bridge" sentence-pair**
SKELETON: "[Sentence ending the previous cause]. Conversely, [alternative actor] would [verb of imposition] [target], [participial phrase weighting the benefit]."

1. **Slots:** preceding sentence ends one cause; this pair pivots with "Conversely" to introduce the opposing actor and the mechanism by which that actor would benefit, weighted by a participial phrase.
2. **How to fill:** Write the prior cause's limit (e.g. "…would expand suffrage"). Use "Conversely" + actor + imposition verb + object + "weighted/biased in favour of [actor]."
3. **Original fill:** "Conversely, annexation would allow the United States to unilaterally impose its economic interests on Hawaii, weighted in favour of the Committee of Safety."
4. **Demonstration fill:** "Conversely, partition would allow the British administration to unilaterally impose a settler franchise on the province, weighted in favour of the Ulster loyalist population."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / alternative-framing**
- "Conversely," → "Conversely, annexation would allow the United States to" (pivot from one cause to the opposite cause).
- "Alternatively," → "Alternatively, the annexation could have been due to" (introduces the *second* competing explanation after the first has been qualified).

**Concession / contrast**
- "However," → "However, the Committee's initial plans were stymied by" (negates the success of the prior cause).
- "However," (second use) → "However, the discourse preceding annexation focused on" (narrows the broad cause to its specific contemporary framing).
- "although" → "although the Committee's fundamental goal was American annexation" (concedes motive while preserving the contrast in the main clause).

**Cause / consequence / specification**
- "would allow… to unilaterally impose" → "annexation would allow the United States to unilaterally impose" (frames cause as enabling mechanism).
- "could have been due to" → "the annexation could have been due to Hawaii's strategic location" (tentative attribution of cause).
- "only realised after" → "these plans were only realised after the inauguration" (marks temporal precondition).

**Evidence handling / authority**
- "said that" → "Congressman Robert Hitt… said that 'it is not possible to send support'" (introduces attributed primary-source quotation).
- "Chairman of the Committee on Foreign Affairs" (appositive credential attached to the source — establishes relevance to the claim being made).

**Contextualisation**
- "topical in a political climate dominated by" → "topical in a political climate dominated by the 1898 Spanish-American War" (anchors the cause in period mood).
- "on the verge of winning as a protectorate" → "the Philippines, which the United States was on the verge of winning" (specifies a contemporaneous fact to make the strategic argument plausible).

**Verdict-setting**
- "The pure intent of…" (begins a characterisation move, signalling that the author is about to judge the *nature* of the cause just cited).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **opposing-factor pattern**: *cause → complicate-cause → alternative-cause → context → narrow-context → authority-evidence → verdict-on-nature*.

To replicate with a new idea:

1. **State the opposing cause clearly.** Name what your chosen actor/group *thought* they were doing. Use a verb of imposition ("would allow… to impose") and a participial phrase to show who benefited.
2. **Complicate that cause with timing.** Use "However" + past passive ("plans were stymied by") to show the cause didn't immediately succeed. Then use "although" to re-state the aim and a temporal phrase ("only realised after…") to identify the enabling condition.
3. **Introduce a structurally different second cause with "Alternatively."** Switch from the *internal/motive* axis to a different axis (here: strategic/functional). State it as a tentative attribution ("could have been due to").
4. **Anchor the new cause in a known contemporaneous event.** Use "topical in a political climate dominated by" so the cause is not anachronistic.
5. **Specify the mechanism.** Give a concrete logistical/functional link between the contemporary event and your cause (here: coaling/supply at Honolulu).
6. **Narrow the discourse with "However."** Move from abstract cause to *exactly* how contemporaries framed it ("focused on using X to aid Y").
7. **Quote a named authority with formal relevance.** Attribute by name + position; quote only the part that bears directly on the causal claim.
8. **Begin a verdict on the *intent* of the discourse.** Use "The pure intent of…" (or equivalent) so the reader knows a critical judgement is about to land — and leaves them expecting the continuation.
