## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **causal-evaluation move in a historical argument** — the student is testing *why* a legislative act passed. The ordered sequence is:

1. **Name a specific actor and their proximate motive** (sets up a straw-man hypothesis the writer will knock down).
2. **Concede that the motive fails to generalize** with comparative numbers (208 vs. 91) (proves the hypothesis insufficient).
3. **Identify the real, broader trigger** using a temporal cleft ("only after the onset of war") (installs the replacement explanation).
4. **Provide paired evidence** — one failed attempt *plus* one successful one — that locates the trigger in time (gives the trigger empirical weight).
5. **Translate the political act into a material consequence** (military entrenchment, Pearl Harbor) (shows the act had real-world weight, not just procedural meaning).
6. **Open a synthesis sentence with "Thus"** (delivers the verdict that the trigger — war — explains the vote).

The *why* of the order: a single-actor motive is the easiest explanation to write down, so the student builds it first only to dismantle it; only after the reader sees it fail is the alternative (war) credible. Numbers come before narrative because they prove the motive doesn't scale. The temporal cleft comes before the treaty evidence because the student needs the reader to *accept* war-as-trigger before the 1897/1898 contrast registers as proof. Consequence comes last because it is what licenses the verdict.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — "Appointed-actor → vested-interest" causal opener**

SKELETON: "[Actor] was appointed to [body charged with a governance task]. [Actor] therefore had a vested interest in [the legislative act], as it [increased proximity to / secured a stake in] [power / decision-making over X]."

1. **What each slot holds:**
   - Slot 1: a named individual + past-tense passive appointment clause ("was appointed to").
   - Slot 2: a *therefore*-led claim using "had a vested interest in" + a legislative noun phrase.
   - Slot 3: an *as*-clause giving the mechanism (a verb of acquisition — *increased, secured, cemented* — plus a proximity/stake noun).
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** pick a historical actor who was placed on a commission/committee/board that later benefited from a law; pick a law that rewarded that placement (trade rights, jurisdiction, funding); state the mechanism as a verb-of-proximity.
3. **Original fill:** "Congressman Hitt... appointed to the commission to establish American governance in Hawaii. Congressman Hitt therefore had a vested interest in the passage of the Newlands Resolution, as it increased his proximity to power."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "Lord Curzon was elevated to the Viceroy's Executive Council in 1898. Lord Curzon therefore had a vested interest in the passage of the Indian Councils Act, as it secured his stake in the reformed chamber's deliberations."

**SKELETON B — "However, single motive → broader pattern with numbers" concession move**

SKELETON: "However, [actor]'s sole [self-interest / personal stake] was not applicable to the [large number] [other actors] who [supported the act], compared to the [smaller number] who [opposed it]."

1. **Slot shapes:** *However* + possessive + *sole* + abstract motive noun + *was not applicable to* + cardinal number + collective noun + *who* + past-tense supportive verb + *compared to* + smaller cardinal number + *who* + opposing verb.
2. **How to fill:** identify a case where one figure's motive has just been named; find a recorded vote, signature count, or roll-call where the majority outcome vastly exceeds the minority; plug both totals into the skeleton and let the asymmetry do the work.
3. **Original fill:** "However, Hitt's sole self-interest was not applicable to the 208 other Congressmen who voted for the resolution in July 1898, compared to the 91 representatives who opposed it."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "However, Curzon's personal stake in patronage was not applicable to the 142 other council members who endorsed the Act, compared to the 37 who rejected it."

**SKELETON C — "Only after [trigger] → legislative shift → material consequence" causal chain**

SKELETON: "It was only after [the onset of X] that [the first of many Y] [passed / was ratified]. Although [earlier attempt] failed to [meet threshold] in [earlier date], [later attempt] was [ratified / signed] in [later date] with [percentage] support. It [entrenched / codified / cemented] [material consequence], and [iconic site / artefact] was [built / founded / established] during the following [period]."

1. **Slot shapes:** a *It was only after…that* cleft (trigger noun phrase in the *after*-clause; legislative noun phrase in the *that*-clause); then an *Although*-paired before/after contrast with a threshold clause and a percentage; finally two coordinated past-tense verbs linking political act to physical artefact.
2. **How to fill:** pick a historical event whose start date is conventionally treated as a turning point; pair a failed earlier bill/treaty with a successful later one that shares the same goal; identify one durable infrastructure outcome of the later act.
3. **Original fill:** "It was only after the onset of war that the first of many resolutions to annex Hawaii passed Congress. Although the 1897 Treaty of Annexation failed to gain a two-thirds majority in the Senate in early 1898, the joint resolution of annexation was signed in July with 70% support. It entrenched existing American military presence, and Pearl Harbor was built as a base during the following year."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea):** "It was only after the outbreak of plague that the first of many sanitary Acts passed Parliament. Although the 1884 Public Health Amendment failed to clear the Lords in 1885, the Infectious Disease Notification Act was enacted in 1889 with 68% support. It entrenched compulsory local reporting, and the Port of London's disinfection station was constructed during the following year."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / cause-consequence**
- "therefore had a vested interest" — binds appointment to motive as cause→effect.
- "as it increased his proximity to power" — *as*-clause giving the mechanism behind the *therefore* claim.
- "It was only after the onset of war...that the first of many resolutions...passed" — cleft construction used to isolate a single trigger.
- "It entrenched existing American military presence" — present-tense framing of a past political act as a still-operative consequence.

**Concession / contrast**
- "However, Hitt's sole self-interest was not applicable" — opens the turn against the just-stated motive.
- "compared to the 91 representatives who opposed it" — comparative particle + opposing group to numerically anchor the concession.
- "Although the 1897 Treaty of Annexation failed to gain a two-thirds majority" — *Although*-paired concession that pre-empts a "but the legislature was always in favour" objection.

**Specification / evidence-handling**
- "the 208 other Congressmen who voted for the resolution in July 1898" — cardinal + collective noun + *who*-relative + date, used to make a vote empirical rather than rhetorical.
- "with 70% support" — compressed statistic closing the contrast pair.
- "the first of many resolutions to annex Hawaii" — ordinal framing that signals a pattern, not a one-off.

**Synthesis / verdict openers**
- "Thus, one can" — closing operator the section uses to pivot from evidence to argument (sentence trails off into the next part of the paragraph).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses a **"straw-man motive → numerical disproof → alternative trigger → temporal evidence → material consequence → synthesis"** causal-historical explanation pattern. To explain a *new* idea with the same pattern:

1. **Build a single-actor motive first.** Pick one named figure tied to the event and present their self-interest in a *therefore/as* construction so the reader assumes it explains the outcome.
2. **Knock the motive down with comparative numbers.** Find a recorded count (vote, signatures, membership) showing that the majority vastly outnumbers the minority. Open with *However* and use *compared to* to juxtapose the two figures in a single sentence.
3. **Install the real trigger with a *only after…that* cleft.** Name the broader event (war, crisis, discovery) and place the legislative result in the *that*-clause; the cleft syntax forces the reader to accept the trigger as the necessary condition.
4. **Support the trigger with a paired concession.** Show one earlier attempt that failed to meet a stated threshold, then the later attempt that passed by a stated margin, in a single *Although… [later clause]* sentence. The asymmetry itself is the proof.
5. **Translate the political act into a physical artefact.** In one sentence, state what the act *entrenched* or *codified*, then add a coordinated clause naming a durable site, structure, or institution built in the following period. This converts abstract procedure into tangible consequence.
6. **Open the verdict with *Thus*.** Begin a final sentence with *Thus, one can…* and let it run into the paragraph's own conclusion clause, signalling that the chain of evidence now licenses a general claim.
