## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This section type — a **secondary-source bibliography** — makes one type of move, repeated: **list a consulted authority**. The order matters not by argument but by *evidentiary layering*:

1. **First**, a contemporary-era commentator (Ishak, 1960) — sets the floor of received opinion.
2. **Second**, a later constitutional historian (Lau, 1989) — adds legal-procedural depth.
3. **Third**, a revisionist scholar (Amoroso, 1998) — introduces critical perspective.
4. **Fourth**, an institutional/organisational historian (Stockwell, 1977) — supplies party-history detail.
5. **Fifth**, a near-contemporary policy witness (Purcell, 1946) — anchors the original constitutional moment.
6. **Sixth**, a historiographical reviewer (Stenson, 1969) — closes by surveying the field itself.

Why this order: it **braids chronology with perspective type** so the marker can see the student consulted both *participants* and *later analysts*. Generalising: any historiography bibliography can be ordered from earliest published take → specialist deepening → revisionist → contemporary witness → field-review, giving the marker breadth without arguing a thesis.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

Because this section is non-discursive, its "connectives" are **structural punctuation**, not logical connectors. Grouped by job:

- **Sequencing (structural):** no explicit connectors — progression is conveyed by *line break + identical citation template repeated*, e.g. "Vol. 11, No. 4 (1977)" → next line begins a new author.
- **Specification (title-internal):** quotation marks around the article title isolate the *specific piece* being credited, e.g. *"'A Malayan Union: The Proposed New Constitution'"* — the colon + gerund phrase specifies which *aspect* of the topic the source covers.
- **Identification (authorship):** the *Surname-Forename-Initial* form, e.g. "A.J. Stockwell" — establishes authority without evaluative language.
- **Volume/issue pinpointing:** "Vol. 20, No. 2 (Sep 1989)" — locates the source precisely so the marker can verify.
- **No contrast/concession vocabulary** — none is needed or appropriate in a bibliography.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **identification by citation template** — i.e., the student *delegates authority* rather than argues. The replication pattern is:

1. **Gather 4–6 secondary sources** that together cover (a) a near-contemporary witness, (b) a mid-period analytical historian, (c) a recent revisionist, (d) a field-reviewer.
2. **For each source, copy the metadata** into the fixed order: Author → Article title in quotes → Journal (italicised) → Vol. → No. → (Month Year).
3. **Order the entries** so the chronological/typological spread is visible to the reader — earliest → most recent → historiographical reviewer often works.
4. **Separate entries by hard line-break**, with a hanging indent so the second line aligns under the first word after the opening quotation mark (this is the visual cue the reader uses to parse each as a *unit*, not a sentence).
5. **End with a page number** (here, "12") flush right — signals bibliography closure.

No argument is constructed; each citation *is* the claim. The marker infers the student's research breadth from the *spread* of journals and decades, not from any connective prose.
