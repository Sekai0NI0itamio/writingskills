## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Liquid–liquid separation and drying** — the crude caffeine solution is partitioned using DCM, and MgSO₄ removes residual water. This sets up a clean organic solvent layer for the next move.
2. **Filtration of the drying agent** — the clumped MgSO₄ is removed so that only the clean DCM-caffeine solution remains, enabling evaporation without solid contaminants.
3. **Evaporation of DCM** — heating removes the solvent entirely, leaving crude caffeine as a solid. This sets up the next move: dissolving the solid for recrystallization.
4. **Dissolution and crystallization** — caffeine is redissolved in water and recrystallized on a watch glass, producing a more purified solid. This sets up the final move: collection and weighing.
5. **Collection and weighing** — the crude caffeine is scraped, stored in vials, and weighed, completing the purification chain and providing the sample for quantification.
6. **Dissolution of sample for titration** — a known mass of crude caffeine is dissolved in distilled water, setting up the acidification step.
7. **Acidification** — sulphuric acid is added to create the acidic medium required for the caffeine–iodine reaction, setting up the oxidizing agent addition.
8. **Iodine addition and precipitate formation** — Lugol's iodine reacts with caffeine, forming a brown precipitate that must be removed before titration.
9. **Filtration and burette setup** — the precipitate is filtered and the flask is positioned under a sodium thiosulphate burette, setting up the titration.
10. **Indicator addition and titration to endpoint** — starch indicator is added and titration proceeds until the color change signals completion, setting up the final measurement.
11. **Final measurement and repetition** — the burette reading is recorded and the procedure is repeated for remaining samples, completing the quantification.

**Why this order:** Purification must precede quantification because the titration measures caffeine content in a purified sample; each step's product becomes the next step's input, and the analytical method follows the preparatory method.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern:** Linear procedural chain — each step names an action, specifies reagents/equipment with measured volumes, states the purpose or expected observation, and hands off to the next step as its direct consequence.

1. **Name the action verb first** — begin each step with an imperative verb (e.g., "Transfer," "Add," "Filter," "Place") so the reader knows exactly what to do.
2. **Specify the object and the container** — state what is being acted upon and where it goes (e.g., "the contents of a vial of crude caffeine into an erlenmeyer flask").
3. **Add measured quantities with uncertainties** — include the volume and its tolerance (e.g., "15.0 mL ± 0.1mL of distilled water") so the procedure is reproducible.
4. **State the purpose or expected observation inline** — explain why the reagent is added or what should happen (e.g., "to act as a drying agent that will clump together," "until a brown solid precipitate begins to form").
5. **Link each step to the next by consequence** — ensure the output of one step (e.g., clumped drying agent) is the input condition for the next step (e.g., filtration to remove it), so the reader follows a single continuous causal chain.
6. **Close with measurement and repetition** — end the procedure by naming the final data point to record and the scope of repetition (e.g., "Take the final measurement of the burette and repeat for the rest of the samples").
