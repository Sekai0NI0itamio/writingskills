## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Recap the target symbol** (e.g. "drag loss −∆D") — anchors the reader in the quantity the upcoming equation defines.
2. **State the governing equation** without explanation — gives the reader something concrete to attach meaning to.
3. **Unpack each variable in turn**, attaching SI units and physical meaning — equips the reader to *read* the equation that was just displayed.
4. **Read a mathematical consequence out of the equation** (the V² scaling) — converts a formula into a *claim* the reader can carry.
5. **Apply that consequence to the specific domain** ("rockets achieve very high velocities") — links pure math to the investigated case.
6. **Issue a generalisation caveat** ("there cannot be a generalized reduction… heavily dependent on the rocket's specifications") — bounds the claim and motivates the need for a *case-specific* calculation.
7. **Introduce the calculation route** ("Calculating the velocity differential… involves the following differential equation") — pivots from theory to procedure.

This order works because each step **earns the right to the next**: you cannot unpack variables until the equation exists; you cannot draw a scaling claim until the variables are defined; you cannot warn against generalisation until the scaling has been stated; you cannot move into procedure until you have justified *why* a procedure is needed.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### SKELETON A — Variable-unpack paragraph
**Template:** "Where [var₁] is [definition₁ with units], [var₂] is [definition₂ with units]… and [varₙ] is [definitionₙ with units]."

1. **What each slot holds:** a single-clause appositive that names a symbol, gives a short physical meaning, and pins an SI unit in superscript or "in [unit]". Grammatically: "[Symbol] is [phrase] in [unit]…".
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** slot 1 — pick the first symbol that appears in your formula and state what it physically represents (e.g. "k is the thermal conductivity"); slot 2 — pick the symbol paired with density or concentration in your formula and give its units; slot n — repeat for the geometric/area term. Use the same "is … in …" cadence so the reader can scan.
3. **Original fill:** "Where CD is the rockets drag coefficient, ρ is the air density in kgm⁻³ at the rocket's instantaneous height, V~ is rocket's instantaneous velocity in ms⁻¹, and A is the frontal area of the rocket in m²."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea — heat loss in a building):** "Where k is the thermal conductivity of the wall in W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, A is the wall surface area in m², ΔT is the temperature difference between inside and outside in K, and d is the wall thickness in m."

### SKELETON B — Equation → mathematical consequence → domain significance → generalisation caveat
**Template:** "As we can see from this equation, [quantitative observation]. Since [domain fact], [consequence]. For this reason, [boundary on the claim], since [reason for the boundary]."

1. **What each slot holds:** S1 — a numerical/mathematical pattern the equation exhibits; S2 — a real-world reason the pattern matters; S3 — a concession that limits how far the pattern can be applied.
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** S1 — isolate one variable raised to a power in your equation and state what happens when it doubles/triples; S2 — name the context where that variable naturally lives at extreme values; S3 — state that the *magnitude* of the effect depends on uncontrolled parameters, so a single number cannot cover all cases.
3. **Original fill:** "As we can see from this equation, as velocity doubles, the drag force is quadrupled. Since rockets achieve very high velocities, this force becomes very significant. For this reason, there cannot be a generalized reduction of performance for all rocket's, since it is heavily dependent on the rocket's specifications."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea — electromagnetic induction in turbines):** "As we can see from this equation, as the rotation rate doubles, the induced EMF is quadrupled. Since wind turbines rotate at very high RPMs, this induced voltage becomes very significant. For this reason, there cannot be a generalized efficiency figure for all turbines, since it is heavily dependent on the turbine's blade geometry."

### SKELETON C — Continuation fragment + equation-introduction pivot
**Template:** "[named quantity with its symbol]. The equation for [phenomenon] is given by the following equation:"

1. **What each slot holds:** a half-sentence fragment that re-states the quantity last discussed (often carrying a symbol), followed by a full sentence that announces a formula.
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:** slot 1 — take the symbol you just defined and re-display it as a noun phrase to remind the reader; slot 2 — name the physical process the next equation captures and announce "is given by the following equation:" so the formula lands cleanly.
3. **Original fill:** "drag loss −∆D. The equation for drag is given by the following equation:"
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea — radioactive decay):** "activity A. The equation for activity is given by the following equation:"

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Equation-introduction pivot:** "is given by the following equation:" (introduces the formula the reader must parse next).
- **Definition pivot (variable list):** "Where CD is the rockets drag coefficient…" (turns a displayed formula into readable symbols).
- **Evidence-handling / reading-from-formula:** "As we can see from this equation, as velocity doubles, the drag force is quadrupled." (uses "as we can see from" to flag that the next claim is *read off* the equation).
- **Cause / domain linkage:** "Since rockets achieve very high velocities, this force becomes very significant." (uses "Since" to chain the abstract V² result to the concrete domain).
- **Consequence / verdict:** "For this reason, there cannot be a generalized reduction of performance…" (uses "For this reason" to convert the previous sentence into a limit on the claim).
- **Specification / next-step pivot:** "Calculating the velocity differential of the rocket involves the following differential equation" (uses "involves the following" to point the reader at the *procedure* coming up).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **formula → variable unpack → quantitative consequence → domain significance → generalisation caveat → procedural pivot**. To explain a *new* idea the same way:

1. **Re-name the target quantity** in a short fragment so the reader knows what is being opened up. (e.g. "heat loss Q.")
2. **Display the governing equation** with no commentary yet — let the reader see the symbols before they are explained.
3. **Unpack every variable in a single "Where…" sentence**, attaching physical meaning and SI units in the same cadence.
4. **Pull one mathematical consequence out of the equation** (a doubling, halving, squaring, inverse relationship) and state it explicitly with "as [var] doubles, [result] is [transformed]".
5. **Anchor that consequence in your specific domain** with a "Since [domain fact], [result] becomes [adjective]" sentence — this converts abstract math into a real-world claim.
6. **Issue a generalisation caveat** with "For this reason, [universal statement] cannot hold, since it is heavily dependent on [context-specific parameters]." This bounds the claim and earns the reader's trust.
7. **Pivot into procedure** with "Calculating [target] involves the following [equation/method]:" so the next chunk of text can deliver a calculation, not more theory.

The rhythm the reader experiences is: *here is a symbol → here is its formula → here is what each piece means → here is what the formula forces → here is what that means for our case → here is why you cannot stop there → here is how you actually proceed.*
