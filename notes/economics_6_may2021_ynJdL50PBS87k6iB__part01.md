## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **journalistic/news-feature opening**, not a method section. Its moves in order:

1. **Precedent by peer comparison** — names a smaller rival spend so Amazon's later scale reads comparatively.
2. **Corporate-authority quote** — spokesperson explains *why* the site was chosen, validating the location.
3. **Quantitative scale inventory** — bullets of offices/centers/workforce that *evidence* the verbal claim made in the quote.
4. **Measurement translation** — converts one key figure into a tangible analogy (football fields).
5. **Symbolic verdict** — lifts the inventory into a defining claim about the national industry.
6. **Human/personal anecdote hook** — a named founder's visit, launching the next beat.

*Why that order*: each move sets up the rhetorical gap the next one fills. Comparison (1) raises "how big is this?"; the quote (2) answers *why here*; the inventory (3) answers *how big*; the football-fields line (4) makes (3) legible; the verdict (5) makes (3–4) *matter*; the anecdote (6) pulls the abstract verdict back to a person. Replicable for any "big-company-in-a-country" topic by following: peer anchor → in-house voice → raw numbers → readable analogy → national-level verdict → named individual.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### SKELETON A — Peer-comparison anchor paragraph

```
SKELETON: "[Rival firm], which spent [currency amount] for the development of its [asset type] there."
```

1. **Slot holds**: a single sentence whose grammatical shape is *Subject — relative clause with past-tense spend — prepositional phrase of place*. The clause functions as a comparative benchmark.
2. **How to fill with a different idea**: name a smaller/earlier competitor in your industry; research a publicly reported investment figure; name the physical asset (offices, factory, lab); keep it one sentence so it reads as a *throwaway* that nonetheless sharpens your main case.
3. **Original filled version**: "Microsoft and Apple, which spent $25 million for the development of its offices there."
4. **Demo fill (different idea — Tesla in Mexico)**: *"Volkswagen and Ford, which committed roughly $800 million for the development of their gigafactory sites there, had signalled the same trend years earlier."*

### SKELETON B — Authority-quote paragraph (significance framing)

```
SKELETON: "'[Location] is a known [field] [hub-noun], and [actor] has been an enabler for us to have [asset] this size,' said [Name], [Title]. 'This is [a confirmation / proof] of how [region] continues to be important to [company].'"
```

1. **Slot holds**: two quoted strings separated by an attribution tag. Quote 1 = locational reason (talent + enabler); Quote 2 = strategic verdict. Attribution = name + corporate role.
2. **How to fill with a different idea**: pick a real spokesperson from a credible news source on your topic; in Quote 1, give the city/region an adjective and name the sector plus a facilitating actor (government, university, port authority); in Quote 2, name what the project *confirms* about the country/region's importance to the firm.
3. **Original filled version**: "'Hyderabad is a known software tech talent center, and the government has been an enabler for us to have a campus this size,' said Minari Shah, an Amazon spokeswoman. 'This is an important confirmation of how India continues to be important to Amazon.'"
4. **Demo fill (different idea — Starbucks in Vietnam)**: *"'Ho Chi Minh City is a fast-growing specialty-coffee market, and the municipal authorities have been a partner for us to scale a roastery this size,' said a Starbucks regional director. 'This is a clear signal of how Southeast Asia continues to matter to our long-term growth.'"*

### SKELETON C — Scale-inventory paragraph

```
SKELETON: "Over the last [decade/period], the [entity] has [metaphor of integration]. And now, [duration] after [event], the [facility], [parenthetical distinction], joins [count A] other [facilities], [count B] [facilities], [count C] [facilities] and a work force of [number D] (plus [number E] [category]) in [region]."
```

1. **Slot holds**: a *time-bracket claim* sentence followed by an *enumerative list* sentence with parallel counting nouns. The metaphor sentence sets tone; the list sentence provides the proof.
2. **How to fill with a different idea**: choose a measurable time span; pick a metaphor of entrenchment (woven into fabric / become embedded / set down roots); then compile 3–4 infrastructure categories with rounded figures (units, headcount) and append a "(plus X [auxiliary category])" parenthetical for precision texture.
3. **Original filled version**: "Over the last decade, the technology behemoth has woven itself into the fabric of Indian life. And now, four years after construction began, the Hyderabad office, Amazon's first fully owned office outside the United States, joins 40 other offices, 67 shipping centers, 1,400 delivery stations and a work force of more than 60,000 (plus 155,000 contractors) in the country."
4. **Demo fill (different idea — DHL in Poland)**: *"Over the last fifteen years, the logistics group has quietly embedded itself into Polish commerce. And now, three years after construction began, the Łódź hub, DHL's first fully automated sortation centre in Eastern Europe, joins 22 depots, 190 pickup-and-delivery branches, 41 bonded warehouses and a staff of roughly 4,800 (plus 7,200 third-party drivers) in the country."*

### SKELETON D — Symbolic-verdict paragraph

```
SKELETON: "The [superlative] size of the [object] — [measurement] — [further measurement] are equal to [concrete comparison]. They have come to symbolize a defining feature of [country/sector]'s [adjective] [sector]: the [adjective] presence of [actor type]."
```

1. **Slot holds**: a *measurement-and-analogy* sentence (with em-dash parenthetical) followed by a *verdict* sentence (colon introduces the symbolic label). The colon is the load-bearing punctuation; without it, the sentence collapses into mere description.
2. **How to fill with a different idea**: take the single most striking figure from your inventory; convert it into two units (absolute number + scaled analogy); then choose a one-word label for what it *symbolises* (the inexorable presence / the unstoppable rise / the quiet takeover).
3. **Original filled version**: "The record size of the building — 1.8 million square feet — and the total campus area are equal to nearly 65 football fields. They have come to symbolize a defining feature of India's booming tech industry: the inexorable presence of international tech companies."
4. **Demo fill (different idea — IKEA in Indonesia)**: *"The sheer footprint of the complex — 78,000 square metres — and its rooftop solar array are equal to nearly eleven football pitches. They have come to symbolise a defining feature of Indonesia's consumer boom: the unstoppable advance of Nordic retail."*

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / temporal framing**
- "Over the last decade" → opens the scale-inventory paragraph with a time-bracket claim.
- "And now, four years after construction began" → bridge word ("And now") that pivots from broad history to the present event.
- "When Amazon's founder, Jeff Bezos, visited India in January" → temporal subordinate clause that launches the anecdote.

**Specification / precision texture**
- "(plus 155,000 contractors)" → parenthetical addition that refines a number without breaking the rhythm.
- "first fully owned office outside the United States" → appositive that distinguishes this facility from all the preceding "other offices."

**Evidence handling**
- "said Minari Shah, an Amazon spokeswoman" → mid-sentence attribution tag that delivers credibility in passing.
- "Amazon's first fully owned office outside the United States" → parenthetical category marker that *is itself* evidence of strategic commitment.

**Explanation / metaphor verbs**
- "has woven itself into the fabric of Indian life" → uses a fabric/weaving metaphor to compress a decade of operations into one image.
- "have come to symbolize" → causal framing verb that promotes an object into a metaphor; moves the paragraph from description to verdict.
- "the technology behemoth" → noun-phrase label that pre-loads the entity with scale before any number is given.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section uses is **precedent → authority → inventory → analogy → verdict → anecdote**. To replicate it on a *new* idea (e.g., "a streaming platform's expansion into Brazil"), follow these steps in order:

1. **Open with a peer benchmark** — name one or two smaller competitors and the figure they committed to the same country. One sentence is enough; its job is to make your main subject look comparatively large.
2. **Anchor with a direct quote** — find a real spokesperson. Their first sentence should name the city/region and the *reason* for the site (talent, market, regulation); their second sentence should state what the move *confirms* strategically. This moves the reader from *what* to *why*.
3. **Quantify with a parallel-count inventory** — list 3–4 infrastructure categories with rounded figures (centres, stations, workforce), and add a "(plus X [auxiliary category])" parenthetical for texture. The counts should *answer* the metaphor you set up next to them.
4. **Open the inventory paragraph with a metaphor of entrenchment** — use a verb like *woven / embedded / rooted / fused* so the reader expects the list that follows.
5. **Translate one number into a tangible analogy** — pick the single most striking measurement, state it absolutely, then give a familiar comparison (football fields, city blocks, swimming pools). Use an em-dash parenthetical to package it cleanly.
6. **Promote to a symbolic verdict** — follow the analogy with a sentence that uses "have come to symbolize" + colon + label. This is the move that lifts your section from description to *thesis-grade* claim.
7. **Hand off with a named individual and a date** — end on a temporal subordinate clause ("When X visited in month") that launches the next section, even if it doesn't resolve inside this one.

The pattern works because each move creates a *gap* (a comparison, a "why," a "how big," a "so what," a "who") that the next move closes, so the reader is always pulled forward by a question they didn't realise they were asking.
