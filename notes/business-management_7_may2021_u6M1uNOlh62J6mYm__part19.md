## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **transcribed interview (Q&A) section**, so its content moves follow a fixed interviewer–interviewee rhythm, not an expository one. The sequence:

1. **Topic-anchor question** — names the practice under discussion (equipment / lessons concession). *Why first:* it situates the reader in the specific issue before any stance is given.
2. **Admission of prior practice** — the interviewee concedes it already happens but in limited form. *Why second:* establishes authenticity before limitations are raised.
3. **Mechanism / limitation (cost)** — explains *why* uptake is restricted (financial barrier). *Why third:* converts the admission into a structured problem.
4. **Current scope** — names who currently benefits (year 10s). *Why fourth:* contrasts the narrow present with the wider "what if."
5. **Wish / desire statement** — declares the department would want more. *Why fifth:* sets up the interviewee's forthcoming verdict on expansion.
6. **Verdict on feasibility** — admits the problem is unsolved. *Why sixth:* signals that the next question will pivot from feasibility to impact.
7. **Interviewer pivot to impact** — broadens from "can we?" to "what would it do?" *Why seventh:* reshapes the interview axis.
8. **Positive premise + boundary** — interviewee names an upside, then sets a hard limit. *Why eighth:* classic "yes, but" structure: builds trust before constraining.
9. **Time-bound specification** — converts the abstract support into a concrete window (winter / half-term). *Why ninth:* the interviewee refuses the binary yes/no and imposes a seasonal frame.
10. **Seasonal example + counter-example** — "winter months, brilliant" / "whole year, not suitable." *Why tenth:* anchors the time-bound verdict in tangible periods so the reader can picture it.
11. **Sub-pivot to a sub-population (teachers)** — interviewer isolates one variable. *Why eleventh:* having settled on the time-frame, the interview zooms into motivation.
12. **Counter-intuitive claim** — denies that motivation would change. *Why twelfth:* rejects the interviewer's framing, which is itself a deliberate analytical move.
13. **Behavioural evidence** — volunteers, weekends, extra time. *Why thirteenth:* substantiates the claim without statistics.
14. **Generalising verdict** — extends the claim to the whole department. *Why fourteenth:* seals the answer and invites the next (cut-off) question.

The *order* matters because each step narrows the scope (general practice → cost → who → when → who within PE → equipment). A student replicating this would first establish what exists and why it's limited, before layering verdicts on top.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The dominant explanation pattern in this section is **"qualified verdict"** — a position that *admits the upside, refuses the extreme, then re-affirms support inside a time-window*. It is not definition→unpack→example, and not authority→application. It is closer to a **"yes-but-with-specification" chain** stacked on top of a **"refusing the premise with behavioural evidence"** pattern.

Steps to explain a NEW idea with the same pattern:

1. **Open with a partial admission.** Use "Yes, this is actually something we have started [time] ago" to concede the practice exists; signal immediately that a limitation follows.
2. **Name the mechanism of limitation.** Attach "however, it requires [resource] and is difficult to [verb] for everybody to [action]." This converts the admission into a structured barrier rather than leaving it as a flat denial.
3. **Specify the current scope.** State who already benefits ("We do this for [subset] already"). This sets up the contrast with the broader case that follows.
4. **Express a conditional wish.** Use "generally we would appreciate it if there was more involvement." Keep the verb softened ("would appreciate," not "demand").
5. **Issue an honest "I do not know" verdict on the macro question.** "How this is going to be [adjective] or solvable I do not know." This closes the feasibility half and invites the interviewer to pivot.
6. **Pivot the topic to effect (when interviewed) or to a sub-axis (when writing).** Move from "can we?" to "what would it do?"
7. **State the upside in conditional future.** "we would be doing X in a nicer / more professional environment."
8. **Reject the extreme version.** Use "I do not think that [institution] should take over…" to bound the argument.
9. **Re-affirm conditional support.** "We as a [group] would be in favour of something like this, however."
10. **Impose a time-window with a cause clause.** "such a program could not be established for longer than [duration] because of [competing program]."
11. **Give one seasonal example as best-case.** "During [season], it would be [positive adjective]."
12. **Close with the year-round rejection.** "But for the whole year, it is not a suitable solution."
13. **(If challenged on motivation/premise)** Open the next move with "I personally believe that the [group] are already pretty [quality]," then list 2–3 volunteered actions, then generalise: "everyone's currently quite [quality], although [trailing concession]."

The key *move*: every claim is paired with a **boundary** (cost, season, group, duration). No verdict is ever unqualified, which is what gives this section its IB-grade-7 analytical texture.
