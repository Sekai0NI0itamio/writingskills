## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **Evaluation of Limitations** section organised as a numbered list where each number is a self-contained *assumption → consequence → mechanism → verdict* block. The ordered moves are:

1. **Name the simplification/assumption** (1st). Sets the target of critique so the reader knows what is being interrogated.
2. **Project the counterfactual consequence** (2nd). Shows *why the assumption matters* by imagining what would follow if it were treated as true.
3. **Supply the physical mechanism** (3rd). Anchors the consequence in a named process (conduction, thermal equilibrium, property loss), converting a vague worry into a causal explanation.
4. **Escalate to a validity verdict** (4th, sometimes absent). Connects the mechanism back to whether the experiment's *conclusion* can be trusted in a real-engine context.

That order works because each move *earns* the next: you cannot show a consequence without first stating the assumption; you cannot justify the consequence without a mechanism; you cannot tie mechanism to verdict without first having a consequence to escalate. A student replicating this with another topic (e.g. evaluating a projectile-motion experiment) should keep this four-beat rhythm intact while swapping the physical content.

**SKELETON A — Assumption Critique with Mechanism (3-sentence form)**
> "The [relationship between A and B] [is/is not] [flawed claim]. In a scenario where [the assumption] would be considered, it would result in [quantity] being [problem]. That results because [mechanism: physical process + endpoint condition]."

1. **Slot 1** (full sentence, claim): state a *negation* of an assumption the experiment relies on. Grammatical shape: "The [X and Y] doesn't affect [Z]."
2. **Slot 2** (conditional sentence, counterfactual): introduce with "In a scenario where…" and state the downstream inaccuracy. Grammatical shape: conditional + future-in-the-past ("would result in").
3. **Slot 3** (causal sentence, mechanism): open with "That results because…" and name a *transfer process* plus an *equilibrium condition*. Grammatical shape: "the [entity]'s [property] will pass to [other entity] by [process], until [endpoint]."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / counterfactual setup**: "In a scenario where the assumption would be considered, it would result in…" (introduces a hypothetical to expose a flaw).
- **Cause / consequence**: "This would further affect the final velocity" — escalates a physical effect; "This would further lead the conclusion to be inaccurate" — escalates a methodological verdict.
- **Mechanism connectors**: "That results because…" — pivots from consequence to physics; "until both temperatures achieve thermal equilibrium" — closes the mechanism with an endpoint state.
- **Concession / assumption negation**: "doesn't affect the lubricant temperature" — flags the simplification being challenged.
- **Specification / numerical anchoring**: "300°C", "20°C to 55°C", "25m/s" — bracket claims with concrete values.
- **Evidence handling**: "The research found out that…" — invokes external authority without naming the source.
- **Explanation verbs / physics terms**: "by conduction", "thermal equilibrium", "lose its properties", "dynamic coefficient of friction" — name processes, not just outcomes.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The dominant pattern in this section is **Assumption-Critique-with-Mechanism** (a four-beat chain: *state assumption → project counterfactual consequence → name the physical mechanism → escalate to a verdict on validity*). To reproduce it on a new idea, follow these steps in order:

1. **Isolate one assumption** your experiment made. Write it as a claim sentence, typically negating what the experiment *pretended* was true (e.g. "The contact between X and Y doesn't affect Z").
2. **Run the counterfactual.** Open with "In a scenario where the assumption would be considered," then state what *would go wrong* with a measured quantity ("it would result in [quantity] being inaccurate"). Use future-in-the-past ("would") to keep it hypothetical.
3. **Drop down to mechanism.** Pivot with "That results because…" and name one transferable physical process plus the *endpoint* it drives the system toward (e.g. "internal energy will pass… by conduction, until… thermal equilibrium").
4. **Escalate to the verdict** (optional, for stronger limitations). Add a "This would lead the experiment to be incapable of…" sentence, then close with "This would further lead the conclusion to be [flawed] due to the lack of [real-world condition]."
5. **Bracket every claim with a number** wherever possible — a temperature, a percentage, a velocity. Specificity is what makes the mechanism credible; vagueness collapses the chain.

Following beats 1→2→3→4 in that order, with explicit "because" and "would further" connectives, reproduces the section's logic path on any new experimental topic.
