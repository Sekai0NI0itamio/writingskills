## What This Section Does (content sequence)

The page is a hybrid: a Table of Figures index followed by the opening of the Hypotheses section.

For the **Table of Figures block** (moves 1.1–1.12):

1. First, the *study site* figures (location maps, country, county, satellite view) — so a reader can orient where the fieldwork happened.
2. Then, the *theoretical model* (Bradshaw Model) — so the reader has the framework before seeing the data.
3. Then, the *equipment and sampling-method* figures — so the reader knows how the data were generated.
4. Then, the *measurement-instrument* figure (Power's Roundness Index) — so the reader can interpret shape values later.
5. Finally, the *data-result figures* in the order they appear in the report (particle size → volume → shape → hydraulic radius → velocity) — so each figure is referenced as the discussion reaches it.

Why this order: it follows page-order in the document, and page-order is itself sequenced so that *context (site → theory → method) is delivered before results*. The list therefore doubles as a guided reading path.

For the **Hypotheses opener** (moves 2.1–3.3):

1. First, a section label *"A. HYPOTHESES"* to signal the move from context into testable claims.
2. Then, a *labelled claim* stating direction of change for the dependent variable(s).
3. Then, the *condition* on the independent variable that triggers that change.
4. Then, the *mechanism* that explains the direction causally.
5. (Often) a *"That is"* pivot that defines the key term before the reader encounters it in the data.

Why this order: direction first lets the reader know *what* is being tested; condition second anchors the test in *where along the river*; mechanism third supplies the *geographic reasoning* that makes the hypothesis defensible; definition last ensures the term used in the data chapter is unambiguous.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — Single Table-of-Figures entry**

SKELETON: `"Fig. [N]: [Descriptive Caption] [(optional bracketed source]) ........... [page]"`

1. **Slot 1 (Fig. N)** — a numeral; grammatical shape = cardinal number.
2. **Slot 2 (Caption)** — a noun phrase naming what is shown, no full sentence; grammatical shape = noun phrase with optional prepositional modifier.
3. **Slot 3 (Source)** — a bracketed reference number `[#]`; used only when the figure is adapted/borrowed.
4. **Slot 4 (Page)** — arabic page numeral, right-aligned by dotted leader.

*How to fill with a different idea:* keep slot 1 as the next number in your document order; pick the simplest noun phrase that names what the reader will see (a map, a model, a photograph, a chart); add a bracketed source only if the figure is borrowed; right-align the page number on which the figure appears.

*Original filled:* "Fig. 1: Map of the Glenderaterra Beck ...................................................................................... 1"

*Demo fill:* "Fig. 1: Photograph of the sand dunes at Formby Point [4] ........................................................ 1"

---

**SKELETON B — Single-variable, mechanism-led hypothesis**

SKELETON: `"[The DV] [direction] as [the IV changes], due to [process]."`

1. **Slot 1 (DV + direction)** — declarative clause with the dependent variable as subject and a directional verb (decreases / increases / becomes more X).
2. **Slot 2 (IV condition)** — prepositional or subordinate clause starting with *"as"*, naming the spatial/temporal gradient.
3. **Slot 3 (Mechanism)** — prepositional phrase beginning *"due to"*, naming the named process from the syllabus (attrition, hydraulic action, refraction, infiltration…).

*How to fill with a different idea:* pick one DV your study measures, choose its predicted direction, write a gradient phrase ("as you move down-coast", "as altitude rises"), and close with a syllabus-process noun.

*Original filled:* "The particle size decreases, and shape increases as the river distances from the source due to attrition."

*Demo fill:* "Soil moisture decreases as the slope angle steepens, due to increased throughflow."

---

**SKELETON C — Condition → claim → "That is" definition pivot (two-clause hypothesis)**

SKELETON: `"As [IV] increases, [DV] increases. That is [, definition/elaboration of DV]."`

1. **Slot 1 (IV condition)** — *"As [distance/variable] increases/decreases"*; subordinate clause fronting the gradient.
2. **Slot 2 (DV claim)** — `[DV name] [directional verb]`; main clause mirroring the conditional structure.
3. **Slot 3 (Specification pivot)** — *"That is"* followed by an appositive or defining clause for the DV term.

*How to fill with a different idea:* keep the conditional fronting, name a DV whose definition a non-specialist reader may not know, and use *"That is"* as the door to a glossary-style definition before the term appears in the data figures.

*Original filled (cut off after the pivot):* "As the distance downstream increases, the Hydraulic Radius increases. That is"

*Demo fill:* "As the proportion of impermeable surface rises, the runoff coefficient increases. That is, the ratio of surface runoff to total rainfall."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

The section is sparse on connectives because most of it is an index. The working vocabulary that *does* move the logic forward is:

- **Sequencing / position** — implicit, expressed through ordered numbering and page-anchoring: *"Fig. 1: Map of the Glenderaterra Beck"* (page locator) → *"Fig. 12: Hydroprop Velocity"* (terminal entry).
- **Source handling** — bracketed numerals mark borrowed figures: *"Fig. 2: Cumbria in England [1]"* and *"Fig. 3: Cumbria - screenshot from Google Maps [2]"* and *"Fig. 6: Power's Roundness Index [3]"*.
- **Cause / consequence** — *"due to attrition"* (mechanism marker inside hypothesis 1).
- **Condition / gradient** — *"as the river distances from the source"* and *"As the distance downstream increases"* (temporal/spatial gradient pivots).
- **Specification / definition setup** — *"That is"* (pivot that introduces the unpacking of "Hydraulic Radius" before the source cuts off).

There is no contrast/concession vocabulary on this page, because a hypothesis opener commits the writer to a predicted direction rather than acknowledging alternatives.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern on this page is **index-by-page-and-source for figures**, plus **direction + gradient + mechanism for hypotheses**. Combined into a single replication procedure:

1. **Build the figure list first**, *before* writing the hypotheses, so you know which visual evidence will exist.
2. **Order entries by page number**, not by topic, because the reader flips by page.
3. **Write each caption as a noun phrase** that names what the figure shows — no full sentence, no verbs of display.
4. **Add a bracketed source only for borrowed figures**; leave it off for originals.
5. **Right-align the page number** with a dotted leader so the eye can scan vertically.
6. **Open the Hypotheses section with a label** (*"A. HYPOTHESES"*) to signal a shift from context to testable claims.
7. **Write each hypothesis as a directional claim first** — name the DV and say how it changes (increases/decreases/becomes more X).
8. **Add the gradient clause second** — *"as [IV] increases/decreases"* — so the reader knows the spatial or temporal axis the claim rides on.
9. **Close with the mechanism third** — *"due to [named process]"* — so the claim is anchored to a syllabus concept, not just an observation.
10. **Use *"That is"* to pivot into a definition** when the DV term is technical (Hydraulic Radius, runoff coefficient, etc.), placing the definition where the reader first meets the term.
