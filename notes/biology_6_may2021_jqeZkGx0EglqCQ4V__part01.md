## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1 (mechanism → comparator → transition)**

1. *Context-anchor (carry-over)* — quotes the end of a prior mechanism ("leaves by preventing the breakdown of proteins and chlorophyll and stabilizing the"). This anchors the reader to the hormone-side story already told and hands off by **defining what concentration levels cause which organ**. ("…low cytokinin concentrations give rise to root development,")
2. *Inverse contrast* — pivots with "whereas the inverse will promote the development of the shoot (Britannica)." The word "whereas" hands the reader by **flipping the condition**: same hormones, swapped ratio, opposite organ. ("…the development of the shoot")
3. *Claim + source tag* — names the substitute ("Lauric acid is a type of saturated fatty acid that can act as a substitute for BAP"). Hands off by **introducing a new actor** that needs its own properties described. ("…substitute for BAP and have similar effects.")

**Paragraph 2 (property → mechanism → implication)**

1. *Physical property* — "Lauric acid is a solid at room temperature but is very soluble in boiling water." Hands forward by **raising a handling question** (how do you get a solid into soil?) that the next sentence won't answer but that the reader now files away.
2. *Definition + source* — quoted molecular fingerprint ("The molecule is a saturated medium-chain fatty acid with a 12-carbon backbone"). Hands off by **specifying what category** the substance belongs to, which then permits the next sentence to slot it into the hormone analogy. ("…a 12-carbon backbone")
3. *Source-of-occurrence* — "and can be found in plant and animal oils, mainly coconut and palm kernel oil (NCI)." Hands off by **bridging back to the hormone mechanism**: now that the reader knows what lauric acid IS and WHERE it comes from, the writer can claim it DOES what BAP does. ("…coconut and palm kernel oil")
4. *Functional parallel* — "Like BAP, lauric acid can react alongside auxins to impede early stage senescence." Hands off by **mirroring the first paragraph's pairing** (auxin + cytokinin → lauric acid + auxin), so the analogy lands.
5. *Added benefit (extension)* — "It also boosts meristematic tissue growth and promotes development." Hands off by **stacking a second effect**, which lets the hypothesis paragraph below claim *faster* development rather than only *some* development.

**Paragraph 3 (hypothesis declaration — bullet form)**

1. *Predictive claim* — "The solutions with the higher amounts of lauric acid will result in faster germination and development." Hands off to the variables list by **declaring what will be measured against what will be changed**. ("…faster germination and development.")

**Paragraph 4 (variables — bullet form)**

1. *Independent variable, defined by role* — "The variable that is being adjusted and tested is the concentration of lauric acid being put into the soil." Hands off by **mirroring the IV/DV pair** so the dependent variable can be stated as its counterpart. ("…concentration of lauric acid")

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This section type is the **"substitute-introduction + operational setup"** block of a bio experiment write-up. The moves, in order, are:

1. **Finish the established mechanism** (carry the cytokinin/auxin ratio story to its natural endpoint so the reader isn't left hanging).
2. **Introduce the test substance as a substitute for the standard hormone** (claim of equivalence — sets up *why* the test substance is even worth trying).
3. **Give the substance's basic chemistry** (solid/liquid, solubility, carbon-chain length — sets up whether it is even *usable* in the planned procedure).
4. **Cite where it naturally occurs** (grounds it in real sources — sets up the credibility of the effect claim).
5. **Re-state the hormone pairing in substitute terms** (lauric acid + auxin instead of BAP + auxin — sets up the predicted mechanism).
6. **Add a second, growth-promoting effect** (gives the hypothesis something extra to lean on).
7. **State the hypothesis as a directional prediction** (concentration ↑ → development ↑ — sets up what the variables must capture).
8. **Define the IV by its function** (what is changed).
9. **Define the DV by what is measured** (what is observed).

**Why this order works:** each move hands the reader a *handle* the next move can grab. Without finishing the standard mechanism first, the "substitute" claim is unmotivated. Without chemistry, the "can be dissolved and applied" step later is unjustified. Without citing a natural source, the equivalence claim reads as pulled from thin air. Without restating the pairing in substitute terms, the hypothesis has no mechanism behind it. Without the second effect, the hypothesis would only predict "some growth," not "faster" growth. And the IV/DV pair only makes sense once the reader already knows *what* is being varied and *what outcome* the writer cares about.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / carry-over**: none — this section is built on analogy and substitution, not on chronological order.
- **Cause / consequence**: "give rise to" ("…low cytokinin concentrations give rise to root development").
- **Contrast / concession**: "whereas the inverse" ("…whereas the inverse will promote the development of the shoot").
- **Specification / equivalence**: "Like BAP," ("Like BAP, lauric acid can react alongside auxins…"); "a type of saturated fatty acid that can act as a substitute for BAP" (definition-by-role).
- **Evidence handling**: "(Britannica)" and "(NCI)" — parenthetical citation tags attached directly to the clause they support, e.g. "found in plant and animal oils, mainly coconut and palm kernel oil (NCI)."
- **Explanation verbs**: "is a solid at room temperature but is very soluble in" (property contrast); "is defined by / is" (identity: "is a saturated medium-chain fatty acid"); "can be found in" (occurrence); "can act as a substitute for" (functional equivalence); "can react alongside" (mechanism verb — describes *how* two substances interact, not just *that* they do).
- **Stacking / addition**: "It also" ("It also boosts meristematic tissue growth…") — the writer's chosen device for adding a second supporting effect without breaking the rhythm of the previous sentence.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **"standard-mechanism → named-substitute → chemistry of substitute → re-pair in substitute terms → directional hypothesis → IV/DV" pattern**. To replicate it on a new idea, follow these steps in order:

1. **State the established mechanism in full**, including the conditions under which it operates and the conditions under which the *opposite* happens. Use "whereas the inverse will…" to flip the ratio — this is what makes the mechanism feel complete and gives the substitute something to be substituted *for*.
2. **Introduce the test substance with a definition-by-role sentence**: "[X] is a [category] that can act as a substitute for [standard agent]." The verb "act as a substitute" is the load-bearing connective; do not write "is similar to" or "resembles" — they do not commit the writer to functional equivalence.
3. **Cite (Britannica / NCI / PubChem / journal) immediately after the claim** so the reader cannot accuse the equivalence of being hand-waved. Put the citation in parentheses at the end of the clause, not in a footnote.
4. **Describe the substance's chemistry in two beats**: (a) physical state and solubility (handling properties), (b) a quoted or quasi-quoted chemical specification with a number ("12-carbon backbone," "80% deactivation," "molecular weight 282"). The number is what distinguishes a *measured* description from a *vague* one.
5. **Name where the substance occurs naturally**, naming the dominant source last ("mainly coconut and palm kernel oil"). This grounds the equivalence biologically.
6. **Restate the original pairing in substitute-agent terms** using "Like [standard], [test] can [verb] to [outcome]." This is the sentence that does the most conceptual work — it lets the reader transfer the mechanism they already accept onto the new substance.
7. **Add a second benefit with "It also…"** — never leave the substitute with only one proven effect; a single effect makes the hypothesis fragile, a stacked pair makes it defensible.
8. **Write the hypothesis as one comparative sentence** ("higher [IV] → faster [DV]"). Avoid qualifiers like "might possibly"; commit to a direction.
9. **Define the IV by its function** ("the variable that is being adjusted and tested is…") and the DV by its measurement unit + landmark ("the [unit] of the [specimen] from [anatomical point]"). The IV sentence borrows the "variable that is being adjusted and tested" phrasing because it is a definition the examiner cannot mark down; the DV sentence borrows the "from [landmark]" phrasing because it forces the writer to commit to a reproducible measurement protocol.

The pattern is: *finish the known story → swap in the new actor → describe the new actor → re-tell the known story with the new actor → predict → operationalise.* Every step exists to make the next step defensible.
