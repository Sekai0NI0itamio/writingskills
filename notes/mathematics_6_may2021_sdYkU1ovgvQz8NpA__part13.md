## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **State controlled conditions** (same thickness, matching radii pairs) — sets up the reader's trust that variation between samples is reduced to one variable.
2. **Quantify the manufactured sample set** (8 rings, 4 radii) — establishes the experimental range before any selection.
3. **Introduce notation formally** (ri, i ∈ {1,2,3,4}) — equips later sentences with reusable symbols.
4. **List the specific values** — operationalises the notation so the reader can plug numbers in.
5. **Select one value and justify the selection** — narrows the experiment to one case with a visible reason, so the verdict will be uncontested.
6. **Point at the figure** — defers visual evidence to a labelled image so the prose stays clean.
7. **Report how the fit-parameter was obtained** (trial and error + numeric pair) — gives the verdict a procedure.
8. **Deliver the verdict against the hypothesis** — names the surface (catenoid) and asserts the match.
9. **Concede the limit of the result** (one α only) — sets up the next move.
10. **Restate the wider aim** (behaviour for all α) — replaces the local result with a general one as the new target.
11. **Declare the algebraic method** — announces the tool that will bridge specific → general.
12. **Identify a boundary/anchor point** (x = α ⇒ y = r) — supplies the substitution that the algebraic step needs.
13. **Launch the derivation** ("Hence … the following") — hands to the calculation that closes the generalisation.

The order matters because each move sets the precondition for the next: you cannot select r1 until the radii are listed, you cannot deliver a verdict until C is found, and you cannot generalise until a boundary condition is anchored.

- **Concession / pivot:** "To investigate the equation, however, I have selected r1" — uses "however" to break from the catalogue and single one out.
- **Causal justification:** "as its radii is the biggest thus the easiest" — uses "thus" to bind visibility to size.
- **Evidence handling / method-reporting:** "The constant C for the image above was found using trial and error" — declares the procedure before the value.
- **Numeric precision:** "in particular, for the measured distance α ≈ 0.87" — narrows from procedure to the specific input–output pair.

- **Term-introduction:** "representing a 'catenoid,' the 3D rotated variant" — uses an appositive ("the … variant") to gloss the surface.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The dominant pattern is **"define the object as a known mathematical surface → concede a single-case limitation → re-aim for the general case → anchor a boundary substitution to launch a general derivation."** It is a *verdict-then-generalise* pattern, where the student first establishes the local fit before generalising.

Step-by-step replication with a new idea:

1. **Begin with a controlled-sample paragraph** that lists the values manufactured and notationally labels them, ending with a justified selection of one case — this pre-empts "why this one?" objections.
2. **Issue a single-sentence figure pointer** that defers visual comparison to a labelled image, keeping the prose uncluttered.
3. **State the fit-parameter and the numeric match** so the verdict is grounded in a reproducible number.
4. **Deliver the verdict in the form "as hypothesised, [object] is [known mathematical term],"** using an appositive to gloss the term for the reader who doesn't already know it.
5. **Insert a "however" concession** that narrows scope from "all values" to "one value," naming exactly what was and wasn't achieved.
6. **Restate the broader aim** ("for all α") so the reader sees the trajectory of the investigation.
7. **Declare the algebraic tool** ("To do this algebraically…") before using it, so the move feels chosen, not improvised.
8. **Anchor a boundary substitution** ("at x = bound I always have y = …") with a one-clause physical reason ("bounded by the radius"), so the substitution looks forced by reality, not asserted.
9. **Open the derivation with "Hence, … the following,"** handing off to the algebra that closes the generalisation.
