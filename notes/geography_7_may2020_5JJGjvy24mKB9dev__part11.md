## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A References section makes a fixed sequence of moves, and the order is conventional, not logical in the prose sense:

1. **Earliest academic or theoretical anchor** — sets the scholarly frame the essay will draw on.
2. **Institutional/policy report(s)** — supply the demographic and statistical backdrop.
3. **Local-authority fact-sheet cluster** — provides granular borough-level data; ordered either chronologically or by topic (economy → businesses → unemployment → income).
4. **Journalistic source** — supplies a contemporary concrete case to humanise the statistics.

WHY this order: it builds evidence from general/abstract (a conference paper) → comparative (two boroughs) → granular local statistics → a real present-day event. The reader who scans the bibliography should be able to infer what *kind* of argument the essay makes: a borough-scale economic/gentrification analysis grounded in both quantitative data and a single named incident.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Authored academic source**
   SKELETON: "[Author surname], [Initials] ([Year]). "[Article title]". In: [Conference/Journal name]. url: [link]."

   1. *Slot 1* (author): name + initials, surname-first, noun phrase.
   2. *Slot 2* (year): bracketed publication year.
   3. *Slot 3* (title): quoted article title, the thesis of the piece.
   4. *Slot 4* (venue): "In: " + venue name.
   5. *Slot 5* (access): "url: " + retrievable link.

   *Original fill:* "Agustı́n Cócola Gant (2015). 'Tourism and commercial gentrification'. In: RC21 International Conference. url: https://..."

   *Demonstration fill (different idea):* "Bhambra, Gurminder K. (2007). 'Postcolonial Europe — or, the dangers of a new Eurocentrism'. In: RC21 International Conference. url: https://..."

**Skeleton B — Institutional report (named body)**
   SKELETON: "[Institution name] ([Year]). [Report title: sub-title]. url: [link]."

   1. *Slot 1*: institution as author, no individual named.
   2. *Slot 2*: bracketed year.
   3. *Slot 3*: report title, often with a colon-separated subtitle scoping the topic.
   4. *Slot 4*: retrieval URL.

   *Original fill:* "New Policy Institute (2016). Demography and deprivation in Southwark and Tower Hamlets. url: https://..."

   *Demonstration fill:* "Joseph Rowntree Foundation (2019). Poverty and place in Bradford and Leeds: a comparative profile. url: https://..."

**Skeleton C — Repeated institutional author with em-dash shorthand**
   SKELETON: "— ([Year][letter]). [Report title: sub-title]. url: [link]."

   1. *Slot 1*: em-dash standing in for the institutional author above.
   2. *Slot 2*: year plus lowercase letter when several reports share one year.
   3. *Slot 3*: a new thematic title each time.
   4. *Slot 4*: URL.

   *Original fill:* "— (2013a). Business Demography – Birth, Death and Survival of Businesses in Tower Hamlets. url: https://..."

   *Demonstration fill:* "— (2017a). Housing Tenure and the Private Rented Sector in Hackney. url: https://..."

**Skeleton D — Newspaper article by named journalist**
   SKELETON: "[Surname], [First name] ([Year]). "[Headline with concrete claim]". In: [Newspaper name]."

   1. *Slot 1*: journalist, surname-first.
   2. *Slot 2*: bracketed year.
   3. *Slot 3*: headline quoted as the article title; the headline itself contains the concrete numerical or event claim.
   4. *Slot 4*: "In: " + paper name, terminating with a full stop.

   *Original fill:* "White, Tom (2019). 'Railway arch shops face eviction after rent rises of up to 85%'. In: The Guardian."

   *Demonstration fill:* "Booth, Robert (2022). 'Brixton market traders told to leave after 40% rent increase'. In: The Guardian."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

Because this is a bibliography, the "connectives" are bibliographic conventions rather than logical ones. Grouped by job:

- **Sequencing / repetition-shorthand:** the em-dash "—" used as a stand-in author across multiple entries from the same institution: "— (2013a)."
- **Identification markers (year+letter disambiguation):** "(2013a)", "(2013b)" — used when one body publishes more than one work in the same year.
- **Containment / placement:** "In:" — marks the larger venue (conference, newspaper): "In: RC21 International Conference"; "In: The Guardian."
- **Quotation framing of titles:** double quotation marks around article/headline titles, signalling the cited work's own title: "'Tourism and commercial gentrification'"; "'Railway arch shops face eviction…'".
- **Title-to-subtitle scope (colon as specification):** "Local Economic Assessment 2010 Fact Sheet Volume 2: Economy and Enterprise" — colon narrows from whole to part.
- **Access-handling verb-form:** "url:" — the only "verb-like" operator in the section, introducing the retrieval path: "url: https://..."

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

A References section does not "explain" — it *sources*. The pattern it relies on is **evidence-stack by type, narrowing from theory to incident**. Replicate with a new idea as follows:

1. **Open with one scholarly anchor** — a single conference paper or journal article that names the overarching concept your essay will use (e.g. gentrification → tourism studies).
2. **Add one comparative institutional report** — a body that compares two places or two datasets, giving the reader a comparative frame.
3. **Stack local-authority statistical fact-sheets** — at least three, each on a different sub-topic of the borough/area, ordered so they read like a profile (economy → businesses → unemployment → income → housing).
4. **Use the em-dash shorthand** for every fact-sheet after the first from the same institution, so the bibliography signals that one body is the recurring data source.
5. **Close with one journalistic source** — a named reporter and a dated article whose headline contains a concrete, specific figure or event (rent rise %, eviction, closure), so the empirical data ends on a human-scale case.
6. **Verify the rhythm**: the reader scanning down the page should encounter scholar → institute → council cluster → journalist, in that order, without ever re-reading the institution name.
