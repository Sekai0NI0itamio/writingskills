## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (fragment)
- Sentence 1: "eyewitness testimonies, where people are unwittingly being deceived by their memories." → **definition / phenomenon framing** — names the object of study (eyewitness testimonies) and the operative mechanism (unwitting deception by memory). Hands to the next paragraph by setting up the *target phenomenon* which then needs to be evaluated methodologically — the reader expects the writer to now assess how well the study can actually capture that deception.

**Paragraph 2**
- Sentence 1: "A strength of our opportunity sample is that it contained 13 males and 11 females, representative of the gender proportion in our schools' IGCSE students, ensuring high population validity." → **claim of strength with quantified evidence** ("13 males and 11 females") → **justification via match** ("representative of the gender proportion") → **consequence for validity** ("ensuring high population validity"). Hands to sentence 2 by completing the strength; the reader anticipates either more strengths or a counter, and the writer uses this as a setup so the upcoming limitation feels balanced rather than damning.
- Sentence 2: "However, due to the participants' age, they were not eligible for a driving license (legal age is 18), which is problematic because the questionnaires required them to answer a question associated with driving; something which they (probably) did not have much experience with." → **concessive pivot** ("However") → **cause** ("due to the participants' age") → **factual specification** ("legal age is 18") → **problem-statement chain** ("problematic because the questionnaires required them to answer… driving") → **soft epistemic hedge on the deficit** ("(probably) did not have much experience"). Hands to sentence 3 because the inexperience just flagged is left dangling — the reader now expects the writer to say *what that inexperience actually does to the data*.
- Sentence 3: "This means that they would have not been strongly familiar with certain speeds; they could have had a lack of schema regarding what 60km/h would visually look like in a real-life crash." → **consequence/implication** ("This means") → **specification of the cognitive deficit** ("not been strongly familiar with certain speeds") → **technical unpacking** ("lack of schema") → **concrete operationalisation** ("what 60km/h would visually look like in a real-life crash"). Hands to sentence 4 because the implication has now made the limitation concrete; the reader expects either a verdict on impact or a fix, and the writer pivots to a fix.
- Sentence 4: "Using 18-year old IB students as part of our sample would be a good" → **recommendation / proposed improvement** (truncated in the text, but the move is clearly "suggest a remediation"). The sentence is left incomplete in the excerpt — the logical handshake it sets up is the *positive counterfactual* (what the sample *should* have been).

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **evaluation of sampling** subsection of a methodology/evaluation block. The ordered moves are:

1. **Phenomenon reminder** — briefly re-anchor the reader to *what is being studied* (memory distortion in eyewitness testimony).
2. **State one strength with numbers** — anchor credibility by giving a concrete feature (gender split) and linking it to a named validity type ("population validity"). *Why first:* a strength must precede the limitation so the evaluation reads as balanced rather than as a takedown.
3. **Concessive pivot to a limitation** ("However") — introduce a sample weakness. *Why second:* IB evaluators expect a paired strength–limitation structure; the pivot creates the contrast that makes the limitation stick.
4. **Cause chain inside the limitation** — explain *why* the weakness matters via factual specification (legal age = 18) and methodological demand (question about driving). *Why third:* a bare limitation sounds like opinion; tying it to a concrete mismatch between sample and instrument makes the criticism principled.
5. **Implication with a technical term** — translate the cause chain into a cognitive consequence using course terminology ("schema"). *Why fourth:* the criterion is methodological but the consequence is *psychological*, and naming it shows the student can connect method to theory.
6. **Suggested improvement** — offer a positive counterfactual (older students). *Why last:* the improvement closes the loop and signals that the writer is not just critical but constructive.

A student replicating this with a different topic should follow: phenomenon → numbered strength + validity term → "However" → cause chain (characteristic → instrument mismatch) → theory-worded implication → improvement.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — Strength-with-evidence sentence**
> "A strength of our [sample type] is that it [contained/showed specific numerical feature], [descriptor of how it matches the population], ensuring high [validity type]."

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: sample type noun phrase (e.g., "opportunity sample").
   - Slot 2: a concrete quantified descriptor in past tense (e.g., "contained 13 males and 11 females").
   - Slot 3: a comparative clause linking to the population (e.g., "representative of the gender proportion in our school's…").
   - Slot 4: a present participle + named validity ("ensuring high population validity").
2. **How to fill differently:** Pick one demographic or procedural feature of *your* sample that genuinely mirrors the population you sampled from; state the count or proportion; name the population it mirrors; attach the IB validity term that fits (population, ecological, temporal — pick the one the feature actually supports).
3. **Original fill:** "A strength of our opportunity sample is that it contained 13 males and 11 females, representative of the gender proportion in our schools' IGCSE students, ensuring high population validity."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea — sleep & memory study):** "A strength of our opportunity sample is that it contained 9 morning-type and 11 evening-type participants, proportional to the chronotype distribution reported by our sixth-form's self-declared survey, ensuring high population validity."

**SKELETON B — Limitation + cause-chain sentence**
> "However, due to [participant characteristic], [factual specification of the constraint], which is problematic because [the instrument required something they lacked]; something which they (probably) did not [verb of relevant experience] with."

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: "However," concession marker.
   - Slot 2: "due to" + a participant feature (age, language, prior exposure).
   - Slot 3: parenthetical factual anchor (legal/factual number or rule).
   - Slot 4: "which is problematic because" + the *task demand* that doesn't fit.
   - Slot 5: "something which they (probably) did not" + past-tense experience verb.
2. **How to fill differently:** Identify one mismatch between *who your participants are* and *what your study asked them to do*. State the demographic constraint, anchor it with a concrete rule/number in parentheses, then specify the task demand that breaks under that constraint, then end with the experience verb (e.g., "have much experience with").
3. **Original fill:** "However, due to the participants' age, they were not eligible for a driving license (legal age is 18), which is problematic because the questionnaires required them to answer a question associated with driving; something which they (probably) did not have much experience with."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea — conformity using Asch-style line judgments):** "However, due to the participants' age, they had only completed one year of sixth-form (joined in September), which is problematic because the critical trials required comparison against peers who had been classmates for over twelve months; something which they (probably) did not have enough relational familiarity with."

**SKELETON C — Implication + technical-term sentence**
> "This means that they would have not been [cognitively familiar with X]; they could have had a lack of [theory term] regarding what [specific stimulus] would [manifest] in [the studied context]."

1. **Slots:**
   - Slot 1: "This means that" consequence marker.
   - Slot 2: modal-perfect phrase stating the cognitive deficit ("would have not been strongly familiar with…").
   - Slot 3: "they could have had a lack of" + IB theory term (schema, working-memory capacity, prior knowledge, etc.).
   - Slot 4: "regarding what" + concrete numerical/operational stimulus.
   - Slot 5: verb phrase of perception/response + "in" + context phrase.
2. **How to fill differently:** Translate the cause-chain's "lack of experience" into a *cognitive* consequence using one course-specific term; ground the term in the actual stimulus your study used (a number, a duration, an image).
3. **Original fill:** "This means that they would have not been strongly familiar with certain speeds; they could have had a lack of schema regarding what 60km/h would visually look like in a real-life crash."
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea — perception study on colour afterimages):** "This means that they would have not been strongly attuned to subtle hue shifts; they could have had a lack of chromatic-adaptation schema regarding what 480nm would perceptually look like after sustained fixation on its complement."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Concession / pivot:** "However, due to the participants' age, they were not eligible for a driving license"
- **Cause / mechanism:** "due to the participants' age, they were not eligible for a driving license (legal age is 18), which is problematic because the questionnaires required them"
- **Consequence / implication:** "This means that they would have not been strongly familiar with certain speeds"
- **Evidence handling / claim:** "A strength of our opportunity sample is that it contained 13 males and 11 females… ensuring high population validity" (number → validity claim, not a direct citation but the same *number-to-validity* inferential move)
- **Specification / parenthetical factual anchor:** "(legal age is 18)"
- **Epistemic hedge:** "something which they (probably) did not have much experience with"
- **Theory-term bridge:** "they could have had a lack of schema regarding what 60km/h"
- **Definition verb:** "ensuring high population validity" (treated as a defining consequence for the named validity type)

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section runs the **"balanced evaluation with cause-chain implication"** pattern. To apply it to a NEW idea (e.g., a limitation of a *different* sample or instrument), follow these steps in order:

1. **Anchor the phenomenon** in one short clause so the reader knows what is being studied and what cognitive bias/process is under threat.
2. **Name one strength** of the sample/method using the formula "A strength of [X] is that it [Y]" — *Y* must be quantified or concrete, not vague.
3. **Link strength to a named validity type** with "ensuring high [validity]." Pick the validity the strength actually supports (population, ecological, temporal, internal — be honest).
4. **Pivot with "However,"** then introduce a *single* sample characteristic that does not fit the task ("due to [characteristic]").
5. **Anchor the characteristic with a factual specification in parentheses** (a legal age, a school rule, a minimum threshold) — this converts opinion into constraint.
6. **Use "which is problematic because"** to name the *task demand* that the characteristic breaks — be specific about what the instrument required.
7. **Hedge the deficit with "(probably)"** if the limitation is inferred rather than directly measured — this is the IB-mature move.
8. **Translate the experience deficit into a cognitive consequence** using "This means that they would have not been [cognitively familiar with…]" — frame it as a participant-internal state, not a procedural complaint.
9. **Insert one course-theory term** ("lack of schema," "insufficient prior knowledge," "limited cognitive load capacity") so the implication lives on the *psychology* side of the rubric.
10. **Ground the theory term in the actual stimulus** ("what 60km/h would visually look like in a real-life crash") — without this grounding the theory word is decoration.
11. **Close with a positive counterfactual** ("Using [alternative sample]… would be a good…") so the evaluation ends constructively, not nihilistically.
