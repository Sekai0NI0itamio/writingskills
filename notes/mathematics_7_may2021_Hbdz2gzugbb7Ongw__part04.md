## Express-Idea Vocabulary
- **Positioning/comparison** — "It serves as an alternative to F = ma" → frames the new formalism against the familiar one.
- **General claim** — "It often involves several variables, and has to use partial derivatives" → flags mathematical complexity before symbols appear.
- **Unpack/operationalise** — "This means the differentiation is focused on one variable" → translates a term into a procedure.
- **Variable naming** — "In the equations, qj is a generalised coordinate, and L is the Lagrangian" → assigns meaning to each symbol in parallel grammar.
- **Signposting forward** — "It is defined below (Morin, 2007):" → cues the reader that the formal definition is coming next, often with citation.
- **Term expansion** — "T is the kinetic energy (which depends on mass m and speed v)" → names the symbol's physical identity and its arguments in one move.
- **Dependency claim** — "The potential energy depends on the existing force" → flags that the term is context-sensitive.
- **Case-transition example** — "In the case of an object under a gravitational field:" → narrows from the general equation to one worked instance.
- **Geometric/physical definition** — "Where y is the height" → translates a math symbol into a concrete physical role.
- **Reference caveat** — "Its value depends on the reference level (where potential energy is defined as zero) chosen" → closes by flagging a convention the reader must respect.
- **Citation handling** — "(Morin, 2007)" / "(Susskind, …, Hrabovsky, 2020)" → source attached at the point of borrowing, not at the end.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is: **frame-as-alternative → show equation → flag-and-unpack the hard math → name symbols → expand the core term → give one worked instance → close with a reference caveat.**

Steps to replicate with a new idea:

1. **Open with the competitor.** State your central result as "an alternative to" the standard result the reader already knows. One sentence, comparison verb.
2. **Display the equation.** Put the full formula on its own line; let it speak before you explain it.
3. **Flag the mathematically unusual feature.** Name in one sentence the calculus/notation/operation the equation requires that the simpler version did not.
4. **Unpack that feature.** One sentence starting "This means …" that translates the flagged feature into an operational rule.
5. **Name every symbol in the equation.** Use a parallel "X is a …, and Y is …" sentence so the reader can map each letter to a physical role.
7. **Signpost and define the core sub-term.** Use "It is defined below / given by / expanded as" and display the supporting equation.
8. **Expand each sub-term in words.** For each sub-term, say "T is the [name] (which depends on [var] and [var])" — name + variables in one parenthetical.
9. **Flag one sub-term's context-dependence.** One sentence: "The [sub-term] depends on [the active force/agent]."
10. **Instantiate with one case.** "In the case of [concrete scenario]:" and show the substituted equation.
11. **Translate the new symbols in that case.** "Where [symbol] is the [plain physical role]."
12. **Close with the convention caveat.** "[Symbol]'s value depends on the [reference] (where it is defined as zero) chosen."

The rhythm to preserve: one equation per move, one sentence of plain-English translation per move, and the move chain runs *abstract → symbols → sub-terms → instance → caveat* without skipping a step.
