## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (one paragraph, five sentences)

1. **Move = personal context (concrete lived experience).** Quote: *"Living in the North required its own precautions: I wore minimum 3 layers of clothing"*. Sets the stage as a first-person encounter with the phenomenon (cold). Hands the reader to S2 by **exposing a gap**: coping action is shown, but the *reason* behind the danger is not yet named — a "however" pivot invites curiosity.

2. **Move = curiosity pivot / research question.** Quote: *"what dangers does the cold really pose in our body?"*. Uses the contrast connector *However* to break from the practical coping of S1 and re-frame the section as inquiry. Hands the reader to S3 by **inviting a personal-observation answer** — a felt, bodily response.

3. **Move = personal observation as informal evidence.** Quote: *"my heart beats faster in order to keep my body warm"*. Narrows from "the cold in general" to a specific body response, using *in order to* to imply mechanism. Hands the reader to S4 by **introducing the heart as the focal organ**, which S4 then defines.

4. **Move = function statement + counter-question.** Quote: *"the function of the heart is to transport blood around the body, however, what would happen if this blood got too cold?"*. Confirms S3 using *Indeed*, then destabilises with *however* by introducing a **conditional disruption**. Hands the reader to S5 because the counter-question demands a researched answer.

5. **Move = external evidence / source-based findings.** Quote: *"Quick research leads me a link to the viscosity of the blood causing a clot"*. Closes the question chain with statistics and a citation (Omega, 2016). Hands the reader to the **next section** by satisfying the curiosity while leaving the biological mechanism itself still to be unpacked.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a single-paragraph Introduction performing the moves:

1. **Lived context first** — establishes a concrete, personal encounter with the topic so the reader sees the writer "in" the phenomenon.
2. **Curiosity pivot** — converts the anecdote into a research question.
3. **Personal observation** — supplies a felt, body-level clue that hints at the mechanism.
4. **Mechanism function + counter-question** — names what the body part *does* and then asks what fails when disrupted.
5. **External evidence** — delivers statistics and a citation to anchor the question in published data.

**Why this order:** the anecdote earns the writer's right to ask; the question gives the section direction; the observation narrows to a mechanism; the function statement makes the mechanism testable; the counter-question forces a search; the statistics finally justify the investigation. Each move shrinks the topic from "cold weather" down to "a specific cardiovascular mechanism under cold stress", which is exactly what an Introduction must do before the analysis proper can begin.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### SKELETON A — Anecdote-to-Evidence Introduction

**SKELETON:** "[Lived context with concrete detail about coping with phenomenon]. [However], [wondering question about the real danger]. [Personal observation hinting at the mechanism]. [Indeed], [the function of the body part] is to [core verb], [however], [what would happen if disruption]? [Quick research] [leads to evidence] such as [statistic 1]; [statistic 2]; and [statistic 3] (Author, Year)."

**Slots:**
- *Slot 1* — Past-tense personal narrative with one concrete number/detail.
- *Slot 2* — Contrast connector + open-ended research question in first person.
- *Slot 3* — First-person bodily observation using *in order to* to imply purpose.
- *Slot 4* — Confirmation connector + definition of the organ's function + contrast connector + conditional counter-question.
- *Slot 5* — Research lead-in + three parallel statistics separated by semicolons + parenthetical citation.

**Original filled version:** *"Living in the North required its own precautions: I wore minimum 3 layers of clothing every day when commuting to school to protect against the cold in the mountainous areas. However, I have always wondered, what dangers does the cold really pose in our body? I have always noticed that my heart beats faster in order to keep my body warm. Indeed, the function of the heart is to transport blood around the body, however, what would happen if this blood got too cold? Quick research leads me a link to the viscosity of the blood causing a clot within veins, causing direct health concerns and facts such as that for each 1.8°F…"*

**Demonstration fill (different idea — dehydration in athletes):**
"Training in the highlands required its own precautions: I drank at least 4 litres of water each session when running at altitude to avoid dehydration in the thin, dry air. However, I have always wondered, what dangers does dehydration really pose inside our cells? I have always felt my muscles cramp mid-race in order to compensate for lost fluid. Indeed, the function of water is to shuttle electrolytes across cell membranes, however, what would happen if these membranes shrank from osmotic stress? Quick research leads me to findings on electrolyte imbalance causing muscular fatigue, with evidence such as that losing just 2% of body mass in fluid can reduce sprint performance by roughly 25%; that heat-related hospital admissions climb 38% during summer marathons; and that the highest dehydration-induced cramp risk appears within hours of prolonged exertion (Patel, 2020)."

### SKELETON B — Mechanism + Counter-Question

**SKELETON:** "[Indeed/Clearly], [the function of X] is to [core action], [however], [what would happen if Y]?"

**Slots:**
- *Slot 1* — Confirmation connective.
- *Slot 2* — Subject + "is to" + infinitive verb of core function.
- *Slot 3* — Contrast connective.
- *Slot 4* — Conditional clause ending in question mark.

**Original filled version:** *"Indeed, the function of the heart is to transport blood around the body, however, what would happen if this blood got too cold?"*

**Demonstration fill (different idea — photosynthesis):**
*"Clearly, the function of chlorophyll is to absorb photons in the thylakoid membrane, however, what would happen if these photons struck at wavelengths the pigment could not capture?"*

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / ordering:** none used — the section relies on logical pivots rather than temporal order.
- **Cause / consequence / purpose:** *"in order to keep my body warm"* (purpose clause); *"causing a clot within veins, causing direct health concerns"* (chain of consequences).
- **Contrast / concession:** *"However, I have always wondered"* (anecdote → question); *"however, what would happen if this blood got too cold?"* (function → counter-question).
- **Confirmation / build:** *"Indeed, the function of the heart is to transport blood"* — confirms and specifies the preceding observation.
- **Specification / exemplification:** *"facts such as that for each 1.8°F or 1°C reduction"* — introduces parallel statistics.
- **Evidence handling:** *"Quick research leads me a link to the viscosity of the blood"* (lead-in to evidence); *"(Omega, 2016)"* (parenthetical citation).
- **Explanation verbs:** *"the function of the heart is to transport blood"* (definitional *is to*); *"causing a clot"* (causal gerund).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is **anecdote-driven scientific hook**: lived context → research question → felt observation → mechanism function → counter-question → external evidence.

Step-by-step to apply it to a new idea:

1. **Open with a personal anecdote** in past tense that names one concrete, quantified coping behaviour (e.g., layers, litres, hours). One sentence.
2. **Pivot with "However"** and pose a first-person wondering question about the *real underlying danger* of the situation. One sentence.
3. **Add a bodily observation** that uses *in order to* to imply purpose, narrowing the topic from the environment to a single body response.
4. **Confirm with "Indeed,"** then define the relevant organ/system using the formula *the function of X is to [verb]*.
5. **Re-pivot with "however,"** and ask a conditional counter-question: *what would happen if [the function] were disrupted?*
6. **Close with external evidence:** a "Quick research leads me to…" sentence that delivers three semicolon-separated statistics or facts and a parenthetical citation.

This sequence shrinks the topic from a broad environmental experience down to a specific, testable physiological mechanism, which is exactly the job of an IB Introduction.
