**Paragraph 1** (intro — context + narrowing)

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is the **problem-framing / motivation opening** of an exploration that draws its question from a cultural artefact (here, an xkcd comic). The ordered moves are:

1. **Pick up the source's catalogue.** Pick up a previously introduced set of items (problems, questions, scenes) from a single named source — open with a tail fragment.
2. **Triangulate to elevate one.** Use a "first … while third … but second …" structure to bracket three siblings and dignify the middle one.
3. **Quote that one verbatim.** Reproduce the chosen item in full so the reader can judge the problem directly. (The colon at the end of the previous sentence is the cue.)
4. **Justify the attempt via the source's own ethos.** Claim the source itself obligates engagement — typically by appeal to its spirit, tradition, or authorial voice.
5. **Cite a parenthetical warrant.** A short bracketed reference to a *related* item from the same author that demonstrates the obligation is real, not invented.
6. **State the paper's intention.** A thesis sentence beginning with "Thus" that narrows scope ("one interpretation") and announces what is about to be done.

**Why this order?** Each move sets up the next: you cannot quote a problem before you have flagged it as the chosen one; you cannot justify attempting it before quoting it (otherwise the reader does not know *what* you are justifying); and you cannot announce your thesis before the obligation that drives it has been established. The bracket–quote–warrant–thesis arc turns a joke-puzzle into a legitimate investigation.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / sifting through a set** — "while" in "The first is a straightforward kinematics problem, while the third is impossible" — used to align first and third as known/easy so the middle can be split off.
- **Concessive pivot** — "but" in "but the second question poses a significant challenge" — used to escalate after the concessive clause.
- **Consequence / derivation** — "Thus" in "Thus in this paper I will attempt" — derives the thesis from the established obligation.
- **Parenthetical evidence marker** — "Indeed," in "Indeed, in another comic, Munroe [3]" — introduces a warranting citation.
- **Authority-via-citation** — "[3]" bracketed in-line reference — locates the claim in a retrievable source.
- **Characterisation verbs (problem-typing)** — "poses a … challenge" in "poses a significant challenge" — labels a problem's status without yet solving it.
- **Modal-obligation verb** — "does not allow" in "The spirit of xkcd does not allow" — names the source as binding the author.
- **Scope-narrowing verb** — "will attempt to solve" in "I will attempt to solve one interpretation" — announces intent with deliberate hedging.
- **Hedge / scope limiter** — "one interpretation" in "attempt to solve one interpretation" — signals that the paper will not solve the whole problem.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **motivating-frame pattern**: bracket-three → quote-one → name-obligation → cite-warrant → announce-thesis. To explain a *new* idea (a problem from a cultural or canonical source) using the same pattern:

1. **Open with the source's catalogue.** Use a tail fragment of a previous sentence that has already named a small set (problems, scenes, theorems) from one named source.
2. **Bracket three to elevate one.** In a single sentence, describe item #1 as routine, item #3 as blocked-by-X, and item #2 as the genuinely difficult one. Use "while" and "but" to flag the contrast and end with a colon.
3. **Quote item #2 verbatim.** Reproduce the problem in full, exactly as the source wrote it. Keep its original formatting and question mark. Do not paraphrase.
4. **Name the obligation.** In one sentence, claim the source's *ethos* (its "spirit", "tradition", "method") forbids leaving the problem untouched. Use a modal verb ("does not allow", "demands") to bind the author.
5. **Cite a parenthetical warrant.** In a single bracketed aside, point to a *related* work by the same source in which the source itself admits to the behaviour you are invoking. Quote a short self-description ("'nerd sniping'", "'strange-loop hunting'").
6. **Announce the thesis.** Begin with "Thus" and state what the paper will do; hedge scope with "one interpretation", "a special case", or "a partial treatment" so the claim is defensible.
7. **Hand off to the body.** The reader now expects the problem to be set up, formalised, and solved — the next paragraph should define the variables or state assumptions, not return to motivation.

The rhythm to preserve: one sentence to narrow, one block quote to expose, one sentence to obligate, one parenthetical to warrant, one sentence to declare — in that order.
