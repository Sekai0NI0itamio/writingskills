## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **Evaluation / Limitation** section (with embedded further-research suggestions). The ordered moves are:

1. **State the assumption or restriction** — name the variable you fixed, removed, or constrained.
2. **Justify the assumption methodologically** — give the reason it was a defensible research choice (typically "to simplify", "to allow comparison", "to stay focused").
3. **Project forward** — name what *could* be investigated next if time/resources allowed, ideally with concrete alternative cases.
4. **Stack the next limitation** — use an additive connective ("Furthermore", "Additionally", "Moreover") and repeat steps 1–3.

**Why this order:** Step 1 creates a doubt in the reader's mind ("didn't that bias your result?"). Step 2 immediately answers it, defusing the critique. Step 3 then demonstrates mature awareness by showing the student already knows what would extend the work. Step 4 lets multiple limitations accumulate without any single one dominating — building cumulative honesty about the scope of the investigation.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern used:** a repeated **LIMITATION → JUSTIFICATION → FORWARD EXTENSION** micro-unit, stacked with additive connectives into a paragraph of cumulative scope-honesty.

To explain a new idea (e.g., a methodological restriction in your own IA) using this pattern:

1. **State the constraint in one short sentence.** Use "I only…" or "I made an implicit assumption of…" plus the variable you fixed, removed, or constrained. Keep it narrow and falsifiable.
2. **Justify it immediately with "This was done to…" or "This is mainly to…".** Give one *practical* reason (e.g., "to allow effective comparison", "to keep the setup feasible").
3. **Add a second reason joined by "and to…"** — give one *conceptual* reason (e.g., "to allow my exploration to remain focused on a goal"). The pairing signals mature reflection.
4. **Pivot forward with "Further areas of research could include…"** and specify two concrete alternatives using "such as X and Y" — concrete cases make the extension credible, not vague.
5. **Open the next unit with "Furthermore" or "Additionally"** and repeat steps 1–4. Stacking two-to-four units is the sweet spot; beyond that the section reads like a confession list.
6. **Close the final unit with "Although this limits…"** — naming the cost of the restriction is what converts a list of caveats into an evaluative argument.
