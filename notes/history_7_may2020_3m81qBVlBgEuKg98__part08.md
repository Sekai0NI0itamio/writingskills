## What This Section Does (content sequence)

The section runs a **CONTEXT → CAUSAL CLAIM → DUAL EVIDENCE → VERDICT/RANKING → MECHANISM** sequence.

1. **Context carry** (the fragment) — supplies the social precondition.
2. **Causal claim** (Consequently…) — converts that precondition into a modal effect on an agent; *sets up* the need for evidence.
3. **First supporting argument** ("An argument in favour of…") — establishes the *redefinition* reading; *sets up* a complementary alternative.
4. **Second supporting argument** ("Another perspective…") — adds the *additive* reading; *sets up* a synthesis by widening what counts as influence.
5. **Verdict paragraph opener** ("It can be seen that… to a greater extent than…") — synthesises the two readings into a *ranking* between two possible drivers; *sets up* the mechanism.
6. **Mechanism sentence** ("They often used… as an argument against…") — instantiates the verdict with a concrete rhetorical move.

The order is causal: a trigger must precede its effect; an effect-claim must precede its proofs; two complementary proofs must precede the synthesis they license; a synthesis must precede the example that grounds it.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — the dual-evidence paragraph**

`SKELETON: "[social trigger noun phrase]. Consequently, [agent] may have [causation verb] the [abstract outcome] to [stated purpose] without [parallel-but-different qualifier]. An argument in favour of [agent]'s influence is that [actor] did not necessarily [original state], but instead [modified state]. Another perspective that can be considered is that [actor] did not [abandon original]; [actor] simply [additive move]."`

1. *Slot 1 (trigger)*: noun phrase naming a social phenomenon; past or timeless tense; 3–5 words.
2. *Slot 2 (causal claim)*: agent + modal "may have [verb]" (catalysed / fuelled / accelerated / legitimised) + abstract outcome noun phrase.
3. *Slot 3 (limiter)*: "without [carrying / replicating] an identical [agenda / programme / mandate]" — softens the parallel.
4. *Slot 4 (first argument)*: structure "did not necessarily X, but instead Y" — a *redefinition* of category or target.
5. *Slot 5 (second perspective)*: "Another perspective that can be considered is that [actor] did not X; [actor] simply Y" — an *additive* reading.
6. *Original fill:* "xenophobia in response to immigration. Consequently, the film may have catalysed the desire for a Klan-like organisation to protect white Protestant interests without carrying an identical agenda. An argument in favour of the film's influence is that the Klan did not necessarily change its central purpose, but instead narrowed its definition of 'white' Americans to exclude all ethnic and religious groups except Anglo-Saxon Protestants. Another perspective that can be considered is that the Klan did not abandon its older prejudices in the 1920s; it simply added new ones."
7. *Demonstration fill (different subject — a 1930s novel and a youth paramilitary):* "youth disillusionment with parliamentary politics. Consequently, the novel may have catalysed the recruitment of a paramilitary youth wing to defend civic-nationalist values without carrying an identical programme. Another perspective that can be considered is that the youth wing did not shed its earlier corporatist instincts in the early 1930s; it simply absorbed new categories of enemy."

**Skeleton B — the verdict-with-mechanism paragraph**

`SKELETON: "It can be seen that [driver A] was at the heart of [actor]'s agenda, to a greater extent than [driver B]. They often used [framed trait of out-group] as an argument against [charged label], characterising [specific instance] as a violation…"`

1. *Slot 1 (verdict opener)*: "It can be seen that" + driver A + "was at the heart of [actor]'s agenda."
2. *Slot 2 (ranking)*: ", to a greater extent than" + driver B from elsewhere in the analysis.
3. *Slot 3 (rhetorical mechanism)*: "They often used [X] as an argument against [Y]" — verb "used… as an argument against".
4. *Slot 4 (worked example)*: "characterising [concrete instance] as a [violation / corruption / perversion]" — gives the specific charge.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / consequence**
- "Consequently, the film may have catalysed" — converts prior context into effect.

**Contrast / corrective**
- "but instead narrowed its definition of 'white'" — within-sentence corrective.
- "Another perspective that can be considered is that" — opens an alternative view without cancelling the prior one.

**Verdict / synthesis**
- "It can be seen that contemporary xenophobia was at the heart of the Klan's agenda" — announces a synthesised claim.

**Comparative ranking**
- "to a greater extent than the threat of blacks portrayed by the film" — ranks competing drivers.

**Evidence / hedging (modal softeners)**
- "An argument in favour of the film's influence is that" — tentative evidence framing.
- "may have catalysed" — modal hedge on causation.
- "did not necessarily change" — softens the claim's scope.
- "can be considered" — softens the alternative's force.

**Explanation / mechanism verbs**
- "narrowed its definition of 'white' Americans to exclude" — redefinition-as-mechanism.
- "used the perceived perversion of non-whites as an argument against" — frames a rhetorical move.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

Pattern used: **CONTEXT-TRIGGER → CAUSAL CLAIM → DUAL EVIDENCE (redefinition + additive) → RANKING VERDICT → RHETORICAL MECHANISM**. Steps to replicate with a new idea:

1. **Name the social trigger** in a noun phrase carried over from the preceding section (e.g., "industrial unrest in the 1880s").
2. **Open with the consequence connective "Consequently,"** and write a modal causal claim: "[agent] may have [catalysed / fuelled / legitimised] the [abstract noun phrase] to [stated purpose]."
3. **Soften the parallel** with a "without carrying an identical [agenda / programme]" clause so the influence is partial, not wholesale.
4. **Insert the first evidence sentence** using "An argument in favour of [agent]'s influence is that [actor] did not necessarily [X], but instead [Y]" — frame it as a *redefinition* of a key category.
5. **Insert the second evidence sentence** using "Another perspective that can be considered is that [actor] did not [abandon X]; [actor] simply [added Y]" — frame it as an *additive* shift.
6. **Open a new paragraph with the verdict marker "It can be seen that"** and rank two competing drivers with "to a greater extent than": "[driver A]… to a greater extent than [driver B]."
7. **Immediately ground the ranking** by naming the specific rhetorical move: "They often used [framed trait] as an argument against [charged label], characterising [concrete instance] as a violation…" — then continue the worked example into the next sentence.
