## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**
1. **Move:** Claim / thesis-statement that names the author's intent. **Quote:** "O'Brien's intention of prioritising an audience's emotional response over truth." **Hand-off:** states the EFFECT — hand-off is via *need-for-proof*: the reader now requires the technique that produces this prioritisation.
2. **Move:** Mechanism / evidence — names the structural device (temporal shifts) and what it does to truth. **Quote:** "Temporal shifts alternate from the events in the story to O'Brien hearing the story, demonstrating how storytelling dilutes the relationship between different types of truth." **Hand-off:** from abstract intent to concrete device — reader is now cued to look at the textual surface where this device operates, which is supplied by the next paragraph's dialogue.

**Paragraph 2**
3. **Move:** Embedded quotation — character-insistence paired with a narrator's physical reaction. **Quote:** "'It doesn't end there,' Rat insisted, as Mitchell Sanders let out a sigh of exasperation." **Hand-off:** via *escalation prompt* — "It doesn't end there" forces the next line to specify what comes after.
4. **Move:** Quoted visceral claim. **Quote:** "'Out there, man, you hear things.'" **Hand-off:** via *contrast with silence* — what is heard will next be paired with what is not heard.
5. **Move:** Unpack / atmospheric deepening — moves from said to unsaid. **Quote:** "It went unspoken that sometimes there was nothing to hear, nothing to see, nothing to do except sit and feel the whole war pressing in around you." **Hand-off:** via *response-invitation* — the overwhelming silence pressures the listener to act, prompting the next line.
6. **Move:** Direct address / dialogue reply. **Quote:** "'Go on, then.'" **Hand-off:** via *storytelling trigger* — the prompt hands the narration to a new voice in the next paragraph.

**Paragraph 3 (cut off mid-sentence)**
7. **Move:** Attribution / narrative relay. **Quote:** "One of the Greenies told him the rest, Rat said." **Hand-off:** via *specification* — names a new teller, the reader expects to learn who this teller is.
8. **Move:** Example / concrete instance. **Quote:** "Xavier Moore, Green Beret - real close-knit group, that squad of six." **Hand-off:** via *characterisation-before-condition* — once the unit is specified, the reader expects the unit's state.
9. **Move:** Claim / observation launching a simile. **Quote:** "They were detached from the war, in the strange way a hand…" **Hand-off:** via *truncated comparison* — the unfinished simile forces continuation.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

The section executes this ordered sequence:

1. **Intent-claim** (what the author is doing) — sets up the analytical lens.
2. **Technique + mechanism** (the device and what it produces) — provides the causal bridge between intent and effect.
3. **Embedded dialogue evidence** — instantiates the effect inside the text, so the reader sees, not just hears, the claim.
4. **Atmospheric unpacking via the unsaid** — shifts from dialogue to felt sensation, deepening the visceral register.
5. **Listener response that triggers narration** — shows storytelling as a social act, echoing the "hearing the story" mechanism named in move 2.
6. **Named-instance specification** — drops from general to particular (a name, a unit, a condition), to make the visceral concrete.

**Why this order:** intent → device → instance of the device → felt texture of the instance → the social relay that produces the instance → specific case. Each move narrows from abstraction to body, so the reader moves *down* the ladder of abstraction in parallel with the writer's deepening emotional engagement.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Intent + Device pairing**

> SKELETON: "[Author]'s intention of [gerund-phrase doing X over Y]. [Named device] [verb of demonstration], [mechanism that produces the effect]."

1. **Slot 1:** Authorial intent phrased as a gerund ("prioritising") competing two things (emotion vs. truth). **Slot 2:** A concrete structural device + a verb of demonstration ("demonstrating how") + a mechanism clause stating what that device does to a relationship or category.
2. **To fill:** Pick an author. Pick two competing values (e.g., beauty over accuracy). Gerundise the prioritisation. Then name the most visible structural device in the text and write what it does to a category (e.g., "dilutes," "complicates," "inverts").
3. **Original fill:** "O'Brien's intention of prioritising an audience's emotional response over truth. Temporal shifts alternate from the events in the story to O'Brien hearing the story, demonstrating how storytelling dilutes the relationship between different types of truth."
4. **Demonstration fill (new idea):** "Atwood's intention of prioritising a reader's ethical discomfort over resolution. Recursive flashbacks fracture the linear chronology of Offred's testimony, demonstrating how memory destabilises the boundary between compliance and complicity."

---

**Skeleton B — Dialogue-then-unspoken pairing**

> SKELETON: "'[Insistent quoted line],' [Character A] insisted, as [Character B] [physical reaction]. '[Second quote about the experience].' [Narration: It went unspoken that / What is not said is] [triadic sensory absence], [climactic felt phrase]."

1. **Slot 1:** A short insisted line from a teller. **Slot 2:** Narrator's physical breath/response. **Slot 3:** A second quoted line stating the visceral phenomenon. **Slot 4:** A narrator's "unspoken" clause using a triadic "nothing to X, nothing to Y, nothing to Z" that bottoms out in a single compressed sensation.
2. **To fill:** Choose a storytelling scene. Write one sentence a character insists, one a listener's reaction, one the sensory phenomenon. Then describe what the character does not say but feels — using three "nothing to" clauses and ending on one crushing sensation.
3. **Original fill:** "'It doesn't end there,' Rat insisted, as Mitchell Sanders let out a sigh of exasperation. 'Out there, man, you hear things.' It went unspoken that sometimes there was nothing to hear, nothing to see, nothing to do except sit and feel the whole war pressing in around you."
4. **Demonstration fill (new idea):** "'You don't understand yet,' Mrs. Ramsay murmured, as Mr. Ramsay set down his fork. 'It comes later, in the dining room, you feel it.' It went unspoken that sometimes there was nothing to say, nothing to taste, nothing to do except sit and feel the whole evening slipping toward its own vanishing."

**Skeleton C — Relay-attribution + specified instance**

> SKELETON: "[Person] told [listener] the rest, [original teller] said. [Named figure], [title/group] - [characterising fragment]. [Observation of detachment/condition], in the strange way a [launched simile]…"

1. **Slot 1:** A relay clause naming a second-hand source ("X told him the rest, Y said"). **Slot 2:** A named figure + military/social role + dash + characterising fragment. **Slot 3:** A claim about that figure's state, launched into an unfinished simile beginning "in the strange way a…"
2. **To fill:** Pick a scene of indirect testimony. Open by naming who told whom, attributed to the original voice. Then introduce one specific character with role and a characterising phrase after a dash. Finish with an observation of their condition and a simile starting "in the strange way a [body part/object]…"
3. **Original fill:** "One of the Greenies told him the rest, Rat said. Xavier Moore, Green Beret - real close-knit group, that squad of six. They were detached from the war, in the strange way a hand…"
4. **Demonstration fill (new idea):** "One of the nurses told her the rest, the matron said. Sister Evangelina, Anglican order - real quiet type, that postulant of three. She was detached from the ward, in the strange way a bell…"

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / framing the device:** "alternating from the events in the story to O'Brien hearing the story" — names a temporal oscillation as a *device*, not as plot.

**Demonstration / mechanism verbs:** "demonstrating how storytelling dilutes the relationship" — packages a verb of showing ("demonstrating") with a verb of erosion ("dilutes") to make the abstract effect concrete.

**Authority / intent verbs:** "intention of prioritising" — locates the argument in the writer's design rather than the reader's response.

**Evidence-handling / attribution:** "Rat insisted"; "Rat said" — re-states who is speaking so the quotation sits inside a chain of voices.

**Atmospheric specification:** "the whole war pressing in around you" — a felt-phrase that uses a body verb ("pressing in") plus a totality marker ("the whole") plus a directional enclosure ("around you").

**Quoted-cut / dialogue as pivot:** "'It doesn't end there,'" "Go on, then.'" — short imperative interjections that exist *only* to move the next voice into action.

**Unpack via negation:** "It went unspoken that sometimes there was nothing to hear, nothing to see, nothing to do except" — three-part "nothing to" ladder that escalates through absence before releasing into one sensation.

**Detachment / simile-launch:** "in the strange way a hand" — opens a comparison with a body part as anchor, leaving the comparison open to deepen.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section runs on the pattern: **INTENT → DEVICE → EMBEDDED DIALOGUE → UNSPOKEN FEELING → RELAY → SPECIFIC INSTANCE.**

To explain a new idea the same way:

1. **Open with the writer's intent.** Phrase it as "[Author]'s intention of [gerund] [A] over [B]." Force a competition between two values so the thesis is debatable.
2. **Name the structural device.** Pick the single most visible feature (time-shift, refrain, focalisation, recurring image) and write "[Device] [verb of showing], [mechanism verb] [what the device does to a relationship]."
3. **Drop into the text with a short insistent quotation** that a character pushes forward, paired with a narrator's *physical reaction* (a sigh, a set-down, a glance). This grounds the abstraction in a body.
4. **Quote a second line** that states the visceral phenomenon in the character's own words.
5. **Unpack via the unspoken.** Write "It went unspoken that…" followed by a three-part "nothing to X, nothing to Y, nothing to Z" ladder, and *finish the ladder with one compressed sensation* using a body verb and a totality marker (e.g., "the whole X pressing in around you").
6. **Hand the narration on.** Use a one-line imperative dialogue reply — "Go on, then." — that triggers the next voice.
7. **Specify the new voice.** Attribution "[X] told him the rest, [original teller] said," then a named figure with role + dash + characterising fragment.
8. **Launch a condition into a simile.** End with "They were [state of detachment], in the strange way a [body part]…" — leaving the comparison unfinished so the next paragraph can deepen it.

The pattern's logic: each move answers the unspoken question of the previous one (intent → how → proof → what the proof feels like → who carries it next → what they look like). A student replicating it must keep each sentence answering the *gap* left by the one before.
