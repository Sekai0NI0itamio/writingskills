## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (3 sentences + table)
- S1 — *Verdict/closure claim*, via a causal-consequence frame. "With the new vertices of the mesh, the investigation is complete." Hands off by naming the *thing* the work has now produced ("the new vertices"), so the next sentence can name what is *done with* that thing.
- S2 — *Specification of the deliverable* (count + destination). "The twelve triangles can now be fed into OpenGL…" Hands off by promising a concrete list, which the next sentence then points to.
- S3 — *Evidence pointer + convention note*. "These triangles are shown in table 1, the vertices of each triangle…" Hands off by closing the artifact side of the pipeline; the reader is now standing at the table, so the next paragraph must explain what happens *after* it.

**Paragraph 2** (3 sentences)
- S1 — *Transition + context-gap*. "There need to be several more steps before the result is actually visible on-screen." Hands off by opening a "several more steps" slot, which the next two sentences fill one by one.
- S2 — *Specification of step 1, with a special rule*. "The colour (gradient) of each triangle needs to be specified…" Hands off by introducing the chronological marker "Then," preparing the final pipeline move.
- S3 — *Sequencing + mechanism (the last stage)*. "Then, everything would have to go through a shader program before it is actually rendered on a display." Closes the loop: the section started with "investigation complete" and ends with the final on-screen result.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A short *conclusion / pipeline-closure* section does, in order:

1. **Verdict of completion** — name the end-state of the work (e.g., "the mesh is built", "the equation is solved", "the data is grouped"). Sets up the reader to expect a *deliverable* rather than more process.
2. **Name the deliverable explicitly** — give it a count, a unit, and a target/destination. This is what you will hand off (to software, to a reader, to a model).
3. **Point to the evidence** — a table, figure, or list, plus a small convention note (ordering, units, format). This lets a reader verify the deliverable.
4. **Bridge the gap** — admit that "the table" alone is not yet the final visible result. This is mandatory after evidence; it prevents the section from ending at the table.
5. **List the remaining ordered steps** — step A, then step B ("Then…"). Use a chronological connective so the reader sees a sequence, not a parallel list.

The order works because each move hands the reader a smaller, more concrete question: *Is it done? What is it? Where is it? What now happens to it? How does it end?*

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — "The investigation is complete" paragraph**
> SKELETON: "With [new product of the work], the investigation is complete. The [N] [units] can now be [fed/produced/submitted] into [destination/audience] with their respective [attributes]. These [units] are shown in [evidence label], the [attributes] of each [unit] are simply in [convention]."

1. **Slots & shapes**
   - Slot 1: noun phrase naming the just-finished artefact (e.g., "the new vertices").
   - Slot 2: small cardinal number + plural countable noun ("twelve triangles").
   - Slot 3: past-passive verb of transfer ("can now be fed into").
   - Slot 4: name of a tool/audience/recipient ("OpenGL").
   - Slot 5: plural of the noun in slot 2, plus a label like "table 1" / "figure 2".
   - Slot 6: ordering/format convention ("in alphabetical order").
2. **How to fill with a different idea** — pick a project whose final output is a *finite list* (e.g., chemical compounds, candidate policies, ranked candidates). The sentence shape is always: *completion claim → numbered list → where the list lives → how the list is ordered*.
3. **Original filled version** — "With the new vertices of the mesh, the investigation is complete. The twelve triangles can now be fed into OpenGL with their respective vertices. These triangles are shown in table 1, the vertices of each triangle are simply in alphabetical order."
4. **Demonstration fill (different subject)** — "With the new substitution rules in place, the investigation is complete. The six candidate reactions can now be entered into the simulator with their respective activation energies. These reactions are shown in table 2, the energies of each reaction are simply listed in order of increasing temperature."

**SKELETON B — "Several more steps remain" paragraph**
> SKELETON: "There need to be several more steps before the result is actually visible on-[medium]. The [property] of each [unit] needs to be specified, in this case [special rule that pairs units into a higher-level form]. Then, everything would have to go through a [process] before it is actually [final visible outcome]."

1. **Slots & shapes**
   - Slot 1: a property/attribute of the unit (e.g., "colour (gradient)").
   - Slot 2: singular of the unit noun from skeleton A (e.g., "triangle").
   - Slot 3: a pairing/folding rule — *N units share one value to form one larger object* (here: 2 triangles → 1 cube face).
   - Slot 4: name of a processing stage (e.g., "shader program", "compiler", "smoothing filter").
   - Slot 5: past-passive verb of final display (e.g., "rendered on a display").
2. **How to fill with a different idea** — keep the *pairing rule* intact: choose a system where smaller pieces combine into a larger named whole (pixels → sprite, notes → chord, samples → mean, data points → trend line). The chronological connective "Then," is fixed.
3. **Original filled version** — "There need to be several more steps before the result is actually visible on-screen. The colour (gradient) of each triangle needs to be specified, in this case two triangles will share one solid colour to form a cube face. Then, everything would have to go through a shader program before it is actually rendered on a display."
4. **Demonstration fill (different subject)** — "There need to be several more steps before the result is actually visible on the page. The shading of each note needs to be specified, in this case two notes will share one dynamic marking to form a phrase. Then, everything would have to go through a notation engine before it is actually typeset in the score."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / chronological**: *"Then, everything would have to go through"* — marker that orders the final pipeline step after the colour step.
- **Causal-consequence / completion frame**: *"With the new vertices of the mesh, the investigation is complete."* — "With…, …complete" pattern that announces closure.
- **Capability / operation verb**: *"can now be fed into OpenGL"* — frames the artefact as ready-to-use.
- **Specification / exemplification**: *"in this case two triangles will share one solid colour"* — narrows a general rule to a single instance.
- **Evidence handling (pointer)**: *"These triangles are shown in table 1"* — direct cross-reference to a labelled exhibit.
- **Convention / format note**: *"are simply in alphabetical order"* — a low-emphasis aside that prevents the reader from misreading the table.
- **Process / obligation verb**: *"would have to go through a shader program"* — soft obligation, not a strict rule.
- **Final-state verb**: *"before it is actually rendered on a display"* — the past-passive "rendered" closes the pipeline.
- **Bridging transition**: *"There need to be several more steps before…"* — a "gap" sentence that prevents the section from ending on the table.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section relies on a **"pipeline-closure" pattern**: the writer treats the investigation as a chain of stages, announces it is finished at the artefact, points to the artefact, and then walks the reader down the remaining stages of the pipeline in order.

To replicate the pattern on a NEW idea:

1. **Step 1 — Issue a completion claim.** State that the work has produced a named artefact. Use a "With [X], the [task] is complete" frame. This sets up the reader to expect a deliverable, not more method.
2. **Step 2 — Name the deliverable with a count, a noun, and a destination.** Always include the number (e.g., "the twelve triangles", "the six reactions") and the recipient ("OpenGL", "the simulator"). The count makes the deliverable auditable.
3. **Step 3 — Point to the evidence and state the convention.** Cross-reference a labelled table or figure and add one short clause on how the rows/columns are ordered. This is the reader's only chance to verify the list.
4. **Step 4 — Insert a "gap-bridge" sentence.** Use the shape "There need to be several more steps before the result is actually visible on [medium]." This sentence is mandatory after the table; it tells the reader the table is *not* the end of the story.
5. **Step 5 — Walk the remaining steps in chronological order.** First the *attribute* step (colour, shading, label), and include a special *pairing rule* ("in this case N units share one value to form one larger object"). Then a final *process* step introduced by "Then," that ends with the past-passive verb of display ("rendered", "typeset", "printed", "exported"). The section must close on the final visible result, not on the table.
