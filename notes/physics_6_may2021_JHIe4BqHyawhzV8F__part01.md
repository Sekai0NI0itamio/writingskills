## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

This section is written as a single paragraph, so I'll map it sentence-by-sentence.

**Move 1 — Definitional claim.** "Friction is a force that significantly affects the motion…" Anchors the whole essay by naming the phenomenon and asserting its general reach. Hands to next by *judging* what was just named — having introduced friction, the writer must now say whether it is a problem.

**Move 2 — Value judgment + cause.** "Friction's presence is often an unfavorable phenomenon due to the shifts…" Calls friction undesirable and supplies the reason (parameter shifts). Hands to next by *consequence* — if it's unfavorable, somebody has tried to fix it.

**Move 3 — Implication / consequence.** "This has led to investigations that aim to find methods to reduce it." Picks up the previous clause via "This" and broadens to the entire field of reduction research. Hands to next by *specification* — general investigations need a concrete instance.

**Move 4 — Specific instance.** "One of the places where we notice the importance… is combustion engines…" Narrows from "investigations" to a chosen application. Hands to next by *evidence* — a chosen context must be justified with data.

**Move 5 — Quantitative evidence.** "Most gasoline combustion engines have an average of around 20%…" Provides a numerical anchor. Hands to next by *unpack* — a single percentage must be converted into a real figure.

**Move 6 — Unpack / worked meaning.** "Meaning that out of 45MJ energy… only 9MJ is useful." Translates the 20% into absolute numbers so the reader can feel the magnitude. Hands to next by *refinement* (contrast) — the calculation has just shown 9 MJ; friction in practice cuts it further.

**Move 7 — Contrast / refinement.** "However, due to the presence of friction… the useful energy concludes to be less…" Uses "However" to undercut the 9 MJ figure and reinforce the earlier claim that friction is unfavorable. Hands to next by *consequence* ("Therefore") — having restated the problem, the writer must now announce the engineering goal.

**Move 8 — Consequence + purpose statement.** "Therefore, to increase an engine's efficiency, it is intended to reduce the value…" Sets the goal: reduce μ. Hands to next by *solution-introduction* — the goal demands a method.

**Move 9 — Method introduction.** "One approach to reducing friction is the use of lubrification." Names a method. Hands to next by *mechanism* — having named the method, the writer must explain how it works.

**Move 10 — Mechanism.** "Lubricants form layers between two objects' surfaces, hence making a partial…" Explains *how* the method achieves the goal. Hands to next by *limitation* (contrast with "However") — a method that always worked would not warrant a study.

**Move 11 — Limitation / boundary condition.** "However, lubricants maintain their own disadvantages to the system; they change phase…" States the temperature windows where the method breaks. Hands to next by *gap-to-aim* — the limitation is exactly what the experiment will probe.

**Move 12 — Aim / research question.** "This investigation experiment will help test how the lubricant layer's temperature… affects the change…" Converts the identified limitation into the variable the experiment will vary, closing the funnel from general phenomenon to specific test.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an Introduction section, and it executes a fixed funnel:

1. **General phenomenon + significance** — sets up *why this matters at all*.
2. **Negative judgment + cause** — establishes *why it must be addressed*, which licenses the rest.
3. **Field-level response** — shows the writer is aware that people have already tried.
4. **Specific application chosen** — narrows the essay to one context.
5. **Quantitative evidence for the problem** — proves the context is genuinely wasteful.
6. **Unpack of that evidence** — turns one number into a concrete loss.
7. **Refinement of the loss** — adds friction back in to sharpen the problem.
8. **Engineering goal derived from the problem** — converts problem into a target variable.
9. **A known solution named** — gives the reader the working vocabulary for the experiment.
10. **Mechanism of the solution** — explains *how* the solution works.
11. **Limitation of the solution** — exposes the open variable the experiment will exploit.
12. **Aim statement** — turns the open variable into the dependent/independent relationship tested.

**Why this order:** each move hands the next move a problem it must answer. Significance justifies a problem; the problem justifies evidence; evidence justifies a goal; the goal justifies a method; the method's mechanism justifies its limitation; the limitation justifies the aim. Remove any one step and the reader feels a logical gap.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — "Context → Judgment → Field response" (sentences 1–3)**
> "[X] is a [category] that significantly affects [process] in [scope]. [X]'s presence is often an unfavorable [phenomenon] due to [cause]. This has led to investigations that aim to [goal]."

1. **Slots & shape:** slot 1 = a noun + relative clause defining the phenomenon broadly (declarative); slot 2 = a value judgment with "due to" + noun phrase (cause clause); slot 3 = a "This has led to…" consequence clause ending in a purpose infinitive.
2. **How to fill with a new idea:** slot 1 — pick any everyday force/factor and state it as affecting a process in a familiar scope; slot 2 — judge it unfavorable and name the physical/economic parameter it disturbs; slot 3 — write the consequential "people are investigating ways to reduce/control it."
3. **Original fill:** "Friction is a force that significantly affects the motion of moving objects… Friction's presence is often an unfavorable phenomenon due to the shifts in force and energy parameters… This has led to investigations that aim to find methods to reduce it."
4. **Demo fill (new idea):** "Thermal expansion is a property that significantly affects the geometry of metallic rails in railway systems. Thermal expansion's presence is often an unfavorable phenomenon due to the warping and buckling it causes in continuous welded tracks. This has led to investigations that aim to find methods to accommodate it."

**SKELETON B — "Specific instance → Quantitative evidence → Unpack → Refinement" (sentences 4–7)**
> "One of the places where we notice [the importance of fixing X] is [context]. Most [items] have an average of around [percent]. Meaning that out of [input], only [output] is [useful]. However, due to the presence of [X], the [output] concludes to be [less]."

1. **Slots & shape:** slot 1 = "One of the places where…" clause specifying context; slot 2 = declarative with a single numerical percentage; slot 3 = "Meaning that…" sentence converting percent to absolute values; slot 4 = "However, due to…" sentence that subtracts a further loss using "concludes to be."
2. **How to fill with a new idea:** slot 1 — pick an industrial/technological setting where the problem shows up; slot 2 — research a realistic inefficiency figure; slot 3 — multiply input by that figure to give the useful output; slot 4 — name one more hidden loss (the variable you will study) that pulls the output lower still.
3. **Original fill:** "One of the places where we notice the importance of reducing friction is combustion engines… Most gasoline combustion engines have an average of around 20% energy efficiency. Meaning that out of 45MJ energy… only 9MJ is useful. However, due to the presence of friction… the useful energy concludes to be less than 9MJ."
4. **Demo fill (new idea):** "One of the places where we notice the importance of reducing heat loss is domestic boilers. Most condensing boilers have an average of around 92% thermal efficiency. Meaning that out of 200 MJ of fuel energy, only 184 MJ reaches the loop. However, due to the presence of soot on the heat exchanger, the useful heat concludes to be below 184 MJ."

**SKELETON C — "Goal → Method → Mechanism" (sentences 8–10)**
> "Therefore, to [goal], it is intended to [action on variable]. One approach to [doing X] is [method]. [Method] [physical action], hence [consequence]."

1. **Slots & shape:** slot 1 = "Therefore, to…," purpose infinitive naming the engineering goal; slot 2 = "One approach to…" declarative introducing the method by name; slot 3 = mechanism clause with "hence" linking physical action to functional consequence.
2. **How to fill with a new idea:** slot 1 — derive a goal directly from the refined loss; slot 2 — name a textbook technique that addresses the goal; slot 3 — describe the *physical* action of that technique (what it does to surfaces/layers/particles) and end with a "hence" giving the functional benefit.
3. **Original fill:** "Therefore, to increase an engine's efficiency, it is intended to reduce the value of the coefficient of dynamic friction. One approach to reducing friction is the use of lubrification. Lubricants form layers between two objects' surfaces, hence making a partial or entire separation between them."
4. **Demo fill (new idea):** "Therefore, to lower the rail temperature in summer, it is intended to increase the albedo of the rail head. One approach to raising albedo is the application of a white reflective coating. The coating scatters incident solar radiation across visible wavelengths, hence reducing the fraction absorbed by the steel."

**SKELETON D — "Limitation → Aim" (sentences 11–12)**
> "However, [method] maintain their own disadvantages to the system; they [boundary 1 at low value] and [boundary 2 at high value]. This investigation experiment will help test how [independent variable] between [materials] affects [dependent variable]?"

1. **Slots & shape:** slot 1 = "However," concessive clause with a semicolon splitting two failure thresholds; slot 2 = "This investigation experiment will help test how…" aim question naming IV, materials, and DV.
2. **How to fill with a new idea:** slot 1 — give the method a lower and upper boundary beyond which it stops working; slot 2 — turn one of those boundaries into the IV, pick two materials for the contact surfaces, name the DV.

This investigation experiment will help test how the lubricant layer's temperature between two aluminum surfaces affects the change in dynamic friction coefficient?"

This investigation experiment will help test how the coating's surface temperature between two steel coupons affects the change in solar absorptivity?"

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / specification**
- "One of the places where we notice…" — narrows the prior general claim to a chosen case.
- "One approach to reducing friction…" — narrows from "the goal" to "the method."

**Cause / consequence**
- "due to the shifts in force and energy parameters" — names the mechanism behind the value judgment.
- "This has led to investigations that aim…" — picks up the previous clause and turns it into a research trajectory.
- "Therefore, to increase an engine's efficiency…" — drives the engineering goal from the refined loss.
- "hence making a partial or entire separation" — links the physical action of the lubricant to its functional outcome.

**Contrast / concession**
- "However, due to the presence of friction…" — undercuts the 9 MJ figure with a hidden loss.
- "However, lubricants maintain their own disadvantages…" — flips from mechanism praise to limitation.

**Specification / unpacking**
- "Meaning that out of 45MJ energy… only 9MJ…" — converts a percentage into absolute numbers.

**Evidence handling**
- No direct "according to" attribution appears; evidence is delivered as a stand-alone figure ("an average of around 20% energy efficiency") with the calculation performed inline.

**Explanation verbs**
- "significantly affects" — generic influence verb used in the opening definition.
- "concludes to be less than 9MJ" — used to express the *result* of the hidden loss.
- "form layers between two objects' surfaces" — verb of physical arrangement that sets up the mechanism.
- "change phase into solid at 3°C" — verb of physical transformation used to mark the limitation boundary.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section uses a **"broad phenomenon → quantified loss → derived goal → method + mechanism → limitation → aim"** funnel. To replicate it for a new topic, take these steps in order:

1. **Open with a definitional claim about the broad phenomenon.** Use the pattern "[X] is a [category] that significantly affects [process] in [scope]." This earns the right to talk about the phenomenon at all.
2. **Judge it as unfavorable and name the parameter it corrupts.** Write "[X]'s presence is often an unfavorable phenomenon due to [shifts in parameter Y]." This licenses the rest of the essay to argue for change.
3. **State the field-level response.** Add "This has led to investigations that aim to [reduce/control X]." This shows the writer is not the first person to notice the problem.
4. **Pick a specific context where the problem shows up.** Write "One of the places where we notice the importance of [fixing X] is [context]." This narrows the essay and prepares the reader for evidence.
5. **Quantify the loss with one headline figure.** State "Most [items] have an average of around [percent/number] [efficiency/loss/accuracy]." One number is enough — it just has to be believable.
6. **Unpack that figure into absolute terms.** Write "Meaning that out of [input], only [output] is [useful]." Converting percent → joules/dollars/units makes the loss tangible.
7. **Refine the figure with a hidden additional loss.** Begin with "However, due to the presence of [X], the [output] concludes to be [less]." This sharpens the problem and makes the engineering goal necessary rather than optional.
8. **Derive the engineering goal from the refined loss.** Begin with "Therefore, to [goal], it is intended to [action on variable]." The goal must follow *directly* from the hidden loss just stated.
9. **Name a known solution.** Write "One approach to [fixing X] is [method]." This gives the reader the vocabulary for the experiment.
10. **Explain the mechanism of the solution.** Write "[Method] [does physical action], hence [functional consequence]." The "hence" is the key connective — it ties the physical action to the engineering benefit.
11. **State the limitation as two boundary thresholds.** Use "However, [method] maintain their own disadvantages to the system; they [fail at low value] and [fail at high value]." Two thresholds make the limitation a concrete range, not a vague complaint.
12. **Convert one threshold into the experimental aim.** Close with "This investigation experiment will help test how [independent variable] between [material A] and [material B] affects [dependent variable]?" The question form signals that the aim came from the limitation, not from nowhere.
