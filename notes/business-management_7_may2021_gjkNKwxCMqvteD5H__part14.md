## What This Section Does (content sequence)

The section is a **reference list**, so its content sequence is not argumentative but **evidentiary**: it stacks sources in an order a reader can follow without rereading.

1. **Anchor price fact (unattributed, current product)** — sets the consumer-facing price baseline. *Why first:* gives the reader a concrete monetary reference point everything else can be measured against.
2. **Analyst cost comparison (attributed, prior generation)** — moves from sticker price to component/boM cost. *Why second:* reframes "price" as something built from parts, opening the door to a supply-chain argument.
3. **Import/customs duty on a key component** — adds the regulatory cost layer. *Why third:* explains why landed cost differs from factory cost, preparing the reader for margin/strategy discussion.
4. **Manufacturer sales + R&D amortisation claim** — returns to the focal firm with a sales-volume and cost-recovery claim. *Why fourth:* shows whether R&D has already been paid back, a precondition for future pricing/strategy moves.
5. **Mainstream market-share verdict + forward-looking caveat** — moves from internal cost story to external market position. *Why fifth:* the reader now needs to know how the firm is performing competitively before strategy is discussed.
6. **Strategic-framework reference (Ansoff Matrix)** — introduces the theoretical tool that the analysis will likely be applied through. *Why sixth:* having established performance, the list supplies the framework that will interpret it.
7. **Capital-intensity of the underlying industry (chip fabs)** — supplies the financial-scale backdrop. *Why seventh:* any strategy claim must be checked against the minimum viable scale of the industry.
8. **Geopolitical disruption event** — closes on a destabilising current event. *Why last:* leaves the reader with the live tension that the essay's argument must address.

A student replicating this sequence on a different topic would do: **headline price/fact → expert comparison of cost structure → regulatory/tax layer → firm-level financial claim → market-position verdict → named analytical framework → industry-scale constraint → live disruption event.**

Because this is a bibliography, the "paragraph skeleton" is really an **entry-block skeleton** (the chunk of metadata that constitutes one citation) and a **list-ordering skeleton** (the logic for arranging entries).

### Skeleton A — Single-citation entry block

   SKELETON: "[Author/Org surname, Given or role]. \"[Article title — often a noun phrase or thesis statement].\" [Publisher/Platform], [Day Month Year], [URL]."

  1. **Slot 1 — Author or organisational author.** Grammatical shape: surname first, optional given name, sometimes a role/descriptor instead of a person. Holds *who vouches for the claim*.
  2. **Slot 2 — Title in quotation marks.** Grammatical shape: noun phrase, often a comparative or causal statement ("Cost Comparison – X, Y, Z"; "Why the … Club Just Got Smaller"). Holds *what the source claims*.
  3. **Slot 3 — Publisher/platform in italics (rendered plain here).** Grammatical shape: proper noun, optional "Magazine" / "Inc." / domain. Holds *where the claim was published and therefore how authoritative/partisan it is*.
  4. **Slot 4 — Publication date.** Grammatical shape: day + month + year. Holds *when the data was current, which frames its reliability*.
  5. **Slot 5 — URL.** Grammatical shape: full link. Holds *traceable access*.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section does not explain ideas — it **stages sources so the essay's future explanation is easy to defend**. The replicable pattern is therefore not "definition → unpack → example" but **evidence stacking in increasing depth**, and the steps are:

1. **Open with a public, recognisable datum.** Pick a number or fact any reader already half-knows (a phone's price, a country's GDP). State it in the most neutral source possible. This anchors the rest of the list in something undeniable.
2. **Add an expert teardown of that datum.** Pick a second source that breaks the first source's number into parts (BoM cost, component price, fee structure). The reader now has the headline *and* its interior.
3. **Insert the regulatory or systemic layer.** Pick a third source that explains why the interior cost is what it is (tariffs, labour law, subsidies, customs). This converts "expensive" from a complaint into a mechanism.
4. **Quote the focal firm on its own internal economics.** Pick a source — even an informal one — that states a firm-internal claim (R&D recovered, margin achieved, target met). This hands the analysis a number it can argue with.
5. **Cite mainstream press on the firm's external position.** Pick a source a non-specialist would recognise (Forbes, Reuters, The Verge). The argument now has competitive context.
6. **Name a theoretical framework you will later apply.** Pick a source that defines the framework (Ansoff, Porter, SWOT). Do not yet apply it — just lodge it.
7. **Anchor the industry-scale constraint.** Pick a source quantifying the minimum scale of the industry (capex, network size, market share threshold). This bounds what the framework in step 6 can realistically recommend.
8. **Close on a live disruption event.** Pick a current-affairs source showing the firm/industry in conflict (sanction, lawsuit, supplier defection, regulation). This gives the essay its open question.

The pattern is *evidence-stacking-by-depth*, not argument-by-claim. To replicate it on any new topic: **public number → expert breakdown → regulatory mechanism → firm-internal claim → market verdict → named framework → industry scale → live shock.**
