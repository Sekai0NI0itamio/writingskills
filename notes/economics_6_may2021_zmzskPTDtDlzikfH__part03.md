## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (1 sentence — a claim with an example fused inside it)
- **Move 1 (Claim + embedded Example via "such as"):** "Many customers say they are increasingly turning to cheaper and unhealthier options, such as, buying cotton-seed oil instead of sunflower oil for cooking." — Names the trend in general terms, then immediately slots one concrete swap inside the same sentence to make the trend visible.
- **Hand-off to next paragraph:** The example shows a *cheaper substitute* pattern; the next line widens the lens to people who cannot buy items at all, which extends the same logic (cost pressure) to a more extreme outcome — a *consequence escalation*.

**Paragraph 2** (1 sentence)
- **Move 1 (Extension / broader category move introduced with "Others"):** "Others are cutting back even on unaffordable 'luxuries.'" — Pivot from substituting one oil for another to skipping items altogether; "even" signals the scope is widening beyond the first example.
- **Hand-off:** A vague noun like "luxuries" invites a concrete instance, so the next move drops into a first-person source — *specification* via testimony.

**Paragraph 3** (1 sentence)
- **Move 1 (Direct-quote evidence with price detail):** "'I don't buy eggs, meat, and fruit anymore. Even apples are too expensive for me at $1-$1.4 a kilo,' another Dushanbe resident told RFE/RL." — Quoted voice supplies the items ("eggs, meat, and fruit") and a numeric anchor ("$1-$1.4 a kilo") that grounds the prior abstract claim.
- **Hand-off:** One resident establishes a pattern, so the next move *layers a second voice* to show the pattern is not isolated.

**Paragraph 4** (1 sentence)
- **Move 1 (Second quote, this time supplying cause):** "'I lost my job as a taxi driver in Dushanbe and now I depend on corn and wheat that my family sends me from our village,' another man said." — Same structure (quote + attribution), but the content shifts from *what is unaffordable* to *why* — a job-loss mechanism.
- **Hand-off:** Two personal stories share a city frame (Dushanbe); the next move *transitions geographically* to broaden the regional picture.

**Paragraph 5** (1 sentence)
- **Move 1 (Geographic pivot + verbal verdict phrase):** "In Sughd, Ozoda said, families like hers have to make 'tough choices.'" — Locator ("In Sughd") opens a new region; the abstract noun "tough choices" foreshadows the concrete illustration that immediately follows.
- **Hand-off:** A verdict phrase needs unpacking; the next move *unpacks the choice* with a worked example.

**Paragraph 6** (1 sentence)
- **Move 1 (Worked example with embedded contradiction):** "'For a snack, I make 'tolqon,' mixing crushed dry mulberry and walnuts that we harvest from the trees in our backyard. But we can't eat them all -- we're selling half of the crop to buy firewood or coal for the winter,' she added." — Names a specific food, explains its preparation, then introduces the contradiction (selling food to buy fuel); this converts the abstract "tough choices" into a tangible trade-off. The section closes on this image — a *concrete closer*.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **General behavioural claim + one inline example.** *Why first:* the reader needs a one-line orientation before being asked to absorb testimonies. The inline example ("such as, buying cotton-seed oil…") does double duty: it illustrates the claim without requiring a second sentence.
2. **Scope extension ("Others are cutting back even on…").** *Why second:* once one swap is shown, widening to a more extreme outcome (cutting back entirely) prepares the reader for the personal testimonies that follow.
3. **First attributed quote with price detail.** *Why third:* a single human voice with a numeric anchor makes the abstract cost-pressure claim verifiable and emotionally credible.
4. **Second attributed quote adding cause.** *Why fourth:* the first quote showed *what*; the second shows *why* (job loss), supplying the mechanism that makes the pattern systematic rather than anecdotal.
5. **Geographic pivot + quotable verdict phrase.** *Why fifth:* moving the lens (Dushanbe → Sughd) signals the pattern is regional; the verdict phrase ("tough choices") is a verbal scaffold for the final worked example.
6. **Worked example that reveals a hidden trade-off (food sold for fuel).** *Why last:* the section needs to land on a concrete image that exceeds what the reader expected, converting the "tough choices" phrase into a demonstrated cost.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / example-introduction connective:** "such as," — used in "turning to cheaper and unhealthier options, such as, buying cotton-seed oil instead of sunflower oil." (Joins general claim to one illustrative instance.)
- **Contrast / scope-widening opener:** "Others are" — used in "Others are cutting back even on unaffordable 'luxuries.'" (Signals a broader or more extreme subset.)
- **Escalation marker:** "even" — used in "cutting back even on unaffordable 'luxuries.'" (Flags that the scope exceeds the expected.)
- **Evidence handling — direct quote attribution phrases:** "told RFE/RL", "another man said", "Ozoda said", "she added" — used to anchor each testimony and to keep speakers distinct.
- **Specification / numeric anchoring:** "at $1-$1.4 a kilo" — used in "apples are too expensive for me at $1-$1.4 a kilo." (Replaces abstract cost with a concrete figure.)
- **Cause / consequence link inside a quote:** "and now I depend on" — used in "I lost my job as a taxi driver in Dushanbe and now I depend on corn and wheat." (Joins cause to behavioural consequence.)
- **Concession / contradiction introducer:** "But we can't eat them all" — used in "we harvest from the trees in our backyard. But we can't eat them all" (Reverses the expected abundance into a deficit.)
- **Explanation of mechanism through naming:** "mixing crushed dry mulberry and walnuts" — used in "I make 'tolqon,' mixing crushed dry mulberry and walnuts" (Unpacks the named practice so a non-local reader can picture it.)
- **Verdict-quoting verb for paraphrased summary:** "have to make 'tough choices.'" — used in "families like hers have to make 'tough choices.'" (Packages the section's point into a portable phrase before the final example.)

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

**Pattern name:** General behavioural claim → inline concrete example → broadening of the same pattern → first-person testimony with a numeric anchor → second testimony that supplies a *cause* → geographic pivot + a portable verdict phrase → worked example that reveals a hidden trade-off (food sold for fuel, etc.).

**Replication steps to apply this pattern to a new idea:**

1. **Open with a one-sentence general claim** about a population's shifting behaviour, naming the direction of change (cheaper, worse, fewer, more extreme). Place the claim in the present tense and identify the actors ("[Group] say they are increasingly…").
2. **Embed a single concrete example inside that opening sentence** using "such as," followed by one named substitute or swap. Do not break into a second sentence yet — keep the reader oriented with one combined move.
3. **Write a short broadening sentence starting with "Others are"** that escalates the behaviour (not just substituting, but skipping; not just reducing, but cancelling). Use "even" to flag that the new category exceeds the reader's expectation.
4. **Insert the first direct quote.** Inside the quote: (a) name two or three specific items lost, (b) add one numeric anchor (price, weight, distance, time), (c) close with attribution that names both a place and an outlet or source ("another [city] resident told [outlet]").
5. **Insert a second direct quote** with a different speaker. This time the quote should answer *why* the pattern is happening (a job loss, a price shock, a policy change). Connect cause and consequence with "and now".
6. **Pivot geographically** by opening a new sentence with "In [new region]," and naming a new speaker. Close the sentence with a short, quotable two- or three-word verdict inside scare quotes ("have to make 'tough choices.'"). This phrase is the bridge to the final example.
7. **Close with a worked example that exceeds the verdict phrase.** Quote a specific named practice, briefly explain how it is done (so an outsider can picture it), then introduce a "But" clause that reveals the hidden cost — what is sacrificed, sold, or traded to afford something else. End on that trade-off image; do not summarise.
