## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A reference list in an IB exploration performs this ordered sequence:
1. **Establish authoritative breadth** (government/institutional sources appear alongside academic work) — sets up credibility for the claims made elsewhere.
2. **Pair mathematical and historical sources** — mathematical sources legitimize the technical apparatus while historical sources contextualize the investigation.
3. **Mix teaching material with primary scholarship** — course notes (MIT OCW, Tseng) sit alongside historical journal articles (Sargent, 1917), signalling the student consulted multiple tiers of source.
4. **Close with computational / lookup tools** — MathWorld and WolframAlpha appear at the end, framing them as supplementary verification rather than primary evidence.

The order is essentially **alphabetical-by-author-with-functional-clustering**, and the implicit logic is: *primary academic → historical context → encyclopedic tool*. Another student replicating this should place the sources they relied on most heavily first (or alphabetically first) and verification tools last.

1. **What each slot holds:** Author in inverted form (proper noun, comma-separated); date in parenthetical ISO-ish order; title as noun phrase; retrieval/publisher clause with colon and hyperlink.
2. **How to fill differently:** slot 1 = last-name, initials of the author you read; slot 2 = publication date (look up exact date where possible); slot 3 = exact title in italics conceptually; slot 4 = "Retrieved from [platform name]" plus URL. ## Express-Idea Vocabulary

Because this section is a bibliography, traditional logical connectives are absent; the "moves forward" mechanism is **citation-format vocabulary**:

- **Sequencing / ordering cues:** no explicit words — the order itself does the sequencing (entries are presented alphabetically by author surname, e.g. "National flag of Canada" → "Niu, R." → "Orthogonality Relations" → "Sargent, N." → "Smith, J. - **Source-classification verbs (evidence handling):** "Retrieved from" — appears in "Retrieved from Government of Canada Publications - Canada.ca"; signals where the student accessed the source.
- **Authorship-attribution verbs:** "Producer" — appears in "(I. S. University, Producer) Retrieved from Polaris Project Evening Star"; used when the entity behind the source is an institution rather than a named author.
- **Date markers / specificity:** parenthetical dates with month and day, e.g. "(1917, November 3)" and "(2004, May 25)"; these act as the equivalent of "specifically" — pinning the source to a precise moment for verifiability.
- **Absence markers:** "(n.d.)" appears in "Weisstein, E. W. (n.d.). Riemann Sum"; functions as a concession / honesty cue — admitting the source has no listed date rather than fabricating one.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section does **not** explain an idea discursively — it *establishes provenance*. The replication pattern is therefore a **provenance-trace pattern**, not a definition→unpack→example pattern. To replicate it for a new topic:

1. **Identify every source you actually consulted** during the investigation (books, lecture notes, websites, journal articles, computational tools).
2. **For each source, locate the metadata:** author(s) or institutional producer, full date of publication or last update (write "n.d." only if truly absent), exact title, retrieval URL or publisher.
3. **Decide on ordering logic.** Alphabetical-by-author-surname is the IB-safe default; alternatively, order by source-type (primary scholarship → teaching material → encyclopedic tools).
4. **Render each entry in a consistent citation style.** Apply the same punctuation, capitalisation, and italics convention across every entry so the list reads as one uniform block.
5. **Verify each link/date once** before submission; the bibliography's function is *traceability*, so any broken or fabricated entry breaks the section's purpose.
6. **Mix source classes deliberately.** Include at least one historical/journal source, at least one institutional/teaching source, and at least one computational/encyclopedic source — the variety itself signals breadth of research, which is what the IB examiner reads off the page.
