## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1 (sentences 1–4: introducing a competitor manufacturer)**

1. **Context** — "First, we can consider Chinese manufacturers" → opens the section by announcing this paragraph will introduce the first alternative supplier.
2. **Specification (naming)** — "the most notable being China's Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC)" → narrows from the broad category to the single relevant example.
3. **Status claim** — "SMIC is an option that Huawei is currently exploring" → establishes what role the named company plays in the investigation (hand-off: because SMIC is a real option, the next sentence must show what it can actually do).
4. **Evidence** — "SMIC has been reported to have started production of Huawei's lower end chips" → supplies proof of the "currently exploring" claim (hand-off: having shown it produces lower-end chips, the reader now needs to know whether that is enough).

**Paragraph 2 (sentences 5–6: the limitation of that manufacturer)**

5. **Concession → contrast** — "However, it is no secret that SMIC is behind in terms of technology when compared to TSMC" → the "However" directly answers the question left by sentence 4 (can it do more?) with "no, it cannot" (hand-off: the reader now needs proof of how far behind).
6. **Evidence / quantification** — "'TSMC is miles ahead of SMIC in terms of leading-edge technology prowess'" → delivers the proof (hand-off: the reader now knows the gap exists in general; next sentence must specify exactly which Huawei product line this rules out).

**Paragraph 3 (sentences 7–9: what this means for Huawei's product range)**

7. **Specification** — "Huawei's high-end chips are based the 7 nano-meter technology" → pins down the missing capability (hand-off: the next sentence must connect this missing capability back to Huawei's options).
8. **Implication** — "This may mean that Huawei can only rely on SMIC for lower end chips" → draws the direct consequence, phrased as a hedge ("may mean") because the inference is the writer's own (hand-off: an implication sentence requires a concrete instance, so an example must follow).
9. **Example** — "An example would be the Huawei Mate 10, a high-end phone released two and a half years ago" → supplies the instance the implication promised, but framed as a concession (the example is an older high-end phone, justifying why it uses lower-end chips).

**Paragraph 4 (sentences 10–12: cost data of the example)**

10. **Specification** — "It uses the Kirin 970 developed by HiSilicon three years ago" → zooms into the chip inside the example (hand-off: once the chip is named, its cost becomes the next logical unit).
11. **Evidence (datum)** — "a Kirin 970 chip costs Huawei $51.7" → delivers the cost figure, attributed (hand-off: a chip cost only makes sense next to a total production cost).
12. **Setup** — "With a combined production cost of $290, the Huawei Mate 10 goes at a retail price of $750" → provides the two surrounding numbers that allow a return-rate calculation in the next paragraph.

**Paragraph 5 (sentences 13–17: the worked calculation)**

13. **Mechanism (worked calculation)** — "750 / 290 = 258%" → computes the return rate introduced implicitly by the previous sentence's two figures (hand-off: a calculation must be followed by what its result allows us to do).
14. **Assumption** — "we will assume that the chip itself also has a return rate of 258%" → makes the move from whole-phone return rate to chip-only return rate explicit (hand-off: a stated assumption allows the next sentence to derive the per-chip selling price).
15. **Derivation (mechanism)** — "This means that each chip is sold at $133.40" → applies the assumption to get a usable number (hand-off: a per-unit price requires a sales volume to produce a profit figure).
16. **Evidence (datum)** — "the Huawei Mate 10 reached 10 million sales worldwide" → supplies the volume (hand-off: volume × price per unit − cost per unit = profit, so the calculation is next).
17. **Mechanism (worked calculation)** — "133.4(10,000,000) − 51.7(10,000,000) = $817,000,000" → executes the gross-profit calculation and scopes it ("Microprocessors only").

**Paragraph 6 (sentence 18: bridge to the next manufacturer)**

18. **Implication → transition** — "With more advanced chips, Huawei can consider using Samsung as their chip manufacturer" → uses the word "With" + the gained profit capability to project forward, and the phrase "can consider using Samsung" hands the reader out of this section into the next one.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **comparative manufacturer evaluation with a worked financial demonstration**. The moves in order are:

1. **Name the first alternative** (Chinese manufacturers, then SMIC specifically) — sets up the section by identifying whom we are now comparing against.
2. **State SMIC's current role for Huawei** (option being explored, already producing lower-end chips) — establishes that SMIC is real but limited.
3. **State SMIC's limitation against the leading player** (behind TSMC, with attributed quote) — provides the contrast anchor that justifies looking elsewhere.
4. **Specify the technological gap concretely** (7 nm unavailable at SMIC) — converts the abstract "behind" into a precise missing capability.
5. **Draw the product-line implication** (Huawei limited to lower-end chips via SMIC) — tells the reader what SMIC can and cannot do for Huawei.
6. **Instantiate with an older high-end example** (Huawei Mate 10 / Kirin 970) — gives a concrete product whose financial profile can be calculated.
7. **Supply the cost data for that example** (chip cost, total production cost, retail price) — gives a complete set of numbers.
8. **Run a worked return-rate calculation** (price ÷ cost = return %) — demonstrates the profit mechanism step-by-step.
9. **State the assumption that links whole-phone return to chip return** — makes the inference the reader needs in order to trust the next step.
10. **Derive the per-chip selling price** (return rate × cost) — produces the chip-level figure the profit calculation needs.
11. **Add the sales volume** (10 million units) — completes the data set.
12. **Run the gross-profit calculation** (volume × (price − cost)) — delivers a hard number that justifies the section's verdict.
13. **Bridge to the next manufacturer** ("With more advanced chips, Huawei can consider using Samsung") — uses the demonstrated capability/profit to justify moving to a better supplier.

The order matters because each move supplies exactly the input the next move consumes: numbers enable assumptions; assumptions enable per-unit derivations; per-unit derivations plus volume enable profit; profit enables the "can now afford a better manufacturer" conclusion.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — "Introduce a competitor with its current role and its limitation"**

SKELETON: "First, we can consider [BROAD GROUP], the most notable being [NAMED COMPANY]. [NAMED COMPANY] is an option that [INVESTIGATED PARTY] is currently exploring. [NAMED COMPANY] has been reported to [WHAT IT CURRENTLY DOES FOR THEM]. However, it is [WIDELY ACKNOWLEDGED ADJECTIVE] that [NAMED COMPANY] is behind in terms of [DIMENSION] when compared to [LEADER]. [AUTHORITY FIGURE, TITLE] was quoted saying '[DIRECT QUOTE COMPARING THE TWO ON [DIMENSION]]'."

1. *Slots*:
   - Slot 1 (BROAD GROUP): noun phrase, regional/category label (e.g., "Chinese manufacturers").
   - Slot 2 (NAMED COMPANY): full proper noun + acronym in parentheses on first mention.
   - Slot 3 (WHAT IT CURRENTLY DOES): past-participle clause reporting an action (e.g., "started production of ... lower end chips").
   - Slot 4 (WIDELY ACKNOWLEDGED ADJECTIVE): idiom signalling common knowledge (e.g., "no secret").
   - Slot 5 (DIMENSION): a competitive axis (e.g., "technology").
   - Slot 6 (LEADER): the benchmark firm.
   - Slot 7 (AUTHORITY FIGURE, TITLE) + (DIRECT QUOTE): attributed quote on a named axis.
2. *How to fill differently*: slot 1 — pick a region/category of suppliers; slot 2 — name one specific company and add an acronym the reader will recognise; slot 3 — state in past tense what they already do for the investigated party; slot 4 — use a "common knowledge" idiom ("well known", "no secret"); slot 5 — choose a single competitive axis (cost, scale, IP portfolio); slot 6 — name the obvious leader in that axis; slot 7 — attribute a one-sentence quote to a titled analyst who quantifies the gap.
3. *Original fill*: "First, we can consider Chinese manufacturers, the most notable being China's Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC). SMIC is an option that Huawei is currently exploring. SMIC has been reported to have started production of Huawei's lower end chips. However, it is no secret that SMIC is behind in terms of technology when compared to TSMC. Neil Shah, the research director at Counterpoint research was quoted saying 'TSMC is miles ahead of SMIC in terms of leading-edge technology prowess, capacity and scale.'"
4. *Demonstration fill with a different idea* (different subject, same skeleton): "First, we can consider European shipping firms, the most notable being Denmark's Maersk. **Skeleton B — "Specify the technological miss, draw the product-line implication, and give an example"**

SKELETON: "[INVESTIGATED PARTY]'s [HIGH-END TIER] are based [on] the [SPECIFIC TECH/MATERIAL] which [SUPPLIER] has not yet been able to implement. This may mean that [INVESTIGATED PARTY] can only rely on [SUPPLIER] for [LOWER TIER]. An example would be the [PRODUCT NAME], a [TIER] [PRODUCT TYPE] released [TIME DEPTH AGO]. It uses the [COMPONENT NAME] developed by [INTERNAL DESIGN HOUSE] [TIME DEPTH AGO]."

1. *Slots*:
   - Slot 1 (HIGH-END TIER + SPECIFIC TECH): noun phrase naming the premium product tier and its required technology (e.g., "high-end chips are based on the 7 nano-meter technology").
   - Slot 2 (SUPPLIER NOT YET ABLE): proper noun + past-tense capability clause.
   - Slot 3 (PRODUCT-LINE LIMITATION): "can only rely on ... for [lower tier]" — modal + restriction.
   - Slot 4 (EXAMPLE PRODUCT): brand + model name, classified by tier, with time-depth modifier.
   - Slot 5 (INTERNAL COMPONENT): chip/code name + developing subsidiary + time depth.
2. *How to fill differently*: slot 1 — name a premium line and the specific feature only the leader supplies; slot 2 — reuse the supplier from skeleton A in a "has not yet been able to ..." construction; slot 3 — frame the limit as a "only ... for ..." sentence beginning "This may mean"; slot 4 — pick a real flagship product from several years ago so its specs match the supplier's capability; slot 5 — name the sub-brand that designs the component and add an "ago" time stamp.
3. *Original fill*: "Huawei's high-end chips are based the 7 nano-meter technology which SMIC has not yet been able to implement. This may mean that Huawei can only rely on SMIC for lower end chips. An example would be the Huawei Mate 10, a high-end phone released two and a half years ago. It uses the Kirin 970 developed by HiSilicon three years ago."

**Skeleton C — "Stack cost numbers, compute return rate, state the linking assumption, derive the per-unit price"**

SKELETON: "With a combined production cost of $[TOTAL COST], the [PRODUCT] goes at a retail price of $[RETAIL PRICE]. [PRODUCT] has a return rate of: [RETAIL]/[TOTAL] = [PERCENT]%. Since the [PRODUCT] with all the components combined has a return rate of [PERCENT]%, we will assume that the [COMPONENT] itself also has a return rate of [PERCENT]%. This means that each [COMPONENT] is sold at $[UNIT PRICE]."

1. *Slots*:
   - Slot 1 (TOTAL COST) and Slot 2 (RETAIL PRICE): two dollar figures, smaller first.
   - Slot 3 (RETURN-RATE FRACTION): retail on top, total on bottom, equals a percentage.
   - Slot 4 (ASSUMPTION LINK): a "Since ... we will assume that the [component] also has ..." sentence; this is the rhetorical hinge.
   - Slot 5 (UNIT PRICE): result of multiplying the assumed return rate by the per-unit cost.
2. *How to fill differently*: slot 1 + 2 — pick real public figures for total cost and retail price so the ratio is meaningful; slot 3 — lay it out as a displayed fraction with the equals-sign on its own line; slot 4 — name the component explicitly and use "we will assume" so the inference is visibly the writer's, not a hidden step; slot 5 — multiply through to show a clean per-unit revenue figure.
3. *Original fill*: "With a combined production cost of $290, the Huawei Mate 10 goes at a retail price of $750. Huawei Mate 10 has a return rate of: 750/290 = 258%. Since the Huawei Mate 10 with all the components combined has a return rate of 258%, we will assume that the chip itself also has a return rate of 258%. This means that each chip is sold at $133.40."
4. *Demonstration fill with a different idea*: "With a combined bill-of-materials cost of $420, the Sony WH-1000XM4 goes at a retail price of $349. The WH-1000XM4 has a return rate of: 349/420 = 83%. Since the WH-1000XM4 with all the components combined has a return rate of 83%, we will assume that the noise-cancellation processor itself also has a return rate of 83%. This means that each QN1 chip is sold at $5.81."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / opening a section on a competitor:** "First, we can consider" — announces the move from prior material to the next alternative supplier.
- **Citing sources:** "has been reported to" — frames a fact as reported (evidence handling); "According to" — frames a cost breakdown as evidence; "was quoted saying" — frames an analyst judgement as evidence.
- **Concession / contrast:** "However" — pivot from "SMIC produces lower-end chips" to "SMIC cannot match the leader"; also "behind ... when compared to" frames the gap as a comparison.
- **Capability / specification:** "based the 7 nano-meter technology" / "has not yet been able to implement" — pinpoints the exact technological miss.
- **Implication language:** "This may mean that" — soft modal that signals the writer is inferring, not reporting; "This would give" — projects the profit number forward.
- **Assumption-marking:** "we will assume that" — makes the leap from whole-product return rate to component return rate visible and ownable.
- **Mechanism / calculation verbs:** "has a return rate of", "reached ... sales", "give ... a gross profit of" — each introduces the result of a computation.
- **Bridge / transition to next supplier:** "With more advanced chips" + "can consider using" — uses the demonstrated capability/profit as the licence to evaluate a better alternative.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern used here is **competitive-evaluation-then-worked-financial-proof**, executed as:

1. **Open with "First"** and name the regional/category group you are about to evaluate (e.g., "First, we can consider [region] manufacturers"). This signals you are comparing, not analysing one firm in isolation.
2. **Narrow to one named firm** with its acronym on first mention, and state in present progressive ("is currently exploring") what role it plays for the investigated party.
3. **Report in past tense what that firm already does** ("has been reported to have started production of [tier]") to anchor the firm as real, not theoretical.
4. **Pivot with "However"** to the firm's weakness against the obvious market leader, naming the competitive axis ("behind in terms of [dimension] when compared to [leader]").
5. **Cite a titled authority on that axis** with a direct quote that quantifies the gap — never let the comparison float without evidence.
6. **Specify the missing capability in concrete technical terms** ("based [on] the [X]-nanometer technology which [firm] has not yet been able to implement") so the gap is measurable.
7. **Draw a hedged implication** ("This may mean that [party] can only rely on [firm] for [lower tier]") using "may" because the inference is yours.
8. **Give a worked example from the lower tier** — an older flagship that uses a named internal component — so the reader has a concrete object to follow through the financials.
9. **Stack three numbers** (component cost, total production cost, retail price) on consecutive sentences so the calculation that follows has every input visible.
10. **Display the return-rate calculation as a centred fraction** with the equals-sign on its own line, so the mechanism is visible rather than buried in prose.
11. **State the linking assumption out loud** ("we will assume that the [component] itself also has a return rate of [X]%") — this is the rhetorical hinge between whole-product and component economics.
12. **Derive the per-component revenue figure** in one sentence ("This means that each [component] is sold at $[Y]").
13. **Add the volume** ("reached [N] sales worldwide") and immediately compute gross profit as a displayed expression of the form p·n − c·n, labelled with a scope tag like "(Microprocessors only)" so the reader sees both the figure and what it excludes.
14. **Close with a forward bridge** built on the demonstrated capability ("With more [attribute], [party] can consider using [next supplier]") so the section ends as a licence to continue evaluating, not as a dead end.
