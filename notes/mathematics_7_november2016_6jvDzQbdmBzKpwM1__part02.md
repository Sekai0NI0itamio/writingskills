## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1 — setting up the human**
1. "Let us first consider the motion of the human." → **Ordering / signposting move** — names what comes next and reserves it as the easier component. Hands off by telling the reader the *human* block is about to be unpacked, prompting the next sentence to justify why the human is treated first.
2. "Based on the first sentence of question 2, we will suppose:" → **Source-citation + transition to a list** — anchors assumption-building to a specific prior text. Hands off by dangling a bullet list, so the reader expects concrete parameter statements.
3. "A human is at the centre of an equilateral triangle…" → **Geometric definition / evidence** — supplies the spatial layout. Hands off because one position-setting assumption now needs a motion assumption to be coherent.
4. "And since question 2 asks for a particular angle, we further suppose:" → **Specification trigger ("further suppose")** — re-cites the source and adds a new assumption. Hands off to the next bullet by announcing it will tighten what came before.
5. "The human begins at the origin, (0, 0), and runs outward…" → **Parameter unpack** — fixes starting coordinates and direction of motion. Hands off because velocity still needs fixing to make the motion complete.
6. "Finally, since question 1 mentions the human reaches its top speed…" → **Sequencing + source trigger ("Finally")** — pulls the last remaining human parameter from a different question. Hands off by promising the final assumption of this block.
7. "The human runs at 𝑣h m s⁻¹ = 6 m s⁻¹, and has infinite acceleration…" → **Final parameter assignment** — closes the human block. Hands off by implying the *other* component (raptors) is now the open problem.

**Paragraph 2 — exposing the contradiction**
1. "Unfortunately, the motion of the other component of the question, the velociraptors, is less clear." → **Verdict + concession ("Unfortunately")** — explicitly compares the two components and flags raptor motion as the messier one. Hands off by making the reader expect evidence of why it is unclear.
2. "While the question states that 'The raptors will run towards' the human, each question suggests different values for the acceleration and maximum velocity of the raptors:" → **Conflict claim with a cited common ground** — establishes shared behaviour, then foreshadows that the *parameters* disagree. Hands off because the reader now needs the specific differences itemised.
3. "Question 1 states that a raptor accelerates at 4 m s⁻² up to a maximum of 25 m s⁻¹, whereas question 3 suggests a constant speed of 10 m s⁻¹, which is in turn contradicted by question 2, which implies that the top speed of an uninjured raptor is greater than 10 m s⁻¹, but does not quote specific values…" → **Comparative evidence chain (whereas → contradicted by → implies)** — stacks three sources to show the conflict is not a simple two-way dispute. Hands off because no resolution has been offered yet, forcing the writer to declare a chosen synthesis.

**Paragraph 3 — declaring the synthesis**
1. "In this paper, we will suppose that:" → **Authority verdict / own-decision marker** — signals the writer's synthesis. Hands off by dangling a list of chosen values.
2. "One raptor, raptor 1, begins directly above the human…" → **Resolution parameter 1** — assigns the lower raptor. Hands off because two more raptors must be positioned.
3. "Two raptors begin 120° either side of the human, raptor 2 to the left and raptor 3 to the right…" → **Resolution parameter 2 (symmetry pair)** — closes the geometric picture. Hands off by symmetry, no more raptors needed.
4. "Each raptor runs towards the current position of the human at all times." → **Behavioural rule / mechanism** — defines pursuit logic, the only non-numeric assumption. Hands off to a figure that visualises the whole setup.
5. "This is depicted in Figure 2." → **Evidence signposting** — closes the formulation block by pointing to a visual.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **State what is fixed and easier first** — the human's motion is resolved before any contested item, so the reader has a clean coordinate frame.
2. **Tackle the unclear component by laying out conflicts among sources** — three contradictory source values are placed side by side so the reader sees why a synthesis is needed.
3. **Declare the synthesis** — the writer's chosen values are presented as a clean, parallel list.
4. **Anchor visually** — a figure reference seals the parameter set so the rest of the paper can rely on it.

The order matters because (a) starting with the easier component builds confidence and saves the contested work for later, (b) showing all three conflicting source statements before choosing makes the synthesis look justified rather than arbitrary, and (c) ending with a figure turns the parameter stack into a single reusable object.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**SKELETON A — Layered-assumption paragraph for the easier component**
`SKELETON: "Let us first consider the [X]. Based on [source A], we will suppose: [bullet 1]. And since [source B] asks for [feature], we further suppose: [bullet 2]. Finally, since [source C] mentions [clue], we will assume: [bullet 3]."`

1. *Slots* — slot 1: noun-phrase for the easier entity (e.g. "the projectile"); slot 2: cited sentence from source text; slots 3/5/7: bullets, each a single declarative clause with a numeric parameter; slot 4: a "since + question-feature" clause; slot 6: a "since + textual clue" clause.
2. *How to fill differently* — slot 1: name your simpler entity in two words; slot 2: pick a sentence from your source brief that fixes one coordinate; slot 3: write one geometry/setup bullet; slot 4: borrow a feature-word (angle, ratio, count) directly from a question prompt; slot 5: write a position/direction bullet using that feature; slot 6: pick the briefest cue word ("quickly", "exactly") from another question; slot 7: write the velocity or rate bullet linked to that cue.
3. *Original fill* — "Let us first consider the motion of the human. Based on the first sentence of question 2, we will suppose: A human is at the centre of an equilateral triangle… And since question 2 asks for a particular angle, we further suppose: The human begins at the origin… Finally, since question 1 mentions the human reaches its top speed of 6 m s⁻¹ 'quickly', we will assume: The human runs at 𝑣h m s⁻¹ = 6 m s⁻¹…"
4. *Demonstration fill (different idea)* — "Let us first consider the motion of the cart. Based on Part (a) of the prompt, we will suppose: A cart of mass 0.5 kg sits on a horizontal track 2 m long. And since Part (a) asks for a particular launch angle, we further suppose: The cart is released from rest at a height 0.4 m above the track. Finally, since Part (b) mentions the cart reaches its bottom speed 'suddenly', we will assume: The cart's lower rollers exert no friction on the track."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing:** "Let us first consider" (positions human block first); "And since" (chains next assumption); "Finally, since" (signals last assumption in block).
- **Cause / consequence:** "Based on the first sentence of question 2, we will suppose:" (cites authority → derives assumption); "we further suppose" (layered consequence of the cited feature).
- **Contrast / concession:** "Unfortunately, the motion… is less clear" (concedes clarity gap); "whereas question 3 suggests" (two-way contrast); "which is in turn contradicted by question 2" (escalates contrast into outright contradiction).
- **Specification:** the writer uses **"And since question 2 asks for a particular angle"** as the specification trigger, and **"we further suppose"** as the precision-marker.
- **Evidence handling:** "Based on the first sentence of question 2" (anchors claim to source); "question 1 states that a raptor accelerates…" (cites value); "question 3 suggests a constant speed" (flags weaker evidence with "suggests"); "question 2… implies that the top speed" (flags inferred evidence).
- **Explanation / assumption verbs:** "we will suppose" (declares chosen parameter); "we further suppose" (extends parameter set); "we will assume" (closes parameter set on a behavioural claim).

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **layered-assumption → contradiction-exposure → synthesis**. To replicate:

1. **Open with an ordering cue** that names the cleaner component and reserves it for the first block.
2. **Cite the source** that justifies each assumption explicitly ("Based on the first sentence of…"), so every parameter is traceable.
3. **Stack assumptions as bullets**, each bullet pulled from a *different* sentence or sub-question, using connector words ("And since…", "Finally, since…") that double as sequencing and justification.
4. **Switch to the messy component with a concession adverb** ("Unfortunately…") to flag that this block will be a conflict, not a clean list.
5. **Quote one sentence that all sources share** to establish the common ground before disagreement.
6. **List at least three sources in escalating contradiction** using *states → whereas suggests → which is contradicted by → which implies*, so the reader sees the dispute has multiple faces.
7. **Pivot with the verdict phrase "In this paper, we will suppose that:"** to mark the end of exposition and the start of the writer's own choice.
8. **Restate the parameters as a clean parallel list**, with one privileged single instance, one symmetric pair, and one non-numeric behavioural rule.
9. **Close with a figure reference** so the parameter block becomes a reusable object for the rest of the paper.
