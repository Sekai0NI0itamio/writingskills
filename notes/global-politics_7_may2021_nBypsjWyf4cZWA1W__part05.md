## What This Section Does (content sequence)

The section performs one **inverted-cone argument ending in a synthesis verdict**:

1. **Extend the ambiguity claim into a new domain** (legal/diplomatic) — sets up that the *same* weakness found earlier also afflicts the law.
2. **Quote an authoritative instrument** — installs the precise wording so the rest of the paragraph can wrestle with it.
3. **Unpack the contested term inside the quote** — lists the criteria the instrument relies on, exposing how vague the "share" is.
4. **Pivot via "However" to a negative verdict** — declares the instrument ineffective.
5. **Drive a rhetorical question into the gap** — names the missing referee (who weights the factors?).
6. **Concrete-ise the gap with two opposing national priorities** — turns abstraction into Egypt-vs-Ethiopia clash.
7. **Convert the whole argument into a numbered synthesis grounded in primary research** — uses "All in all" to claim ownership of the answer and lists the EE's findings.

**Why this order:** each move progressively narrows. The author moves *out* into international law, *in* to one instrument, *in* to one phrase, *in* to the question it leaves open, *in* to one bilateral clash, and finally *into* the student's own data. The reader is funnelled from a broad critique of the legal regime to a personal, enumerated conclusion. A student replicating this must use the same funnel: broad critique → authoritative quote → contested term → expose the gap → dramatise with parties → land in primary findings.

- **Slot 3 ("What is considered…"):** a restatement of one quoted phrase, then a list of criteria also pulled from the same source.
- **Slot 4 ("However…"):** concessive verdict on the instrument's usefulness; one noun phrase + reason phrase.

- **Slot 6 ("Party A may see…"):** parallel-clause comparison using "compared to," naming each side's priority.
- **Slot 7 ("All in all…"):** synthesis sentence starting with "All in all," referring to first-person research, ending in a colon-led numbered list (two items).

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / addition:**
- *"Furthermore, such ambiguity is also present in…"* — extends a previously made critique into a parallel domain.
- *"For instance, the Helsinki Rules…"* — introduces a member of the just-named category.

**Cause / consequence:**
- *"…is heavily dependant on relevant factors such as…"* — names what produces the contested outcome.
- *"…can be deemed as ineffective due to the inherent ambiguities present."* — cause attached via "due to."

**Contrast / concession:**
- *"However, such diplomatic tactics can be deemed as ineffective…"* — overturns the apparent usefulness of the instrument just quoted.

**Specification:**
- *"What is considered 'reasonable and equitable' is…"* — uses "What is considered …?" as a specification prompt for the quoted phrase.
- *"…such as 'geography, past and present utilization, economic and social needs.'"* — narrowing list introduced by "such as."

**Evidence handling:**
- *"'comprehensive codification[s] of international river basin principles…'"* — quoted directly with footnote reference; treats the source as authority.
- *"…may see that it's 'historical rights' to the Nile…"* — quotation marks signal a term the actor itself uses.

**Explanation / synthesis verbs:**
- *"helped me comprehend some of the reasons behind…"* — first-person framing verb that does explanatory work, ending in "behind."

**Comparison:**
- *"…compared to Ethiopia's need to provide stable electricity…"* — "compared to" juxtaposes two parties' priorities.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern is **definition → unpack → expose gap → dramatise → own with primary evidence**.

1. **Name a category of solutions** and claim a flaw carries over into it (connective: "Furthermore"). This widens the critique.
2. **Quote one or two real instruments/documents** that *try* to solve the problem, embedding the official definition verbatim inside quotation marks. This installs authority.
3. **Pick one contested phrase inside your quote** and ask "What is considered [phrase]?" Then list the factors the source itself attaches to that phrase (connective: "such as"). This unpacks.
4. **Verdict via "However"** — say the instrument *cannot* resolve disputes because of those factors. This is the analytical turn.
5. **Rhetorical question** ("Who decides which…?") that names the missing decision-maker. This dramatises the gap.
6. **Concrete bilateral example** using "Party A may see X compared to Party B's Y." This grounds the abstract.
7. **Convert into "All in all,"** refer to your own interviews/sources, and end with a colon-led two-item list of reasons. This makes the explanation *yours* and closes the funnel.

The discipline of this pattern is that **each sentence either narrows the scope or hands off to the narrower one**. If a sentence does not narrow, it should hand off via a connective that *promises* the next sentence will.
