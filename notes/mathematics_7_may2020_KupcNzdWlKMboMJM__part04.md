## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**
1. **DEFINITION + COMPARISON claim** — *"Quaternions are number systems that extend that of complex numbers"* — anchors quaternions as a superset of complex numbers, handing the reader to the next sentence by naming the reference system that will be defined first.
2. **CONTEXT (defining the reference system)** — *"Complex numbers take the form of a real part and an imaginary part"* — unpacks the "extend" claim by defining the thing being extended, handing forward to a concrete instance of that form.
3. **EVIDENCE / EXAMPLE (illustrative form)** — *"z = a + bi; a, b ∈ R, z ∈ C"* — supplies the canonical shape the reader will contrast against the quaternion form in paragraph 2.

**Paragraph 2**
1. **TRANSITION + CLAIM (mirroring move)** — *"Quaternions take the form of:"* — directly parallels the earlier "Complex numbers take the form of" sentence, handing the reader to the quaternion equation by structural symmetry.
2. **EVIDENCE / formal statement** — *"q = w + ai + bj + ck; w, a, b, c ∈ R, q ∈ H"* — presents the form being introduced, handing to the reader a need to know what each symbol denotes.
3. **SPECIFICATION (defining the symbols)** — *"Where w, a, b, c ∈ R and i, j and k are the fundamental quaternion units"* — names the conditions and the unit symbols, handing to a structural unpacking of the two halves.
4. **UNPACK (splitting the form into parts)** — *"In this case, w refers to the real part, and ai + bj + ck the vector part"* — separates scalar from vector component, handing to a verdict on how much the section will actually use.
5. **SCOPE LIMITATION / VERDICT** — *"Quaternions are much more complicated, but in this investigation I will not go in depth into them, and only apply what is neccessary to solve my problem"* — concedes further depth is possible, then sets the boundary of what will be used, handing the reader forward into the applied portion of the coursework.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **Definition / Background** section. The order is:

1. **Define the new concept by reference to a known one** — anchors the reader in familiar territory (complex numbers).
2. **Define the known concept and show its form** — grounds the comparison with a concrete equation.
3. **State the new concept's form** — mirror move, so the reader sees the structural difference (more terms) immediately.
4. **Specify the symbols/units** — legitimises the form by naming what each piece is.
5. **Unpack the form into named parts** — splits the equation into real / vector halves so the reader can read it.
6. **Bound the scope** — tells the reader what depth is *not* coming, so later application doesn't feel incomplete.

The order works because the reader is taken **known → unknown → formal → broken-down → bounded**, each move setting up the justification for the next (you can only extend something you've defined; you can only unpack something you've stated; you can only bound depth after you've shown the surface).

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / structural ordering**
- *"In this case"* — "In this case, w refers to the real part" — opens the unpacking split.
- *"Where"* — "Where w, a, b, c ∈ R and i, j …" — introduces a specifying subordinate clause after the equation.

**Contrast / concession**
- *"but"* — "Quaternions are much more complicated, but in this investigation I will not go" — concedes depth, then narrows scope.

**Specification**
- *"i, j and k are the fundamental quaternion units"* — names the unit symbols explicitly.
- *"w refers to the real part"* — assigns a role to a single symbol.

**Evidence handling / illustrative**
- *"e.g.:"* — signals a worked example of the complex-number form immediately follows.

**Explanation verbs (definition / form-giving)**
- *"are number systems that extend"* — categorises and relates simultaneously.
- *"take the form of"* — used twice, once for the reference system, once for the target system — the verb that carries the whole comparison.
- *"refers to"* — assigns meaning to a symbol inside the unpacking.

**Boundary-setting verbs**
- *"I will not go in depth into them"* — explicit scope cut-off.
- *"only apply what is neccessary"* — narrows the use of the concept to the investigation.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is **Definition-by-Extension → Mirrored Form → Symbol Specification → Two-part Unpack → Scope Boundary**.

To explain a NEW idea with the same pattern:

1. **Name the new concept and frame it as an extension of something your reader already knows.** Use the verb *"extend"* and name the predecessor explicitly.
2. **Define the predecessor and give its canonical form** (one short sentence + one displayed equation with ∈ notation).
3. **Mirror the form-giving sentence** for the new concept ("[New concept] take the form of:"), so the reader sees the structural inheritance.
4. **Give the new concept's equation**, keeping the same ∈-set style as the predecessor so the comparison reads visually.
5. **Add a "Where" clause** that names the coefficient domain and lists any new unit/index symbols.
6. **Open an "In this case" sentence** that splits the form into exactly two named parts (e.g. real vs vector, scalar vs tensor), so the reader has a reading key.
7. **Close with a scope sentence** of the shape *"X is much more complicated, but in this investigation I will not go in depth into them, and only apply what is neccessary to solve my problem."* — this signals the reader that depth is intentionally withheld and licenses brevity in later sections.
