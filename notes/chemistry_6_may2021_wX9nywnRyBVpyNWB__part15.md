## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **trend-interpretation analysis** section. The sequence is:

1. **State the trend in own data** — establishes what is being explained.
2. **Acknowledge competing model choices** — opens the possibility that more than one fit is defensible.
3. **Cite literature supporting the chosen trend shape** — authorises a specific model before installing it.
4. **Install the chosen model with its equation** — gives a concrete artefact (the equation) to interpret.
5. **Unpack the coefficients physically** — converts the equation into a verbal claim about behaviour.
6. **Introduce the statistical evaluation tool** — raises the question "but how good is the fit?"
7. **Define the tool's meaning/threshold** — gives the reader the ruler.
8. **Apply the tool to own fit and read off the result** — answers "how good?"
9. **Cite authority for the threshold value** — defends the qualitative label ("strong").
10. **Introduce an alternative model as contrast** — opens the comparison that complicates step 8.
11. **Apply the alternative and show it scores better** — destabilises the first model's claim to being "best."
12. **Interpret the alternative model physically** — converts the second equation into a behavioural claim.
13. **Concede a limitation of the alternative** — restores balance; (text cuts off before the limitation is completed).

Generalised: *observe → name competing fits → back chosen fit by authority → write equation → read equation → introduce goodness-of-fit metric → apply metric → benchmark with authority → pivot to alternative fit → show alternative scores higher → interpret alternative → concede limitation.* The order matters because each move either **licenses** (literature enables model choice), **installs** (equation enables interpretation), **tests** (stat tool evaluates), or **destabilises** (alternative fit problematises the verdict) — and the section never skips a licence step before installing.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — "Install and interpret a chosen model"
**SKELETON:** "[Figure reference] illustrates a [direction] correlation between [independent variable] and [dependent variable]. [Model A] or [Model B] could be applied to the data. When referring to literature, [source A in similar range] and [source B in different range] suggest that a [chosen shape] correlation is present. A [chosen model] line of best fit is thereby applied to the data, represented by [colour] line on [graph], the equation is [equation]. The [sign/magnitude] of the [coefficient] suggests, with increasing '[x]' value, the '[y]' value will become [qualitative change]."

1. **Slot holds & shape:**
   - Slot 1: a figure reference + "illustrates" + direction of correlation + two variables (declarative).
   - Slot 2: a disjunction naming two model families (declarative with modal "could").
   - Slot 3: two parenthetical authority citations bracketing range; a "suggest that" clause naming the chosen shape (compound sentence).
   - Slot 4: "A [model]… is thereby applied" + visual locator + the equation as an appositive (declarative with embedded symbol string).
   - Slot 5: "The [sign] of the [coefficient] suggests…" — a single declarative unpacking the coefficient's meaning.
2. **How to fill with a DIFFERENT idea:**
   - Slot 1: pick the figure that shows your IV vs DV; name the direction of the trend you see.
   - Slot 2: list the two model families your data could plausibly fit (e.g. exponential vs logarithmic).
   - Slot 3: find two literature sources — one in a range like yours, one in a different range — whose findings match the shape you will commit to; cite with author names in parentheses.
   - Slot 4: install the chosen fit, describe its visual position (colour, line number), paste your equation.
   - Slot 5: state what the leading coefficient implies physically — increasing/decreasing, accelerating/decelerating.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / installation:** *""A linear line of best fit is thereby applied to the data""* (consequence marker *thereby* — installs the model that the previous sentence authorised).

**Cause / consequence:** *""suggest that a negative linear correlation exists""* (*suggest* — used to convert literature into a licence); *""The negative coefficient… suggests… the 'y' value will become numerically smaller""* (*suggests* — converts coefficient into behavioural consequence); *""this is seen mathematically, as the equation contains""* (*as* — converts physical claim into formal grounding).

**Contrast / concession:** *""Alternatively, a curve of best fit could be applied""* (*Alternatively* — opens a competitor); *""However, the problem with taking a quadratic equation as a curve of best fit, is""* (*However* — pivots to limitation).

**Specification / narrowing:** *""the equation is 𝑦 = −0.001880𝑥 + 0.982522""* (appositional specification of the model just named); *""The closer an R2 value is to 1, the stronger the correlation""* (correlative specification defining the metric).

**Evidence handling / authority:** *""When referring to literature, studies… (Jakubowska)""* (authority by parenthetical citation); *""Strong correlations are illustrated by R2 values greater than 0.750 (Francis)""* (authority defending the chosen threshold); *""suggesting that when represented as a linear relationship""* (hedged reading of own data).

**Explanation / mechanism verbs:** *""illustrates a negative correlation""* (*illustrates* — names a trend); *""can be used to find out the strength""* (*can be used to* — introduces evaluator); *""may represent correlation better""* (*may represent* — hedged claim about superiority); *""The closer… the stronger""* (aphoristic definition).

**Hedging language:** *""could be applied""*, *""may represent correlation better""*, *""suggesting that… the correlation is strong""* — the section consistently softens claims while still progressing logically.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The section uses a **"trend → authorised model → installed equation → unpacked coefficient → statistical evaluation → benchmarked alternative → mechanism reading → concession"** explanation pattern. To explain a NEW idea the same way:

1. **Open with the observed trend in your own data** — one sentence naming direction (positive/negative/constant) between your IV and DV, using *illustrates*.
2. **Name competing model families** — one sentence listing the two shapes your data could plausibly fit, using *could be applied*.
3. **Authorise the chosen shape with literature** — one sentence citing two sources (one in a similar range, one in a different range), using *When referring to literature… suggest that*.
4. **Install the chosen fit with its equation** — one sentence using *thereby* (consequence) + a visual locator (colour, line number) + the equation as an appositive.
5. **Unpack the leading coefficient** — one sentence using *The [sign] coefficient of the [term] suggests…* to convert the symbol into a behavioural claim.
6. **Introduce the statistical evaluator** — one sentence using *can be used to find out the strength of the relationship*.
7. **Define the metric's ideal and your threshold** — one aphoristic sentence (*The closer… the stronger*) followed by an authority citation defending your cutoff (*Strong X are illustrated by values greater than [N] ([Author])*).
8. **Apply the metric and label the strength** — one sentence reporting your value and what it qualifies as.
9. **Pivot to an alternative model** — one sentence using *Alternatively* and naming the competitor.
10. **Show the alternative scores higher and interpret it physically** — one or two sentences: present its equation, state the higher metric value, then use *this is seen mathematically, as the equation contains…* to ground the behavioural reading in a formal feature.
11. **Concede a limitation of the alternative** — one sentence starting with *However* that names a cost of the alternative model (the text cuts off, but the move is "name what the statistically better model fails to capture").

The pattern works because each step either **licences** (3), **installs** (4), **interprets** (5, 10), **tests** (6–8), or **destabilises** (9–11) — and the reader is never asked to interpret an equation that has not yet been installed, nor to evaluate a model that has not yet been cited.
