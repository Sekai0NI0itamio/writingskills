## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is a **discussion / scope-and-positioning coda**. The ordered moves are:

1. **Reaffirm breadth of method** — remind the reader the technique generalises beyond the tested case.
2. **Concede a boundary** — flag where the method stops working.
3. **Instantiate the boundary with a conditional example** — show, via a specific variable dependence, why it fails.
4. **State the remedy in one beat** — tell the reader what would have to be done instead.
5. **Pivot to prior literature ("Finally")** — move from internal scope to external positioning.
6. **Claim equivalence of numerical output** — assert that the results match an earlier paper.
7. **Distinguish by interpretation** — undercut the equivalence by showing the *model* differs, not just the numbers.

**Why that order:** establishing internal scope *before* ceding the floor to outside work lets the student be honest about the method's limits without undermining the paper; the literature comparison then acts as a *triangulation* move — same answer, different route — which reinforces the credibility of the student's own path. A replicateable student should always: (a) state what generalises, (b) admit what doesn't, (c) show it concretely, (d) then situate against someone else's answer.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The dominant pattern in this section is **scope-with-concession, then an interpretive contrast against prior literature**. To replicate it on a new idea:

1. **Anchor to a prior case** in one short comparative clause ("as in X") so the reader carries the analogy forward without re-orienting.
2. **State the general reach of your method** in one sentence, packaging the method itself in parentheses so the technical content does not interrupt the claim.
3. **Insert "however" mid-sentence** to convert the reach claim into a scope claim — this is the hinge of the paragraph; do not split it into two sentences or the contrast softens.
4. **Instantiate the boundary as a conditional** ("if [variable] depends on [parameter]…") so the limitation is concrete, not vague.
5. **Name the lost structural property** ("would not be [separable / linear / continuous]…") so the reader sees the mechanism, not just the fact, of failure.
6. **Close with a "so" clause** stating the remedy in 4–6 words — this gives the paragraph a verdict and prevents it from sounding merely critical.
7. **Open the next paragraph with "Finally, it is noted that…"** to signal you are now positioning against the literature, not extending your own scope.
8. **Claim equivalence of output, hedged** ("appear to be very similar, if not identical") — never flat "the same as".
9. **Cite the prior work in compact form** (Name [#]) inside the equivalence clause.
10. **Use a second "however" to pivot from output-equivalence to assumption-difference**, isolating *one* modelling assumption so the contrast is sharp and falsifiable.
11. **End with a single demonstrative pointer** ("This…") to stage the elaboration that follows the cut-off.
