## What This Section Does (content sequence)
This is a **process/mechanism explanation with an embedded return loop**. The ordered moves are:

1. **State the function** — what the system is *for* (reduces friction). This licenses the rest of the paragraph by giving it purpose.
2. **Identify the energy/power source** — what *drives* the system. The reader must know who "pushes" the oil before being told where it goes.
3. **Trace the intake path** — entry of the fluid into the driving component. Sets up filtration.
4. **Trace the cleaning step** — because contaminated oil would damage the very parts the system exists to protect; the reason for cleaning is implicit from the earlier friction-reduction purpose.
5. **Trace the primary distribution route** — through the main rotating component.
6. **Add the secondary/branching route** — using "also" because there is more than one place that needs lubrication.
7. **Close the loop with the return path** — the drain and re-entry. Without closure the reader is left mid-cycle.

A student replicating this on a new topic (e.g., a cooling system, a hydraulic brake, a household water heater) should follow the same order: **purpose → power source → intake → cleaning/processing → main route → branch route → return loop**.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing:** "Oil is then sucked…" / "The oil then passes through…" — two "then" markers carry the reader leg-by-leg through the cycle.
- **Cause/consequence:** "due to its connection to the drive belt" — packs a cause phrase into the very first sentence so the reader knows *why* the pump works at all.
- **Specification / addition:** "The oil lines are also connected to oil sprouts…" — "also" introduces a parallel branch the reader didn't yet know about.
- **Definition/identification through relative clause:** "an oil filter which ensures to clean oil dust particles" / "oil sprouts that spread oil upwards to lubricate the piston" / "the sump, which gathers up the used oil" — "which/that" clauses are used to specify what each *named component actually does*.
- **Purpose infinitive:** "to lubricate them" / "to lubricate the piston" / "to be circulated again in the engine" — every destination clause ends with a "to + verb" stating *why* the oil is being sent there.
- **Excess/return marker:** "which excesses" — a relative-clause flag for surplus, setting up the drain.
- **Cyclical closure:** "returns the oil into the pump to be circulated again" — "again" closes the loop explicitly.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section relies on the pattern: **function statement → energy source → sequenced process trace → branch → return loop**. To explain a *new* idea (any closed mechanical/chemical cycle) in the same way:

1. **Open with the system's job.** One clause: what it *does* (reduces / cools / transmits / filters). This gives every later step a reason to exist.
2. **Name the driver and its power source in one sentence.** Use "due to its connection to…" so the reader sees both the component and the *cause* of its motion at the same moment.
3. **Begin the fluid's journey with "then"** at the intake. The word "then" signals to the reader that a sequence has started; reuse it at every new leg.
4. **Insert a cleaning/conditioning step immediately after intake.** Phrase it as a relative clause ("which ensures to remove X") so the component is both named and defined in one breath.
5. **Move to the primary destination.** Use another "then" + a prepositional phrase ("through [channel] and [internal feature]") plus a "to + verb" purpose clause so the reader knows *what work the fluid does there*.
6. **Add the branch with "also."** Say the *same* channel is "also connected to" a second outlet; describe the directional spread with "that spread [fluid] [direction]."
7. **Close the loop.** Mark the surplus with a relative clause ("which excesses"), name the collection point ("the sump"), and end with "returns … into the [driver] to be circulated again." The word "again" is the single marker that tells the reader the explanation has come full circle.
