## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This section is a **primary-source interview transcript** whose moves must follow the logic of evidence-gathering, not narrative storytelling.

1. **Meta-frame first** — establish the researcher, the interviewee, the location, and the academic purpose. Why: a transcript must prove authenticity and relevance before any claim can be cited; it also tells the reader the interview's scope (single co-owner, single site).
2. **Origin question** — probe motivation/personal driver. Why: motivation grounds later financial numbers in human rationale, giving the reader a "why" before a "how much."
3. **Temporal anchor (founding dates)** — pin the business in time. Why: dates allow the reader to interpret growth figures as trajectories, not snapshots.
4. **Capital-structure question** — move from inputs of ideas → inputs of money. Why: the reader needs to know how the business was funded before sales figures become meaningful (a loan vs. self-funded business carries different risk).
5. **Break-even projection** — supply the threshold figure. Why: a target alone is abstract; pairing it with the next question turns it into a comparable benchmark.
6. **Past-vs-present sales comparison** — measure performance against both the trajectory and the break-even. Why: comparison is the natural follow-up once a threshold is on the table.
7. **Product-range question** — pivot from results to the engine behind the results. Why: a reader seeing sales below break-even must be shown the product and channel levers that could close the gap.

**Generalised sequence for replication:** Meta-frame → motivation → chronology → capital → target benchmark → performance vs. benchmark → product/channel levers. Each move supplies the *input* the next move *needs* to be interpretable.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / chain-handovers**
- "First off, lest start with your business" — transitional opener that announces the first research move.
- "So, when did Kreme De La Kreme start?" — "So" chains the date question to the prior origin answer.
- "So, what were your sales like when you started, and how is it now?" — "So" + comparative "and how is it now" chains the comparison question to the break-even threshold.

**Causal / consequential connectors**
- "So, we wanted to bring quality Gelato to people" — "So" converts personal passion into market intent (cause → motive).

**Qualification / concession**
- "which we are yet to repay" — relative-clause concession that tempers the funding answer with ongoing liability.

**Evidence / claim verbs**
- "We started with the planning and the working around October 2017" — temporal evidence verb "started with."
- "We expect that we would need monthly sales of about 300,000 INR…to break even" — projection verb "expect" + threshold noun "break even."
- "We mainly offer different flavors of gelato, which is made in-house" — offering verb "offer" + control phrase "made in-house."

**Explanation / mechanism phrases**
- "which is made in-house" — defines the production locus.
- "We have a professional chef who develops all the flavors" — names the human capital behind the control claim.
- "to break even" — defines the financial benchmark in operational terms.

**Enumeration / specification**
- "sundaes, shakes, cookies, waffles, and brownies" — comma list with final "and" for parallel SKUs.
- "We sell the brownies in bulk to another local café" — specifies one SKU as a wholesale channel.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section relies on a **"probe → answer → specify → chain" pattern** for primary-source interviews, and on a **"core claim → unpack mechanism → extend list → flag one extension"** pattern for product/strategy answers. To explain a NEW idea (e.g., a different business area) using the same pattern, follow these steps:

1. **Open with a meta-frame sentence** stating the research purpose and the entity under study. This pre-empts the reader's "why am I reading this?" question and gives every later quote an audit trail.
2. **Pose a single, open probe question** that targets the dimension you want to evidence (origin, capital, staffing, marketing, etc.). Keep one question per turn so the answer stays focused.
3. **Anchor the answer in a personal/team driver first**, then convert it into a market-facing motive using a "So, we wanted to…" causal connector. This makes motivation evidence-based rather than asserted.
4. **Supply dates or numbers in pairs** (planning date + launch date; start figure + current figure) so the reader can extract a trajectory at a glance without re-reading.
5. **Pair a target/benchmark sentence with a comparative probe** ("We expect X to break even" → "So, what was Y then and now?"). The benchmark gives the comparison its meaning.
6. **In product/strategy answers, open with a "mainly offer…made in-house" core-claim sentence that establishes both the SKU and the locus of production in one line.
8. **Immediately unpack the control claim with a human-capital clause** ("We have a [role] who develops all the [parts]") — this converts a vague boast ("in-house") into a verifiable mechanism.
9. **Enumerate complementary items in a comma-then-final-and list** so the reader can see the breadth of the offering in a single beat.
10. **Flag one item as a B2B channel** ("We sell [item] in bulk to [partner] who [resells]") to signal that the offering has both direct and indirect revenue paths.
11. **End every answer-block at a point where one more piece of information is logically owed** (e.g., after naming the B2B partner, the channel mechanics are owed) — this is the handoff that lets the next question follow without forcing.
