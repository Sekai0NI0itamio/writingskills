## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1**
1. **Sentence 1 (Claim + Evidence + Cause):** "the metals in the half cells, because Fe2+ has a more positive electrode potential value, it will be the more readily reduced species" — states the ranking cause (electrode potential values) and the consequence (which species is reduced). Hands to next by handing the reader the identity of which metal plays which role, which the next sentence needs.
2. **Sentence 2 (Verdict):** "hence this experiment has an iron cathode and zinc anode." — applies the verdict to the experimental setup, turning the theoretical ranking into the physical arrangement. Hands to the next paragraph by introducing the table that visualises those assignments.

**Paragraph 2 (table)** — functions as a visual inventory of half-reactions, net reactions, and cell-diagram convention; the following prose paragraph depends on its labels ("cathode", "anode", "salt bridge").

**Paragraph 3**
1. **Sentence 1 (Specification — concentrations):** "The solution at the cathode and anode was 1.0M ferrous (II) sulphate and 1.0M zinc sulphate, respectively." — fills in the chemical identity and concentration for each half-cell, specifying what the table left generic. Hands to next by pivoting from the cell contents to the bridge between them.
2. **Sentence 2 (Definition + Cross-reference):** "The salt bridge, represented by the double-vertical line on the cell diagram, is 1.0M potassium nitrate solution." — defines the salt bridge by tying the symbol in the table to its real composition. Hands to next by shifting from setup specifics to the thermodynamic behaviour of the cell.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A galvanic-cell setup section makes these moves, in this order, for this reason:
1. **Ranking cause → identity of electrodes** (which metal is cathode, which anode) — sets up every later label.
2. **Inventory of equations and notation** (half-reactions, net forward, net reversible, cell diagram) — gives the reader the symbols that the following prose must refer back to.
3. **Concentrations of each half-cell solution** — turns the generic table entries into measured specifics.
4. **Composition of the salt bridge, explicitly tied to its cell-diagram symbol** — closes the loop between diagram and bench, because earlier the reader saw "||" but was not told what it physically is.
5. **Energy/sign verdict on the cell type with citation** — caps the paragraph by classifying the cell's behaviour (spontaneous, exothermic).

The order matters: identity must come before equations (you cannot write "cathode reaction" before saying what the cathode is); equations must come before concentrations (concentrations attach to the species named in the equations); salt-bridge identification must come before closing the cell description (the bridge links them, so it is the bridge of the setup); the verdict comes last because it is a summary characterisation, not a setup step.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — "Ranking-cause → electrode-identity verdict"**
SKELETON: "[Species/entities] in the [system], because [property X] has a [comparative] value, it will be the more readily [action] species and hence this [experiment/context] has [outcome arrangement]."

1. *Slot 1 ("the metals in the half cells"):* the entities occupying two comparable roles, named in the plural and in the system.
2. *Slot 2 ("Fe2+ has a more positive electrode potential value"):* the comparative property that determines the ranking, with one specific value cited.
3. *Slot 3 ("more readily reduced species"):* the consequence verb-phrase that the property dictates.
4. *Slot 4 ("this experiment has an iron cathode and zinc anode"):* the concrete arrangement of named roles in the actual investigation.
5. *Fill instructions:* pick two competing entities; pick a measurable property that ranks them; state the ranking with a comparative ("more positive," "higher," "lower"); derive which role each plays; name the physical arrangement. Use past/present tense consistent with the experimental voice.

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / ordering connectors:** "respectively" — slots different substances against named positions in one clause.
- **Causal / consequence verbs:** "hence" — turns the electrode-potential ranking into the physical arrangement; "will be" — projects the consequence of the ranking.
- **Comparison / ranking language:** "more positive electrode potential value," "more readily reduced species" — frames the decision as a comparative scale, not a binary choice.
- **Specification / cross-reference:** "represented by the double-vertical line on the cell diagram" — pins the abstract symbol to the real component.
- **Explanation verbs:** "is" used definitionally ("The salt bridge … is 1.0M potassium nitrate solution") to convert a labelled part into a substance.
- **Authority / evidence handling:** "(Columbia University)" — parentheses-cited source appended to the verdict clause "Galvanic cells are spontaneous and exothermic."
- **Role-labelling nouns:** "cathode," "anode," "reduction," "oxidation," "galvanic cells" — technical labels that let the reader map prose onto equations.
- **Spatial/conventional notation cues:** "Half-Reaction at the cathode (Reduction)" — embeds the role in parentheses to bind symbol to function.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section relies on is: **comparative property → role assignment → symbolic inventory → physical-specification cross-reference → classification verdict with citation**.

To explain a NEW idea (e.g. another galvanic cell, an electrolytic cell, or any two-component linked system) in the same pattern:

1. **Identify the comparative property** that ranks the two candidates (electrode potential, pKa, resistivity, etc.) and state which candidate has the higher/lower value of it.
2. **Derive the role assignment** as a direct consequence of that ranking (use "hence" or "therefore" so the reader sees the deduction, not an assertion).
3. **List the symbolic inventory** — half-reactions or equivalent equations, the net forward reaction, the net reversible form, and any diagrammatic convention — in a labelled table so later prose can refer back by name.
4. **Specify physical contents**, attaching each named role to a real substance and concentration using "respectively" so the reader can map prose onto the table.
5. **Cross-reference any diagrammatic symbol** (e.g. "double-vertical line," "single line," "dashed boundary") to its real physical component, naming concentration and identity.
6. **Close with a classification verdict** on the system's behaviour (spontaneous/exothermic, endothermic, non-spontaneous) and cite the source in parentheses so the reader can verify the claim.

This order forces the reader to know *why* before *what*, and *what* before *how it looks on paper*, before the system is finally classified.
