3. Move: **closed yes/no consent request** — "Is it okay with you?" → Hands reader forward by **requiring an answer**; no topic can be opened until consent is given.

**Paragraph 2 — Broad opening question**

**Paragraph 3 — Resource probe (general)**

4. Move: **concession / scope-softener** — "As everything, it could be improved." → Hands reader forward by **closing the resource question on a soft generalisation**, which sets up a concrete "what would you want?" probe.

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

This is an **interview transcript section**, and the ordered content sequence is:

1. **Ethical gate (recording + consent).** Must come first because nothing can be recorded, said, or cited without the interviewee's agreement; it legally and ethically unlocks the rest.
2. **Broad opening question on future plans.** Comes second because it sets the *macro frame* (department direction) before any specific sub-issue can be probed.
3. **Resource adequacy question (general).** Comes third because it tests whether the macro plan is realistic — equipment is the most concrete operationalisation of "improvement."
4. **Resource wish-list question (specific).** Comes fourth because the previous answer named a gap; the natural next move is to populate that gap with concrete items.
5. **Implication question (does the gap cause harm?).** Comes fifth because once a gap is named and populated, the logical question is whether it *matters* — i.e. consequences for students/teachers.
6. **Student-suggested example / closed yes-no wrap-up.** Comes sixth because the interviewer must confirm the soft claim and then generalise before pivoting topics.
7. **Topic pivot to external partner with a bridging awareness question.** Comes after internal PE is exhausted; the new entity is positioned against an analogue (another department / external club).
8. **Hypothetical scenario ("If…").** Comes last in this segment because once the new entity is introduced and the interviewee is shown to have no current opinion, the only remaining logical move is a *what-if* probe — which is also where the transcript ends, suggesting the next speaker turn would have answered.

A student replicating this should: **gate → macro frame → concrete resource probe → populated example → consequence check → wrap-up → new-entity awareness → hypothetical.** Each move *earns* the right to the next by closing the prior slot.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — "Consent Gate"

**SKELETON:** "[Procedural statement that I will record]. Thus I am asking for your [permission/consent] to [record]. Is it okay with you?"

**Slot instructions:**
- **Slot 1 (procedural statement, first-person future):** State the act (recording) and the *end product* (transcript / record). Past-or-present framing fails because consent is for a *future* act.
- **Slot 2 (justification + request, present tense):** Use a connector ("Thus", "Therefore") to bind the request to slot 1, and explicitly name the *thing* being asked for (consent/permission).
- **Slot 3 (closed yes/no question, second person):** End with a direct yes/no interrogative; do not embed another question here.

**Original fill:** "I will be recording this interview in order to create a transcript in the end for my IA. Thus I am asking for your consent and permission to record. Is it okay with you?"

**Demonstration fill (different idea — photography consent for a pupil):** "I will be taking photographs of your class during the experiment in order to include them in my written report. Therefore I am asking for your consent and permission to photograph. Is that okay with you?"

### Skeleton B — "Broad-to-Narrow Probe with Contrast Answer"

**SKELETON:** "Okay, for the first question, what are your [broad topic]? — [It is fine for X, but not for Y, specifically Z]. [General softening clause]."

**Slot instructions:**
- **Slot 1 (sequence marker):** Open with "Okay" plus an ordinal ("first") to show this is the *start* of a structured sequence.
- **Slot 2 (broad open question, second person):** Use a *what / do you* interrogative on a wide-scope noun phrase (department, programme, policy).
- **Slot 3 (split verdict with contrast connector):** Answer must use a balancing construction ("adequate for… but not for…") to force a follow-up specification.
- **Slot 4 (specific narrowing):** Use "specifically" or "in particular" to name the lacking sub-category.
- **Slot 5 (soft generalisation / concession):** Close with a sweeping comparative ("As everything, …", "Like anything, …") to prevent the answer feeling like a complaint.

**Original fill:** "Okay, for the first question, what are your future plans for the PE department? — It is adequate for some of the sports, but not for others, specifically the ones we do not offer. As everything, it could be improved."

**Demonstration fill (different idea — school library):** "Okay, for the first question, what are your plans for the school library this year? — It is sufficient for the fiction section, but not for the non-fiction area, specifically the science reference shelves. As everything, it could be modernised."

### Skeleton C — "Wish-Item with Built-in Constraint"

**SKELETON:** "[Follow-up question asking for concrete items]. — [It would be nice to have X, however, we also need Y which we lack]. [Student offers example]? [Yes, and category expands]."

**Slot instructions:**
- **Slot 1 (concrete follow-up, second person):** Use "What are some of the [items] you wish [entity] had?" — keeps the question narrow but plural.
- **Slot 2 (desire + contrast connector + constraint):** Answer must contain *both* a wish ("nice to have") *and* a but-clause naming a second barrier (space, money, staffing).
- **Slot 3 (student candidate, "Like X for example?"):** Student proposes one concrete instance to test whether the category is understood.
- **Slot 4 (confirmation + expansion, ellipsis-friendly):** Interviewee confirms and lists one or two more items; the answer often trails with "…" because it is conversational.

**Original fill:** "What are some of the equipment you wish that the PE department had? — I think it would be nice to have some proper gym equipment, however, we also need proper space for it which we lack. Like dumb bells for examples? Yeah, like dumb bells and multi gym things…"

**Demonstration fill (different idea — music department):** "What instruments would you like the music department to add? — It would be nice to have a class set of keyboards, however, we also need proper storage cabinets which we lack. Like electric pianos, for example? Yeah, electric pianos and synthesisers…"

### Skeleton D — "Consequence Check with Soft Denial"

**SKELETON:** "[Causal question linking the prior gap to outcomes]. — [I do not think it causes Z, however, obviously having more X would be more beneficial]. [Student summary: So would you like X overall]? [Confirmation + analogy]."

**Slot instructions:**
- **Slot 1 (causal probe, second person):** Use "Do you believe that [lack of X] limits [outcome]?" — forces a yes/no on a *consequence*.
- **Slot 2 (soft denial + concession):** Answer must *deny the strong claim* but *affirm the mild one*; uses "however" to split the two halves.
- **Slot 3 (student restatement, "So…?"):** Compresses the soft claim into a closed yes/no to extract confirmation.
- **Slot 4 (analogy-based generalisation):** Final answer extends the claim to a *class of entities* ("just like any other…"), preparing the next topic pivot.

**Original fill:** "Do you believe that lack of equipment like these limits the potential of students and/or teachers? — I do not think it limits the potential, however, obviously having more equipment would be more beneficial. So would you like more equipment overall in PE? Yep, having a higher range of equipment is obviously better just like any other educational department in our school."

**Demonstration fill (different idea — art supplies):** "Do you believe the shortage of art supplies limits students' creative output? — I do not think it limits the output, however, obviously having more materials would be more enriching. So would you welcome a bigger art budget overall? Yes, a wider range of materials is obviously better, just like any other creative department in our school."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

**Sequencing / ordering**
- "for the first question" — opening an ordered list of probes.
- "Okay, for the first" — same, prefixed with the procedural "Okay".

**Cause / consequence**
- "in order to create a transcript" — purpose-clause marker naming the *end* of the act.
- "Thus I am asking" — result-clause connector binding request to purpose.

**Contrast / concession**
- "however, obviously having more equipment would be more beneficial" — splits a denial from a concession in one breath.
- "however, we also need proper space for it which we lack" — embeds a second barrier inside a wish-statement.
- "but not for others" — half of the split-verdict construction.
- "As everything, it could be improved" — universal-comparative softener.

**Specification / narrowing**
- "specifically the ones we do not offer" — narrows the denial half of the split verdict.
- "Like dumb bells for examples?" — student offers one *instance* to pin the category.

**Evidence handling**
- (No formal "according to" — the transcript *is* the evidence.) The verb "I've heard of it" is itself the citation of awareness.

**Explanation / framing verbs**
- "I would first like to start by saying that I will be recording" — opening-routine verb ("start by saying") used to mark an ethical frame.
- "I do not have an opinion on it" — neutral-stance marker that *unlocks* the upcoming hypothetical.
- "This is already happening to an extent" — reality-check verb that converts a hypothetical into an existing partial practice.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

This section uses the **Gate → Macro-frame → Narrow-and-Populate → Consequence → Soft-Denial-and-Generalise → Pivot-to-New-Entity → Hypothetical** pattern. Each step below is one move the interviewer makes.

1. **Gate first.** State the recording/consent act, link it to a concrete end-product ("transcript for my IA") with a result connector ("Thus"), then close with a yes/no question. Do not start topic work before consent.
2. **Open with the broadest possible question** about the interviewee's *plans/direction/vision*. Use an ordinal ("first") so the reader knows more questions will follow.
3. **Move from plan to resource.** Ask whether the most visible *operational tool* (equipment / budget / staffing) is adequate — this tests whether the macro plan is realistic.
4. **Force a split verdict with "but".** When the interviewee answers, the structure "adequate for X but not for Y" naturally produces a follow-up that names Y. Always demand a *specific* sub-category with "specifically" or "in particular".
5. **Populate the gap with a concrete wish-list question**, then offer a *student-suggested example* ("Like X, for example?") so the answer is forced to confirm *and* expand the category.
6. **Probe consequence.** Ask whether the gap *limits* an outcome. Expect a *soft denial* ("I do not think it limits… however, obviously… would be more beneficial") — this is the standard interviewee move and you should not fight it; instead, *summary it* back ("So would you like X overall?").
7. **Generalise with an analogy** ("just like any other…") in the interviewee's final answer — this is the cue for you to *pivot topics*.
8. **Bridge to a new external entity** with an awareness check ("Have you heard of X, and what do you think about it?"). If the answer is neutral, escalate to a **hypothetical conditional** ("If X and Y established Z, what would your opinion be?"). The hypothetical is the natural endpoint of a neutral-awareness answer.

To explain a new idea with the same logic, decide first which *gap* you want to surface, then walk backwards through the steps: gap → populated example → consequence question → resource adequacy → macro plan → consent gate. Each step must *earn* the right to ask the next by closing or specifying the prior slot.
