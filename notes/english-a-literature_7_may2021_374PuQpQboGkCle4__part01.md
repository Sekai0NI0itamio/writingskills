## Paragraph Flow (move by move)

**Paragraph 1** (one continuous paragraph that crosses a page break)

1. **Sentence 1** — Move: **Claim about technique and its dual purpose.** "The poet uses direct address to captivate the readers and emphasize the relevancy…" Hands the reader to the next sentence by **asserting relevance** — once "relevancy" has been claimed, the next sentence must show what specifically the poem is relevant *to* (i.e. the anthology's content/theme).

## What This Section Does (content sequence)

1. **Technique claim with paired purpose.** A short claim that names a literary device and its *two* functions (e.g. "captivate" + "emphasize relevance"). *Why first:* it justifies why the poem merits analysis at all, so the rest of the paragraph feels earned rather than arbitrary.
2. **Work identification + central-theme statement.** A sentence that names the collection and pins a single emotion/idea to it as "central" and "a major concern." *Why second:* it converts the technique claim into a concrete object of study and raises the specific term ("resentment") that will be defined.
3. **Authority-quoted definition.** A sentence introducing an expert by name and embedding a short, quotable definition of that term. *Why third:* it converts the abstract term into a precise academic concept and lends the paragraph critical weight before the writer's own argument begins.
4. **Extension/paraphrase of the authority into a general principle.** A sentence that retains the authority's voice ("He is of the opinion that…") but generalises the definition to "subdominant groups." *Why fourth:* the quoted definition is narrow; the paraphrase widens it into a claim the writer can then re-apply to the text's actual subjects.
5. **Application to the specific group in the text.** A sentence that names "Women" and begins specifying how they have been treated. *Why last:* every preceding move has been preparing the conceptual machinery needed to say something concrete about the text's characters.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

### Skeleton A — Full-paragraph template

SKELETON: "The poet uses [TECHNIQUE] to [FUNCTION 1] and [FUNCTION 2]. '[WORK]' is a [GENRE] in which [THEME] is central to the [ELEMENTS] presented and can be considered a major concern of the poet. According to [AUTHORITY], [THEME] can be described as '[QUOTED DEFINITION]'. [AUTHORITY] is of the opinion that [GENERAL CLAIM about a group/condition]. [SPECIFIC GROUP], who have been [VERB-ed]…"

1. **Slots and shapes:**
   - Slot 1 (technique claim): noun phrase naming a device + *to* + verb + object + *and* + verb + object.
   - Slot 2 (work + theme): title in quotes + copula + genre noun + *in which* + theme noun phrase + *is central to* + plural noun + *and can be considered* + noun phrase.
   - Slot 3 (authority definition): *According to* + name + *can be described as* + short quote.
   - Slot 4 (extension): *He/She is of the opinion that* + clause generalising to a category of people.
   - Slot 5 (application): *Specific group*, + relative clause beginning *who have been…*
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Pick a poet/text with an identifiable technique; choose a single emotion or concept the work explores; find one academic source that defines that concept in a quotable phrase; generalise that definition to "individuals of [X] groups"; then name the specific group in your text as the lead-in to your worked example.
3. **Original filled version:** "The poet uses direct address to captivate the readers and emphasize the relevancy of the poem's meaning. 'The World's Wife' is an anthology in which the emotion of resentment is central to the stories of the women presented and can be considered a major concern of the poet. According to the sociologist Warren D. TenHouten, resentment can be described as 'arising out of powerlessness…neither forgotten nor forgiven'. He is of the opinion that individuals of subdominant groups are prone to feel resentful towards those treating them unfairly. Women, who have been treated…"
4. **Demonstration fill (different idea, same skeleton):** "The poet uses first-person interior monologue to immerse the reader in the speaker's collapse and undercut the reliability of confession. 'Ariel' is a sequence in which the tension between liberation and self-destruction is central to the imagery presented and can be considered a major concern of the poet. According to the critic Aliyah Busby, self-destruction can be described as 'a hunger for the unbearable, performed as flight'. She is of the opinion that individuals confined by domestic expectation are prone to romanticise rupture as autonomy. Women, who have been measured by the household…"

### Skeleton B — Authority → definition → extension mini-skeleton

SKELETON: "According to [AUTHORITY], [TERM] can be described as '[QUOTED DEFINITION]'. [AUTHORITY] is of the opinion that [GENERAL CLAIM about a category of people]."

1. **Slots and shapes:**
   - Slot 1: *According to* + proper name + , + term + *can be described as* + short quoted phrase.
   - Slot 2: pronoun referring to authority + *is of the opinion that* + clause containing a plural or categorical noun.
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Choose a critic with a punchy short definition; restate that definition in your own clause that names a class of people affected.
3. **Original filled version:** "According to the sociologist Warren D. TenHouten, resentment can be described as 'arising out of powerlessness…neither forgotten nor forgiven'. He is of the opinion that individuals of subdominant groups are prone to feel resentful towards those treating them unfairly."
4. **Demonstration fill:** "According to the sociologist Beverly Skeggs, respectability can be described as 'a classed resource, neither equally distributed nor self-authored'. She is of the opinion that women of working-class origin are prone to be denied the legitimacy their respectability seeks to confer."

### Skeleton C — Opening technique claim

SKELETON: "The poet uses [TECHNIQUE] to [EFFECT 1] and [EFFECT 2]."

1. **Slots and shapes:** singular noun (device) + *to* + bare verb + object + *and* + bare verb + object.
2. **How to fill with a different idea:** Name one concrete device from your poem; pair it with two verbs of effect — one aimed at the *reader* (e.g. captivate, implicate, unsettle) and one aimed at *meaning* (e.g. emphasise, foreground, dramatise).
3. **Original filled version:** "The poet uses direct address to captivate the readers and emphasize the relevancy of the poem's meaning."
4. **Demonstration fill:** "The poet uses second-person imperative to implicate the reader and dramatise the complicity of the addressed figure."

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

- **Sequencing / evidence-source marker:** "**According to** the sociologist Warren D. TenHouten, resentment can be described…" — names the source before the claim.
- **Specification / narrowing:** "**in which** the emotion of resentment is central to the stories…" — embeds the abstract term inside a defining relative clause.
- **Specification / category marker:** "**individuals of** subdominant groups are prone to…" — converts the definition into a sociological category.
- **Explanation verb (definition):** "**can be described as** 'arising out of powerlessness…neither forgotten nor forgiven'" — frames the quote as a definition.
- **Explanation verb (framing the work):** "**can be considered** a major concern of the poet" — treats the theme as a named concern.
- **Evidence-handling / reporting verb:** "**He is of the opinion that** individuals of subdominant groups are prone…" — paraphrases the authority rather than quoting again.
- **Cause/consequence (purpose):** "**to captivate** the readers and emphasize the relevancy…" — *to* + bare infinitive marks purpose of the technique.
- **Cause/consequence (origin, inside the quote):** "**arising out of** powerlessness…" — locates the emotion's cause.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

The pattern this section runs is **literary framing → authority quote → extension of the quote → application to the text's group** (an *authority → application* explanation).

1. **Open with a technique claim paired to purpose.** State the writer's chosen device and assign it two verbs of effect, one reader-oriented and one meaning-oriented, so the paragraph announces *why* the rest matters.
2. **Name the work and isolate one central theme.** Use the construction *"'[Title]' is a [genre] in which [theme] is central… and can be considered a major concern of the poet"* to lock the paragraph onto a single term.
3. **Introduce an authority and quote their definition.** Use *According to [Name], [term] can be described as '[quote]'* — pick a quotation short enough to embed and conceptually precise enough to drive the rest of the paragraph.
4. **Extend the authority's claim with your own clause.** Use *He/She is of the opinion that [generalisation about a category of people]* so the quoted definition is broadened into a principle you can re-use.
5. **Apply the principle to the specific group in your text.** Open with the group's name and a relative clause (*Women, who have been treated…*) so the paragraph pivots from the general principle back to the text's actual case — this is where the worked example begins.
