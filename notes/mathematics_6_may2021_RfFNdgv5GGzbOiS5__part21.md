## What This Section Does (content sequence)

A bibliography performs one function: **establish the evidentiary trail of every claim made earlier in the IA.** The required sequence is:

1. **Author surname, given name (or institutional name).** Sets up identification of *who* is responsible for the source.
2. **Title of work in quotation marks.** Specifies *which* work by that author is being cited.
3. **Publication venue (italicised journal/container name).** Locates the work *where*.
4. **Publication date.** Anchors the work *when* (currency).
5. **URL or DOI string.** Provides *where the reader can retrieve it*.
6. **"Accessed [date]."** Records *when the student personally retrieved it* (verification timestamp).

The order matters because the reader scans left-to-right: who → what → where → when → link → verification. Slot 5 only makes sense if slots 1–4 are established (you cite a retrievable artifact, not a vague claim). Slot 6 only makes sense once slot 5 has been given (the access date is meaningless without a URL to access). Grouping by alphabet allows a marker/examiner to locate any source instantly.

## Paragraph Skeletons (replicable templates)

**Skeleton A — Web/news article:**

```
SKELETON: [Author Surname], [Author First Name]. "[Article Title]." [Publication Name], [Date], [URL]. Accessed [Date].
```

1. **Slot 1** = surname + comma + given name (proper noun, person).
2. **Slot 2** = article title in quotes, sentence case, end in punctuation inside quotes.
3. **Slot 3** = publication outlet name, comma, full date (Day Month Year).
5. **Slot 4** = bare URL, comma, "Accessed [Day Month Year]."
6. **Fill rule:** slot 1 picks a named journalist or academic; slot 2 picks a topical headline; slot 3 picks the outlet they wrote for; slot 4 is a publication date and link; slot 5 is the day you last opened the link.

**Original fill:** `Haahr, Mads. "RANDOM.ORG - List Randomizer." Random.org, www.random.org/lists/. Accessed 5 Apr. 2021.`

**Demonstration fill (different idea):** `Bryant, Katie. "How Lithium-Ion Battery Recycling Works." Wired, 14 Mar. 2023, www.wired.com/story/lithium-recycling-explainer/. Accessed 22 Oct. 2024.`

---

**Skeleton B — Journal article with DOI:**

```
SKELETON: [Author Surname], [Author First Name], et al. "[Article Title]." [Journal Name], vol. [#], no. [#], [Date], pp. [#]–[#]. [Publisher], [URL], [DOI]. Accessed [Date].
```

1. **Slot 1** = lead author + "et al." if 3+ authors (proper nouns).
2. **Slot 2** = article title in quotes.
3. **Slot 3** = journal name, italicised, followed by volume/issue/page numbers.
4. **Slot 4** = DOI string beginning "10.…".

**Original fill:** `Tyrovolas, Stefanos, et al. "The Burden of Disease in Saudi Arabia 1990–2017…"` (uses the full Lancet citation).

**Demonstration fill:** `Nakamura, Hiroshi, et al. "Microplastic Concentrations in Pacific Tuna." Environmental Science Letters, vol. 12, no. 4, Apr. 2024, pp. 88–101. Nature, www.nature.com/articles/example, 10.1038/s12345-024-00123-4. Accessed 2 Nov. 2024.`

---

**Skeleton C — Repeat-author short form:**

```
SKELETON: ---. "[Second Title]." [Publication], [Date], [URL]. Accessed [Date].
```

1. **Slot 1** = three em-dashes (NOT a long dash), substituting for the previous author's name.
2. **Slots 2–5** = same shape as Skeleton A/B.
3. **Fill rule:** use only when two or more works by the *exact same author (or institution)* sit adjacent in the alphabet. Three em-dashes tell the reader "same person as above."

**Original fill:** `---. "Quick Linear Regression Calculator." Www.socscistatistics.com…` (after Stangroom, Jeremy).

**Demonstration fill:** `---. "How Plastic-Eating Enzymes Were Discovered." New Scientist, 9 Jan. 2023, www.newscientist.com/example. Accessed 1 Nov. 2024.` (preceded by a first Bryant, Katie entry).

## Express-Idea Vocabulary

A bibliography does not deploy prose connectives; its "connectives" are **structural markers** that signal ordering and repetition:

- **Sequencing / ordering marker:** the em-dash placeholder "---." — e.g. `---. "Quick Linear Regression Calculator." Www.socscistatistics.com` (signals "same author as entry just before").
- **Specification marker:** quotation marks framing the title — e.g. `"Why US Health Care Costs More, but Isn't Better than Other Countries"` (signals "this is the title of the work").
- **Evidence-venue marker:** italicised container name — e.g. `The Lancet Planetary Health, vol. 4, no. 5` (signals "this is where the article lives").
- **Verification marker:** the literal word "Accessed" — e.g. `Accessed 6 Apr. 2021.` (signals "this is when I personally retrieved it").
- **Authority marker:** surname-first ordering — e.g. `Tyrovolas, Stefanos, et al.` (signals "this is the responsible author").
- **Date-shape marker:** `Day Month Year` format — e.g. `13 Sept. 2019` (signals MLA 9th-edition convention).

These are the only "moves" a bibliography performs; there are no causal, concessive, or comparative connectives because the section makes no argument.

## How to Explain an Idea (replication steps)

A bibliography uses the **documentary-evidence pattern**, not an explanatory pattern. The pattern is **identity → location → retrieval-proof**. To replicate the pattern with a NEW set of sources:

1. **Identify the responsible party.** Decide whether the source has a personal author (surname, given name) or an institutional author (organisation name spelled as it appears on the work).
2. **Specify which work.** Quote the exact title of the specific article, dataset, or tool in quotation marks, preserving original capitalisation.
3. **Locate the container.** Name the publication, database, or website that hosts the work; for journals, add volume, issue, and page range.
4. **Anchor the date.** Record the publication date in Day-Month-Year form; if no date exists (e.g., WHO entry above), write "n.d." or omit.
5. **Provide the retrieval string.** Insert the URL; for journal articles also append the DOI.
6. **Stamp the verification.** End with "Accessed [Day Month Year]." matching the day you personally opened the source.
7. **Order alphabetically by surname.** Place entries A→Z; when the same author recurs adjacent, replace their name with three em-dashes "---." on subsequent lines.

To reproduce the IA author's evidence trail on a *different* topic (say, comparing renewable-energy adoption across countries), the student would repeat steps 1–7 for: a news article on the topic, an OECD/World Bank dataset, a journal article with DOI, two or three statistical tools (correlation/regression calculators), and a repeat-author second piece — mirroring the **personal author → database → journal → tool cluster → institutional author cluster** rhythm visible in entries 1–17.
