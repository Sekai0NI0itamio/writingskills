# Thesis

**Purpose**  
A thesis is the central claim of an essay; it presents the writer's main insight or argument about the source or topic. It appears early, invites disagreement, and justifies the essay’s direction by answering a question or solving a problem.

**Structure / Moves in order**  
1. **Frame the question or problem** – state the analytical or practical issue that motivates the essay.  
2. **Explain the stakes** – clarify why the question matters to the reader.  
3. **State the thesis** – present an arguable claim that answers the question or proposes a solution.  
4. **Specify claim type** – indicate whether the claim is analytical (interpretation of evidence) or normative (value/policy).  
5. **Set scope** – ensure the claim is neither too broad nor too narrow for the available evidence.

**Sentence shapes with slot anatomy**  
1. *“While [source] argues that [X], I claim that [Y] because [evidence].”*  
   - Slots: source, opposing view, your claim, supporting evidence.  
2. *“The question of [issue] matters because [stakes]; therefore, this essay argues that [thesis].”*  
   - Slots: issue, stakes, thesis.  
3. *“In my analysis of [text], I interpret [element] as [meaning], which reveals [insight].”*  
   - Slots: text, element, meaning, insight.  
4. *“To address the problem of [problem], we should [action] in order to [value].”*  
   - Slots: problem, action, value.  
5. *“Although many assume [common view], close reading of [evidence] shows that [counter‑claim].”*  
   - Slots: common view, evidence, counter‑claim.

**Rules**  
- Make the thesis arguable; a reader should be able to disagree.  
- Frame the thesis around a question or problem you have identified.  
- Explain why the question matters (what is at stake).  
- Choose an analytical claim when interpreting sources; choose a normative claim when advocating a value or policy.  
- Keep the thesis scope precise: narrow enough to be supported by evidence, broad enough to allow depth.  
- Ground the claim in specific evidence from the text or data, not generalizations.  
- Place the thesis early in the essay, before the body paragraphs develop the argument.

**Don'ts / Defects to fix**  
- **Descriptive thesis** – merely summarizes the source; it does not invite argument. *Why:* readers already see the summary, so the essay adds nothing.  
- **Too broad thesis** – e.g., “Sandel’s argument ignores many issues.” *Why:* lacks focus, cannot be supported with limited evidence.  
- **Too narrow thesis** – e.g., “Sandel mentions HGH once.” *Why:* insufficient depth for a full essay.  
- **Obvious or uncontroversial claim** – e.g., “Genetic engineering is controversial.” *Why:* no disagreement, no need for argument.  
- **Unsupported generalization** – claiming something without textual evidence. *Why:* undermines credibility and fails the assignment.  
- **Missing stakes** – failing to explain why the question matters. *Why:* readers may not see the relevance of the essay.

**Quality bar**  
1. The thesis is arguable; a thoughtful reader could disagree.  
2. It directly answers a framed question or problem.  
3. The stakes are explicitly stated or implied.  
4. The claim type (analytical or normative) matches the assignment.  
5. The scope is appropriate: can be supported with available evidence.  
6. The thesis is specific, avoiding vague language and generalizations.
