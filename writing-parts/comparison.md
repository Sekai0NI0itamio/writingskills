# Comparison
## Purpose
Comparison makes connections between texts or ideas to move beyond mere description or summary. By reflecting on similarities and differences, you engage in critical thinking, gain a deeper understanding of the items and their relationship, and generate interesting analysis. It provides a focused roadmap for your reader, helping them navigate the points you are about to make.

## Structure / Moves in order
1. **Identify the Task:** Determine whether the assignment asks for comparison, contrast, or both, and whether it is the sole focus or part of a larger evaluation. Look for explicit words like "compare," "contrast," "similarities," and "differences," but also watch for implied comparisons.
2. **Brainstorm & Filter:** Generate a comprehensive list of similarities and differences using tools like Venn diagrams or charts. Then, filter them based on relevance to the assignment, the course, and your developing argument.
3. **Formulate a Thesis:** Replace vague reports ("A and B are similar but different") with a specific, analytical thesis that proposes an argument and answers the "So what?" question.
4. **Organize Directly:** Structure the paper using point-by-point or subject-by-subject organization, ensuring points are compared directly rather than just listed.
5. **Prioritize the Argument:** Place your most important or persuasive point last to leave a strong impression on the reader, especially if writing in service of an argument.

## Sentence shapes with slot anatomy
1. **Analytical Thesis:** "[X] and [Y] share [similarity], but [difference] makes [X] [evaluation/impact]." (Adapted from UNC's pizza example: "Pepper’s and Amante both offer a greater variety of ingredients... but the funky atmosphere at Pepper’s makes it a better place..."). This shape establishes both common ground and a specific, arguable distinction.
2. **Point-by-Point Contrast:** "While [Item A] [trait], [Item B] [trait], which shows [insight about their relationship]." This shape forces a direct juxtaposition of traits within a single sentence.
3. **Lens Comparison:** "Although [Lens Subject] [trait], [Main Subject] [trait], revealing [deeper understanding of main subject]." This shape uses one item as a framework to illuminate the other.
4. **Filtering Irrelevant Traits:** "[Trait] is [superficial/obvious], but [central trait] is [relevant/insightful] because [reason]." This shape helps you decide what to include by contrasting surface-level details with central themes.

## Rules
*   Go beyond description: Reflect on similarities and differences to generate analysis, not just summary. The goal is to make connections and engage in critical thinking, rather than merely reporting facts.
*   Be specific: Replace vague reports with detailed, focused arguments that answer "So what?". Consider why you chose these specific items to compare and why the context of the class matters.
*   Connect directly: Tie points together so the reader sees how traits are similar or different and why those similarities or differences matter. Avoid leaving the reader to make the connections alone.
*   Contextualize: Consider the purpose of the assignment and the course to determine what is central. For literature, focus on characterization, plot, setting, and themes rather than superficial details like typeface.

## Don'ts / Defects to fix
*   **Don't just list traits:** The subject-by-subject structure risks becoming a mere list of points about one item then another, leaving the reader to reflect on the connections themselves. (WHY: Instructors want direct comparison, not detached lists. If you use this form, you must have a very strong, analytical thesis and at least one body paragraph that ties all points together).
*   **Don't use vague theses:** Avoid "Thing A and Thing B are similar in many ways but different in others." (WHY: It fails to propose a specific argument or answer "So what?", leaving the reader without a clear roadmap).
*   **Don't include irrelevant details:** Avoid comparing superficial traits (like typeface in a literature paper) unless the assignment specifically requires it. (WHY: It distracts from central themes and wastes the reader's time).
*   **Don't state the obvious:** Avoid pointing out widely known similarities (like Romantic poets' fondness for nature) without analyzing the different ways they are depicted. (WHY: It is not revealing or interesting; it shows a lack of sophisticated understanding).

## Quality bar
1. The thesis proposes a specific, analytical argument rather than a vague report of similarities and differences.
2. The paper directly compares or contrasts items point-by-point, avoiding a detached list of traits.
3. The chosen criteria are relevant to the assignment and course, focusing on central themes rather than superficial details.
4. The analysis answers the "So what?" question, explaining why the similarities or differences matter to the argument.
5. The organization makes it easy for the reader to follow the comparison, with the most important point placed last.
