# Academic Essay Conclusion — Skill File

## Purpose
The conclusion transitions readers out of the argument by briefly recalling the thesis and journey (“what”), restating why the argument matters (“so what”), and projecting forward to new understandings, questions, applications, or actions (“now what”). It exists to leave readers with a changed perspective, not a mere summary.

## Structure / Moves in Order
1. **What — Echo & Transition**  
   Remind readers of the thesis/main idea and the distance traveled from introduction to final body paragraph. Accomplishes: closure on the argument’s core claim.
2. **So What — Restate Stakes in Broader Context**  
   Revisit the significance established in the introduction, placing it in a new or wider frame. Accomplishes: reinforces relevance and intellectual weight.
3. **Now What — Forward-Looking Implications**  
   Offer final thoughts: new insights, raised questions, further research, other applications, recommended action, or larger context. Accomplishes: extends the argument’s value beyond the paper.

## Sentence Shapes with Slot Anatomy
1. **Thesis Echo**  
   “This [analysis/literature] demonstrates that [restated claim], a [connection/finding] that cannot be explained in full by [prevailing hypothesis/explanation].”  
   Slots: [analysis/literature], [restated claim], [connection/finding], [prevailing hypothesis/explanation].

2. **Stakes Restatement**  
   “Recognizing [key finding] has profound implications for [domain/context], suggesting that [action/understanding/policy].”  
   Slots: [key finding], [domain/context], [action/understanding/policy].

3. **New Insight Prompt**  
   “Readers can now understand [new insight], a step closer to [larger phenomenon/why stakes matter].”  
   Slots: [new insight], [larger phenomenon/why stakes matter].

4. **Question Generation**  
   “This raises questions for further research: [specific question]? Could [method] be applied to [different data/context]?”  
   Slots: [specific question], [method], [different data/context].

5. **Action Recommendation**  
   “In light of this argument, [specific action] should be taken / could lead to [solution].”  
   Slots: [specific action], [solution].

6. **Larger Context Placement**  
   “This argument fits into a broader [field/conversation] where [implication for that field].”  
   Slots: [field/conversation], [implication for that field].

## Rules
- Open with a genuine transition (summary sentence or thesis echo), not a mechanical “In conclusion.” (Harvard: “transition between your final body paragraph and your concluding paragraph”)
- Restate the thesis in fresh language that reflects the journey. (Harvard: “reminds us of where she started by echoing her thesis”)
- Revisit the stakes from the introduction and reframe them more broadly. (Harvard: “bring readers back to those stakes… put those stakes into a new or broader context”)
- Provide at least one “now what” move: new understanding, question, application, action, or larger context. (Harvard: “leave your reader with some final thoughts” — lists five prompt types)
- Match disciplinary conventions (STEM: further study; humanities: textual resonance). (Harvard: “consult your instructor about expectations for conclusions in a particular discipline”)

## Don’ts / Defects to Fix
- **Complete restatement** — repeats everything; fails to synthesize. (Harvard: “a complete restatement of all that you have said”)
- **New substantial counterargument** — no space to refute; belongs earlier. (Harvard: “a substantial counterargument that you do not have space to refute”)
- **Apology for omissions** — signals weakness; scope belongs earlier. (Harvard: “an apology for what you have not said”)
- **Fake transition** — “In conclusion” followed by non-concluding sentences. (Harvard: “fake transitions like ‘in conclusion’ that are followed by sentences that aren’t actually conclusions”)

## Quality Bar (AI-Verifiable)
1. **Thesis Echo Present** — Final paragraph contains a sentence that paraphrases the thesis and signals the argument’s trajectory.
2. **Stakes Rearticulated** — Explicitly names why the argument matters, using broader language than the introduction.
3. **Now-What Move Included** — At least one forward-looking element (insight, question, application, action, or context) appears.
4. **No Prohibited Elements** — Zero complete restatements, new counterarguments, apologies, or “In conclusion” filler.
5. **Discipline Awareness** — Tone and move selection fit the field (e.g., STEM leans to further research; humanities to textual resonance).
6. **Length Proportion** — Conclusion is concise (typically 5–10% of essay), not a second summary.
