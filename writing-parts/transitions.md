# Transitions

## 1. Purpose
Transitions connect ideas within and between sentences, paragraphs, and sections so readers can see how each unit relates to the next and to the essay's overall argument. They convert a sequence of statements into a coherent argument by making the logical relationship between ideas visible. Without them, readers must infer connections the writer never names.

## 2. Structure / Moves in Order
1. **Identify the relationship** between the outgoing idea and the incoming idea — compare, contrast, cause-effect, elaborate, concede, or conclude.
2. **Anchor with old information** — repeat a key term, claim, or concept from the prior sentence/paragraph at the start of the new one.
3. **Introduce new information** that advances the argument.
4. **Name the relationship** with a transitional word or phrase that matches the actual logical link.
5. **Verify the connection in the margin** by stating what each sentence or paragraph does before choosing the connector.
6. **For paragraph-level moves**, end the prior paragraph on the old term and open the next paragraph with that same term before pivoting.

## 3. Sentence Shapes with Slot Anatomy

**Shape A — Old-to-new bridge (within or between paragraphs)**
"[Prior claim about X]. [Shared anchor term from prior sentence], [new information about X]."
Skeleton adapted from Harvard's climate-change example: "Some experts argue [claim A]. Other experts argue [related claim B]." The shared noun ("experts," "individual actions") carries the bridge.

**Shape B — Counter-argument pivot**
"[Prior view]. However/Yet/Nonetheless, [counter-view]."
Slots: signal word → concession or disagreement → new claim.

**Shape C — Cause-and-effect**
"[Cause]. Therefore/Consequently/Hence/Thus, [effect that genuinely follows]."
Slots: causal antecedent → reserved causal signal → direct consequence.

**Shape D — Elaboration or example**
"[General claim]. For example/For instance/In particular/To illustrate, [specific case]."
Slots: claim → elaborator → concrete instance.

**Shape E — Nuance within similarity**
"While X may appear [shared quality], it actually [new distinction]."
Slots: "While" clause acknowledging overlap → comma → sharper reframing (Harvard's "While x may appear the same, it actually raises a new question" pattern).

**Shape F — Conclusion pivot that shows relationship**
"[Recap of prior argument]. [Therefore/Ultimately/As a result], [implication that follows from the recap]."
Slots: condensed restatement → relational signal → forward-looking claim.

## 4. Rules
- Start with information familiar to the reader before introducing new information so they can build connections (Harvard).
- Choose transitional words to match the actual relationship — comparison, contrast, agreement, concession, cause-effect, elaboration, or conclusion (Harvard).
- Repeat a key term from the previous sentence or paragraph at the start of the next to make the link visible across paragraph breaks (Harvard).
- Identify the function of each paragraph in a margin note ("This paragraph summarizes X"; "This paragraph challenges X") before picking the connector (Harvard).
- Reserve therefore, hence, consequently, thus, and so for genuine cause-and-effect links (Harvard).
- For conclusions, prefer transitions that show the relationship between ideas over phrases that merely flag the paragraph as a conclusion (Harvard).

## 5. Don'ts / Defects to Fix
- **Dropping in bare connectors** ("therefore," "moreover," "also," "in addition") without showing the underlying relationship — readers cannot tell what kind of connection is being made (Harvard).
- **Stacking "therefore" or "hence"** where no causal link exists — these words signal cause-and-effect, so misusing them misleads the reader; reserve them for real causal progressions (Harvard).
- **Front-loading the new idea** before the shared context — readers can't see what the new point connects *to*, producing the "Ineffective Transition" pattern Harvard names.
- **Flagging conclusions with "in conclusion" or "overall"** as a substitute for a logical bridge — strong conclusions derive their force from restating the relationship, not from label words (Harvard).
- **Using one transitional word for every relationship type** — "however" signals disagreement, not elaboration; "for example" signals elaboration, not contrast. Word must match function (Harvard).

## 6. Quality Bar
- [ ] Every paragraph and sentence boundary has an identifiable connection to what precedes it.
- [ ] At least one shared anchor term or concept appears at the join between adjacent paragraphs.
- [ ] Each transitional word matches its named relationship category (compare, contrast, cause-effect, etc.).
- [ ] No "therefore," "hence," or "thus" appears without a real causal antecedent in the prior sentence.
- [ ] The margin-note test passes: the writer can state in one sentence what each paragraph/sentence does relative to its neighbor.
- [ ] Conclusions restate the argument's relationship, not just announce closure.
