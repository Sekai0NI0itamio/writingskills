# Evidence Skill File

## Purpose
Evidence turns a claim into an argument by showing why the claim is believable. It provides the factual or textual support that lets readers see how the writer’s point follows from reliable information, making the essay persuasive rather than merely opinionated.

## Structure / Moves in order
1. **State the claim or point** you are supporting.  
2. **Introduce the evidence** (quote, paraphrase, data, example, etc.) with a signal phrase that names the source or type.  
3. **Present the evidence** exactly as it appears (quotation) or in your own words (paraphrase/summary), and include a proper citation.  
4. **Explain the significance**: answer “so what?”—what the evidence shows, why it matters, and how it supports the claim.  
5. **Connect back to the thesis or larger argument**, showing the logical link between evidence and overall point.

## Sentence shapes with slot anatomy
- “[Claim about X]. As [author/source] observes, ‘[exact quotation]’ ([citation]). This shows that [what the evidence demonstrates], thereby supporting the idea that [link to thesis].”  
- “[Claim about X]. For example, [paraphrased information] ([citation]). This indicates that [interpretation], which reinforces the argument that [thesis connection].”  
- “[Claim about X]. The data reveal [statistic/trend] ([citation]), suggesting that [interpretation] and thus confirming that [broader point].”  
- “[Claim about X]. In [source], [author] argues that ‘[paraphrase]’ ([citation]), highlighting that [implication] and strengthening the claim that [thesis‑related outcome].”  
- “[Claim about X]. A case in point is [specific example] ([citation]), illustrating that [what the example proves] and thereby bolstering the view that [argument].”  
- “[Claim about X]. According to [source], [summary of evidence] ([citation]), which means that [consequence] and directly supports the assertion that [thesis element].”

## Rules (do’s, traceable to source)
- **Introduce every quotation** and follow it with discussion; avoid “plop quotations” dropped without intro or explanation.  
- **Cite the origin** of all evidence you did not create yourself.  
- **Explain why and how** each piece of evidence supports your argument; answer questions like “so what?”, “what does this imply?”, “how is this related to my thesis?”.  
- **Choose evidence appropriate to the discipline** (e.g., graphs and stats for sciences, novel passages for literature).  
- **Use a mix of methods**—quotation when wording is brilliant or authoritative, paraphrase when the idea matters more than the exact language, and visual data when a hard fact is needed.  
- **Provide enough evidence**; if feedback asks for more examples, proof, or deeper analysis, add additional support.  
- **Balance evidence with analysis**; aim for at least as much analysis as evidence to avoid mere summary.  
- **Consider primary vs. secondary sources** according to your purpose; know when an item functions as primary (original) evidence.  

## Don’ts / Defects to fix (with why)
- **Dropping a quotation without introduction or follow‑up** – leaves the reader unsure why the quote matters and makes evidence appear to “speak for itself.”  
- **Assuming evidence is self‑explanatory** – readers cannot infer your reasoning; you must state the connection explicitly.  
- **Relying solely on personal experience in most papers** – many disciplines discount anecdotal evidence as insufficient or inappropriate.  
- **Listing evidence without explanation (“laundry list”)** – produces a summary rather than an argument; each point needs analysis of how it supports the claim.  
- **Ignoring disciplinary expectations for evidence type** – using opinion polls in a philosophy ethics paper, for example, will be seen as inadequate.  
- **Failing to cite sources** – constitutes plagiarism and removes verifiability.  
- **Providing evidence but no analysis** – results in a paper that is more description than argument, weakening persuasiveness.  

## Quality bar (checklist for AI self‑review)
- [ ] Each evidence item is introduced with a signal phrase that names the source or type.  
- [ ] Every quotation, paraphrase, statistic, or visual is followed by a clear explanation of its significance (“so what?”).  
- [ ] All external evidence includes an accurate citation (in‑text and reference list as required).  
- [ ] The evidence type matches the conventions of the assignment’s discipline.  
- [ ] For each claim, there is at least one piece of evidence that is explicitly linked back to the thesis or main argument.  
- [ ] The amount of analysis (explanation, interpretation, connection) is equal to or greater than the amount of raw evidence presented.  
- [ ] No quotations appear without introductory language and subsequent discussion (no “plop” quotes).  
- [ ] Evidence is sufficient to convince a skeptical reader; if the prompt or feedback asked for more, the draft includes additional support.
