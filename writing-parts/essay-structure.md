# Essay Structure: Skill File for AI Agents

**Purpose**
Essay structure arranges ideas, evidence, and analysis so that a reader can follow the argument from thesis to conclusion. It exists to make the essay’s logic transparent, ensuring every part serves the central claim and contributes to a coherent whole.

**Structure / Moves in order**
1. **Decompose thesis into claims** – Break the thesis into the main claims needed to support it, then subdivide into sub-claims. This maps the thesis to paragraph-level argumentation (Strategy #1).
2. **Use subheadings in draft** – Insert informal subheadings above draft sections to visualize how ideas fit together and identify where transitions are needed (Strategy #2).
3. **Create a reverse outline** – After drafting, list each paragraph’s main point and assess order, gaps, redundancy, and topic-sentence clarity (Strategy #3).

**Sentence Shapes with Slot Anatomy**
1. *"[Claim about X]. [Evidence]. [What the evidence shows]."*
   - Slot 1 (Claim): A debatable statement advancing the thesis (e.g., “Sandel argues that striving for perfection is problematic”).
   - Slot 2 (Evidence): A specific data point, quote, or example from the source text.
   - Slot 3 (Warrant): The implication or reasoning that connects the evidence to the claim.
2. *"Show what is [persuasive/not convincing] about [author]’s claims regarding [topic]."*
   Adapted from Strategy #1: “Show what is persuasive about Sandel’s claims about the problems with striving for perfection.”
   - Slot 1 ([persuasive/not convincing]): The evaluative stance.
   - Slot 2 ([author]’s claims): The author’s specific position.
   - Slot 3 ([topic]): The subject matter of the claims.
3. *"The paragraph’s main point is [point]. This connects to the thesis by [connection]."*
   From reverse-outline guidance: topic sentences should clearly state main points and their relation to the thesis.
   - Slot 1 ([point]): The paragraph’s central claim.
   - Slot 2 ([connection]): A phrase linking the point back to the overall thesis.
4. *"Subheading: [topic]. What this section achieves: [summary]."*
   From Strategy #2: using subheadings to organize draft sections.
   - Slot 1 ([topic]): A working title for a draft section.
   - Slot 2 ([summary]): The purpose or outcome of that section in service of the thesis.

**Rules** (concrete do’s, traceable to source ideas)
- Do decompose your thesis into a list of required points before drafting: “Put your thesis at the top of a blank page and then make a list of the points you will need to make to argue that thesis effectively” (Strategy #1).
- Do use subheadings in early drafts to visualize idea placement and flow: “typing subheadings above the sections of your draft” (Strategy #2).
- Do create a reverse outline after drafting by listing each paragraph’s main point (Strategy #3).
- Do ensure every topic sentence clearly states the paragraph’s main point, as checked by the reverse-outline question: “Do the topic sentences of the paragraphs clearly state these main points?” (Strategy #3).
- Do verify that paragraph order supports the thesis; reorder if points near the end would be more effective earlier, per the reverse-outline question: “Do you have some paragraphs that include points near the end of the draft that would be more effective earlier in your paper?” (Strategy #3).

**Don'ts / Defects to fix** (explicit anti-patterns, with WHY)
- Don't treat the essay like a five-paragraph theme with one claim and three supporting points; this structure can be “daunting” for longer essays and restricts organic idea development (source comparison note).
- Don't leave paragraph connections implicit; the source states that “your reader should be able to see the connections between your ideas,” so every paragraph must signal its relation to the thesis and to preceding points.
- Don't include multiple paragraphs focusing on the same point without subdivision; the reverse-outline questions flag “more than one paragraph that focuses on the same point” as redundant.
- Don't allow a paragraph to cover too many points; if a paragraph includes more than one main idea, split it, as the source asks whether “some paragraphs include too many points” (Strategy #3).
- Don't place critical supporting claims only near the end of the draft; reorder so that claims build logically toward the conclusion, per the reverse-outline question about points that “would be more effective earlier” (Strategy #3).

**Quality bar** (4-6 checkable criteria an AI can verify its own draft against)
- [ ] The thesis has been decomposed into a list of distinct supporting claims or sub-claims.
- [ ] A reverse outline exists, with each paragraph’s main point listed and its connection to the thesis noted.
- [ ] Every topic sentence clearly states the paragraph’s central point, and that point is unique to that paragraph.
- [ ] Paragraphs are ordered so that each claim logically advances the thesis, with no critical points stranded at the end.
- [ ] Subheadings or transitional cues show how each section contributes to the overall argument.
- [ ] No paragraph covers more than one main point, and no supporting claim is repeated across paragraphs without purpose.
