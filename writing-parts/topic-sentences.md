# Topic Sentences

## Purpose
A topic sentence does double duty: it makes a claim or states the paragraph’s main idea, and it signals how that paragraph connects to the essay’s overall argument. By establishing what readers should expect, it guides the development of evidence and analysis that follow.

## Structure / Moves in order
1. **Identify the paragraph’s core point** – decide what single idea you need to develop.  
2. **Formulate a claim** – turn the point into a declarative statement that can be supported.  
3. **Link to the thesis** – add a phrase that shows the claim’s relevance to the larger argument.  
4. **Preview support** – ensure the sentence hints at the evidence or analysis that will follow.  
5. **Review for focus** – confirm the sentence is not merely descriptive and stays on one main idea.

## Sentence shapes with slot anatomy
| Shape (adapted from Harvard example) | Slots & Description |
|---|---|
| **In [prepositional phrase], [subject] [verb] [object] by [gerund phrase].**<br>*Example:* “In his numerous writings, Marx critiques capitalism by identifying its flaws.” | • **[prepositional phrase]** – sets context or scope.<br>• **[subject]** – the actor (person, group, or concept).<br>• **[verb]** – the action or relationship.<br>• **[object]** – the target of the action.<br>• **[gerund phrase]** – explains *how* or *what* is being done. |
| **[Subject] [verb] [object] that [clause].**<br>*Example:* “Marx argues that capitalism is a system of exploitation.” | • **[subject]** – actor.<br>• **[verb]** – main predicate.<br>• **[object]** – the thing being described.<br>• **[clause]** – a subordinate clause that adds detail or qualification. |
| **[Subject] [verb] [object] because [reason].**<br>*Example:* “The author criticizes the policy because it harms workers.” | • **[subject]** – actor.<br>• **[verb]** – action.<br>• **[object]** – target.<br>• **[reason]** – the causal explanation. |
| **[Subject] [verb] [object] – [additional detail].**<br>*Example:* “Marx critiques capitalism – its systemic flaws.” | • **[subject]** – actor.<br>• **[verb]** – action.<br>• **[object]** – primary focus.<br>• **[additional detail]** – a dash‑introduced elaboration. |
| **[Subject] [verb] [object] by [means].**<br>*Example:* “She supports the reform by presenting data.” | • **[subject]** – actor.<br>• **[verb]** – action.<br>• **[object]** – what is being supported.<br>• **[means]** – the method or evidence used. |

## Rules
- **Make a claim** – the topic sentence must state a main idea that will be developed (Harvard: “makes a claim or states a main idea”).  
- **Signal connection** – include a link to the thesis or previous paragraph (Harvard: “signals to readers how the paragraph is connected to the larger argument”).  
- **Stay focused** – each paragraph should address one point; start a new paragraph when moving to a new idea (Harvard: “focus each paragraph on one main point”).  
- **Set up expectations** – the sentence should preview the evidence or analysis that follows, helping both writer and reader organize the paragraph (Harvard: “helps you know if you’ve effectively made your point”).  
- **Use active voice** – stronger, clearer claims are expressed with active constructions (Harvard example uses active voice).  

## Don’ts / Defects to fix
- **Descriptive only** – a sentence like “Marx wrote a critique of capitalism” merely states a fact; it does not claim what will be argued (Harvard warns against descriptive sentences that give no direction).  
- **Multiple claims** – packing two separate arguments into one topic sentence confuses focus and makes support difficult (violates the “one main point” rule).  
- **Missing connection** – a claim that ignores the thesis leaves readers unsure how the paragraph fits the overall argument (fails the “signal connection” rule).  
- **Vague language** – phrases such as “is important” without specifying *why* or *how* do not set up evidence (undermines the preview function).  
- **Fragmented or run‑on** – a topic sentence that is not a complete sentence hampers readability and analytical clarity (contradicts the “single sentence” expectation).  

## Quality bar
1. **Clear claim** – the sentence contains an explicit argument or main idea, not just a fact.  
2. **Thesis link** – it includes a phrase that shows relevance to the essay’s thesis or prior paragraph.  
3. **Non‑descriptive** – it does not merely describe an event; it indicates what will be analyzed.  
4. **Preview support** – the sentence hints at at least one piece of evidence or analysis that will follow.  
5. **Single‑sentence** – the topic sentence is a complete, standalone sentence.  
6. **Active construction** – the sentence uses active voice for stronger impact.  

These criteria let an AI self‑audit a draft by scanning each topic sentence against the checklist above.
