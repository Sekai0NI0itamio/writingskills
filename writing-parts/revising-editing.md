# Revising & Editing

## Purpose
Revision is the iterative process of re-seeing a draft at macro and micro levels to ensure the argument is worth making, matches the writer's intent, and meets reader needs. It precedes proofreading and focuses on ideas, organization, and evidence before sentence-level polish. Effective revision transforms a discovery draft into a coherent, reader-ready essay.

## Structure / Moves in order
1. **Incubate** — Step away (hours to a day) to gain fresh, critical distance before re-reading.
2. **Think big** — Evaluate focus (assignment fit, scope), thesis (agreement, sophistication, specificity), and purpose (clarity for readers).
3. **Audit macrostructure** — Check balance (proportion of parts), promise-keeping (thesis claims supported, tone/audience fit), organization (logical pattern, transitions, topic sentences), and evidence (accuracy, sufficiency, citation).
4. **Resolve conclusion** — Ensure the ending ties the paper together and ends on a stimulating note, not redundancy or abruptness.
5. **Cycle agendas** — Run multiple passes, each targeting 2–3 areas (e.g., thesis + organization; evidence + balance) rather than everything at once.
6. **Sentence-level edit** — Read aloud; apply forceful verbs, cut prepositional phrases, vary sentence openings, choose precise words, eliminate "It is/There are" expletives.
7. **Proofread** — Fix commas, spelling, and mechanics only after all revision is complete.

## Sentence shapes with slot anatomy
1. **[Specific verb] + [object]**. — *Replace long verb phrases with a more specific verb.* Slots: [Specific verb] = single strong action verb; [object] = what receives the action. (Source: "She defends the idea" vs. "She argues for the importance of the idea.")
2. **[Subject] + [forceful verb] + [complement]**. — *Cut prepositional phrases without losing meaning.* Slots: [Subject] = agent/topic; [forceful verb] = direct action; [complement] = essential detail. (Source: "Huck Finn repeatedly addresses the issue of integrity" vs. "There are several examples of the issue of integrity in Huck Finn.")
3. **[Varied opener], [subject] + [verb]...**. — *Break repetitive subject-verb starts.* Slots: [Varied opener] = subordinate clause, prepositional phrase, adverb, or transition; [subject] + [verb] = core clause. (Source: "If more than two sentences in a row start the same way… try using a different sentence pattern.")
4. **[Precise term] instead of [vague term]**. — *Swap approximate words for exact ones using thesaurus + dictionary.* Slots: [Precise term] = word that says exactly what you mean; [vague term] = placeholder from first draft. (Source: "Don't settle for the best word you can think of at the moment—use a thesaurus… to search for the word that says exactly what you want to say.")
5. **Read aloud → [Stumble point] → [Cut/clarify]**. — *Diagnose sentence health by ear.* Slots: [Stumble point] = where reading halts, energy drops, or focus breaks; [Cut/clarify] = remove extra words, vagueness, or digression to restore energy. (Source: Elbow: "Look for places where you stumble or get lost… Cut through the extra words or vagueness or digression; get back to the energy.")

## Rules
- Separate revision (ideas, structure, evidence) from editing (sentence craft) and proofreading (mechanics); do them in that order. (Source: "That's called proofreading… When you finish revising, that's the time to proofread.")
- Wait before revising; distance enables honest, big-picture judgment. (Source: "Wait awhile after you've finished a draft… A day—a few hours even—will work.")
- Test thesis against discoveries made while writing; modify or replace if it no longer fits. (Source: "Do you still agree with it? Should it be modified in light of something you discovered as you wrote the paper?")
- Verify every thesis claim is supported; check tone and formality for audience. (Source: "Does your paper follow through on what the thesis promises? Do you support all the claims in your thesis? Are the tone and formality… appropriate for your audience?")
- Ensure logical organization: sensible pattern, smooth transitions, topic sentences that forecast paragraphs. (Source: "Does your paper follow a pattern that makes sense? Do the transitions move your readers smoothly…? Do the topic sentences… appropriately introduce what that paragraph is about?")
- Cut ruthlessly; the best material often obscures the argument. (Source: "The idea or metaphor or paragraph that I think is most wonderful… is often the very thing that confuses my reader… Writers must be willing to sacrifice their favorite bits.")
- Read aloud at sentence level; fix every stumble, energy drop, or distraction. (Source: "Read your paper out loud, sentence by sentence… Look for places where you stumble or get lost… Listen even for the tiniest jerk or stumble.")
- Use forceful verbs, minimize prepositional phrases, vary openings, choose precise words, eliminate expletive starts. (Source: "Use forceful verbs… Cut as many prepositional phrases… Check your sentence variety… Aim for precision… Look for sentences that start with 'It is' or 'There are'.")

## Don'ts / Defects to fix
- **Proofreading while drafting** — Wastes time on sentences that may be cut; short-circuits creativity. (Source: "Don't proofread as you go. You may waste time correcting the commas in a sentence that may end up being cut anyway.")
- **Equating revision with rewording** — Editing ≠ revision; rephrasing weak ideas doesn't strengthen them. (Source: "Don't think of revision as just rewording. It is a chance to look at the entire paper, not just isolated words and sentences.")
- **Falling in love with your draft** — Attachment blocks necessary cuts and thesis changes. (Source: "Don't fall in love with what you have written… Start out with a working thesis, and don't act like you're married to it.")
- **Ignoring contradictions or loose ends** — Hoping readers won't notice erodes credibility. (Source: "You could choose to ignore the loose ends and hope your reader doesn't notice them, but that's risky.")
- **One-pass revision** — Trying to fix everything at once causes overload and missed issues. (Source: "Don't try to tackle all of them in one draft. Pick a few 'agendas' for each draft so that you won't go mad.")
- **Expletive openings ("It is/There are")** — Drain energy and delay the real subject. (Source: "Look for sentences that start with 'It is' or 'There are' and see if you can revise them to be more active and engaging.")
- **Repetitive sentence starts** — Creates monotony and signals undeveloped syntax. (Source: "If more than two sentences in a row start the same way… try using a different sentence pattern.")
- **Bloated verb phrases & prepositional chains** — Obscure agency and slow reading. (Source: "Replace long verb phrases with a more specific verb… Cut as many prepositional phrases as you can without losing your meaning.")

## Quality bar
- [ ] Thesis is specific, provocative, and matches the body's actual claims.
- [ ] Every major claim in the thesis is supported with accurate, cited evidence.
- [ ] Organization follows a logical pattern; transitions and topic sentences guide the reader.
- [ ] Proportion is balanced; no trivial point overshadows a key argument.
- [ ] Conclusion synthesizes and ends on a stimulating note, not summary or abrupt stop.
- [ ] Sentences pass the read-aloud test: no stumbles, energy drops, expletive starts, repetitive openings, or vague verbs.
